### STORY 1: CPSC Pajama Recall Wave

#### Long-Form Post — 8:00 AM MT

```
Before you wrap those holiday pajamas, check this.

The CPSC has issued multiple recalls for children's sleepwear this month. Qpancy recalled their kids' pajama sets — they were sold on Amazon from March through August 2026 for about $20, and they don't meet federal flammability standards. That's a burn hazard. The recall number is 27-094.

They're not alone. Mallimoda, Black Sheep Fam, and Veseacky also had kids' pajama recalls in 2026 for the same type of violation. And SHEIN's toy sets (about 265,000 of them) were recalled in November for a separate safety issue.

Federal law requires kids' sleepwear to either use flame-resistant fabric or be snug-fitting (which doesn't catch fire as easily). These brands failed that standard.

If your family bought kids' pajamas online this year — especially from smaller Amazon sellers — do a quick check at cpsc.gov/Recalls before those gifts go under the tree.

For the Qpancy recall specifically: stop using the pajamas, cut them in half, and email a photo to the company for a full refund.

Has anyone else been keeping up with the recall list this holiday season?
```

### STORY 4: AAP AI Guidance

#### Long-Form Post — 3:00 PM MT

```
Your child's pediatrician might soon be using AI tools during your appointments — and the American Academy of Pediatrics has something to say about it.

In November 2026, the AAP published a policy statement in Pediatrics on the safe and equitable use of generative AI in pediatric care. It was presented at the AAP's national conference in October in San Diego, and it sets out what the country's leading pediatric organization thinks clinicians need to do when using AI with kids.

The key points from the AAP:

1. AI tools used in pediatric care should be built for children's healthcare — not just adapted from adult-care tools, which often don't account for the specific needs of kids.

2. Rigorous human oversight is essential. The AAP isn't saying AI is bad — it's saying that a doctor still needs to be responsible for clinical decisions.

3. Equitable access matters. The AAP wants AI tools to work for all kids, regardless of zip code, insurance status, or language.

What does this mean for your family? If you notice your pediatrician using AI-assisted documentation or diagnostic tools, it's completely reasonable to ask how they're being used and what oversight is in place. The AAP's guidance gives you the framework to have that conversation.

Have you noticed AI being used at your pediatrician's office?
```

The AAP's New AI Guidance: What It Means for Your Child's Pediatric Care

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | November 28, 2026

If you've been to a pediatric appointment recently and noticed your child's doctor using a tablet or software tool differently than you remember — documenting faster, pulling up data you hadn't seen before, or having a clinical assistant that's not quite a person — you're not imagining it. Generative AI is arriving in pediatric healthcare, and the American Academy of Pediatrics has published guidance on how it should be used.

The AAP's policy statement on the safe and equitable use of generative AI in pediatric clinical care was published in the November 2026 issue of Pediatrics, the organization's flagship journal. It was first presented at the AAP National Conference and Exhibition in San Diego in October. For Colorado Springs parents, here's what it says and what it means for your family's healthcare experience.

What the AAP Is Saying

The AAP's statement has three central arguments, and they're worth understanding in plain language.

1. AI tools for pediatric care need to be built for children, not adapted from adult tools. Most of the AI clinical tools being deployed right now were developed using adult patient data and adult care workflows. Children are not small adults — their drug dosing is different, their developmental stages create different diagnostic considerations, and their care often involves parents or guardians as active participants in a way that adult care doesn't. The AAP is calling for AI tools specifically designed with pediatric care in mind.

2. Human oversight is non-negotiable. The AAP is not saying AI is dangerous or shouldn't be used. What they're saying is that a physician has to remain responsible for every clinical decision, including ones that an AI tool contributed to. If an AI flags something in your child's chart or generates a clinical summary, a doctor needs to verify it. The AAP's statement is a push for accountability structures that make sure that happens.

3. Equity matters. AI tools in healthcare have a documented history of performing worse for patients who weren't well-represented in the training data — often lower-income patients, patients of color, and patients who primarily speak languages other than English. The AAP is calling for tools that work equitably for all pediatric patients, regardless of zip code, insurance, or background.

What This Might Look Like at Your Pediatrician's Office

Most families won't see dramatic changes at their next appointment. The most common use of AI in pediatric settings right now is in documentation — tools that listen to the encounter and generate clinical notes so the doctor can focus on the patient rather than typing. That's generally a positive development; less time looking at a screen often means more time looking at your child.

Other uses include clinical decision support (AI flagging potential concerns or drug interactions), diagnostic imaging interpretation, and predictive analytics. These are more complex and carry more risk if they're not monitored carefully.

As a parent, you have every right to ask your pediatrician whether they're using AI-assisted tools and how those tools are overseen. The AAP's statement gives you the framework to have that conversation. Most pediatricians will welcome it.

What's Next

The AAP's guidance doesn't change anything immediately at your Colorado Springs pediatrician's office. But it sets the standard that the professional organization expects practices to meet. As AI tools become more common in clinical settings over the next few years, parents who understand what good oversight looks like will be better equipped to advocate for their children. The full policy statement is available at aap.org.

81% of Toddler Foods at the Grocery Store Are Ultra-Processed, New Study Finds — What Colorado Springs Parents Should Know

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | August 8, 2026

Nearly four out of five toddler food products sold at grocery stores are ultra-processed, and almost half fail at least one World Health Organization nutritional recommendation for young children — those are the central findings of a study published this week by researchers at the University of Texas at Austin.

The study, presented at the American Society of Nutrition's NUTRITION 2026 annual conference, analyzed 2,783 food products marketed for children between 6 months and 3 years of age, sourced from 21 grocery stores in Austin. The researchers found that 81% of those products qualified as ultra-processed under standard food classification criteria.

Of the products that failed WHO nutritional recommendations for this age group, the breakdown was significant: 25% exceeded sodium limits, 17% had too much added sugar, 12% were too calorie-dense, and 11% exceeded fat recommendations.

Quick Reference: What the Study Found

  • Products analyzed: 2,783 toddler foods (marketed for ages 6 months–3 years)
  • Ultra-processed: 81% of products
  • Failed at least one WHO recommendation: 49% of products
  • Too much sodium: 25%
  • Too much added sugar: 17%
  • Too calorie-dense: 12%
  • Too much fat: 11%
  • Source: University of Texas at Austin; presented at NUTRITION 2026 (American Society of Nutrition)

Why Ultra-Processed Foods Matter in Early Childhood

The researchers noted that ultra-processed foods in early childhood do more than just add excess sugar or salt. They displace the vital nutrients that young children need during a critical window for brain development, immune function, and healthy weight management.

In the long term, early and consistent consumption of ultra-processed foods has been linked to increased risk of childhood obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. These associations are especially significant in the 6-to-36-month window, when eating habits and food preferences are being formed.

The challenge for parents is that "ultra-processed" does not always correspond to how a product looks or what the front of the package says. Many toddler snacks are labeled with terms like "organic," "natural," "no artificial colors," or feature fruit imagery — while still qualifying as ultra-processed under the NOVA food classification system used by researchers.

What Colorado Springs Parents Can Do

The researchers' practical guidance is not about eliminating packaged foods entirely. For most families, convenience foods play a real role in daily routines — especially during back-to-school weeks when time is short. The goal is to become a more informed label reader rather than a more stressed shopper.

A few approaches that nutritionists recommend when shopping for toddler foods:

  • Read the ingredients list, not just the front of the package. A shorter ingredients list with whole-food items near the top is a strong indicator of less processing.
  • Check the sodium number on the nutrition facts panel. Toddler foods should generally contain less than 200mg sodium per serving.
  • Look for added sugars separately from total sugars. The nutrition facts label now lists added sugars as its own line — this is what to minimize.
  • Prioritize whole foods where practical. Soft cooked vegetables, mashed fruit, lentils, and eggs are not ultra-processed and do not carry a premium price.
  • Choose pouches with visible nutritional profiles. Pouches are not automatically bad — but a pouch with apple and spinach puree as the only ingredients is different from one that includes added starches, concentrates, or natural flavors.

Colorado Springs grocery options including King Soopers, Safeway, Natural Grocers, and Walmart all carry a wide range of toddler foods. The study's findings are not specific to any one retailer or brand — the pattern held across 21 different stores. The label, not the store, is the guide.

What's Next

The University of Texas at Austin researchers have not yet published the full peer-reviewed study — the findings were presented at the NUTRITION 2026 conference. The full publication is expected to undergo peer review before it appears in an academic journal. The conference presentation data is the most current form of the findings available to the public.

Parents looking for additional guidance on toddler nutrition can visit the American Academy of Pediatrics at healthychildren.org, which maintains current recommendations for introducing solid foods and building healthy eating habits in the first three years of life.

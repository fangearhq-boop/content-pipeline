Before You Add to Cart: Toy Safety Checklist for Black Friday and the Holiday Shopping Season

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | November 24, 2026

Tier 2 | National Parenting / Evergreen pillar

Black Friday is two days away. If toys are on the list this year, it's worth taking five minutes to run a safety check before clicking buy — especially after a 2026 that has seen a notable volume of toy and infant product recalls.

Here's what to check, in order of priority.

1. Age Labels and Choking Hazards

The choking hazard warning — "not for children under 3, small parts" — exists because testing has established that children under three are at meaningful risk from objects small enough to pass the CPSC's small-parts cylinder test. This isn't a marketing label. If a toy has that warning and your household has kids under three, that toy stays on the shelf or goes on a shelf they can't reach.

If you're shopping for mixed-age households, this requires real attention. A toy appropriate for a seven-year-old can be genuinely dangerous if a toddler gets hold of it.

2. Magnets

High-powered magnets in children's products are banned under CPSC rules, but they continue to appear in toys sold on third-party marketplace platforms. The danger is severe: if a child swallows two or more small high-powered magnets — or a magnet and a metal piece — they can attract through intestinal walls and cause perforations that require emergency surgery.

Earlier this month, the CPSC recalled over 101,000 CHE-US magnetic robot toys sold on Amazon for exactly this reason. Before buying any toy with magnet components for a child under 14, check cpsc.gov and confirm the product isn't under a recall or warning action.

3. Button and Coin Batteries

Button batteries are in an enormous range of toys, gadgets, and holiday items. If swallowed, a button battery can cause a severe internal burn injury in under two hours — the battery's electrical current generates a chemical reaction that damages tissue even when it looks "dead."

The rule: any toy with a button battery compartment should require a screwdriver or coin to open — not just a snap or slide that a child can manage. If the battery compartment doesn't have a secure closure, it's not safe for young children. Test it in the store if you can.

4. Noise Levels

Toys that exceed 85 decibels at close range can damage children's hearing over time. The ones most likely to exceed this threshold are electronic toys with built-in speakers, toy musical instruments, and anything with sound effects triggered by buttons. If it sounds loud to you at arm's length, it's loud for a child with the toy pressed against their ear — which is how small children typically hold toys.

Some manufacturers include volume controls. That's worth looking for. Noise levels aren't typically printed on packaging, so a listening test in the store or a quick search for product reviews mentioning noise level is the best check available.

5. Flammability on Sleepwear

If you're buying pajamas or sleepwear, check for the flammability labeling. Children's sleepwear must either be made from inherently flame-resistant fabric or be snug-fitting (so it doesn't catch as easily). If you see a label that says "not intended for sleepwear" on what looks like a kids' pajama set, that's a warning to pay attention to.

Earlier this month, the CPSC recalled approximately 1,309 Qpancy pajamas sold on Amazon because they failed the flammability standard. That was a reminder that this category of risk still shows up in products reaching consumers.

What's Next

The CPSC maintains a searchable recall database at cpsc.gov/recalls. Before completing any toy purchase — especially from third-party marketplace sellers — it takes about 30 seconds to run a product name search. For Colorado Springs parents doing Thanksgiving weekend shopping, this is the simplest and most reliable check available.

December brings more toy shopping and more deals. COS Parenting will continue to cover new CPSC recalls and safety alerts as they're announced through the holiday season.

Parenting Yourself Through the Holidays: Why Your Wellbeing Is Your Family's Foundation

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | November 24, 2026

Tier 2 | Evergreen pillar

Thanksgiving week is the beginning of the most logistically complex stretch of the parenting year. School break starts, family visits arrive or don't, the cooking and traveling and managing and coordinating stacks up, and somewhere in the middle of it all you're supposed to feel grateful.

Research published this year in the American Academy of Pediatrics journal Pediatrics found something worth holding onto during this stretch: children of caregivers who weren't dealing with their own mental health challenges benefited most from parenting support programs. The study, which followed 260 children over eight years, reinforced a finding the AAP has been building toward for years — the best thing you can do for your kids is to genuinely take care of yourself, not just manage yourself.

That's not a guilt trip in reverse. It's permission.

What Holiday Stress Actually Looks Like

The holiday version of parenting stress often doesn't look like a crisis. It looks like being short-tempered more often than usual. It looks like the mental list that runs constantly. It looks like getting to the end of the day and feeling like you held it together but didn't actually enjoy any of it.

These signals are worth paying attention to. They're early indicators, not emergencies — which is exactly when it's most useful to do something about them, before they compound.

Practical Things That Help

The most useful interventions aren't dramatic. Here's what has consistent evidence behind it:

Sleep is first. Everything else is harder when sleep is compromised, and the holiday season is full of incentives to stay up later and wake up earlier. Protecting even one additional hour of sleep, even on three or four nights of the week, has measurable effects on emotional regulation the next day.

Movement counts. The Turkey Trot on Thursday is a good example of exercise as a social and community event rather than a chore. A walk through Garden of the Gods on a clear November morning costs nothing and covers the movement, the nature exposure, and the perspective shift that comes from being somewhere genuinely beautiful. Any of those things alone would help. Together they tend to compound.

Social connection — specifically, talking to someone who isn't expecting anything from you in the moment — matters more during high-stress periods than it does the rest of the year. This is the friend call you've been meaning to make, or the conversation after the Turkey Trot.

Local Resources in Colorado Springs

If what you're managing is more than the ordinary holiday stress, there are real options in Colorado Springs. El Paso County Behavioral Health (elpasocountyhealth.org) connects residents to mental health resources and can help identify whether a referral to a therapist or counselor makes sense. If you're a D11 family, the district's family support services team is a point of contact for community referrals and school-based support.

The YMCA's family wellness programs are another resource — not just fitness, but family programming that can reduce the isolation that tends to increase for parents during school breaks when the regular routines that structure the week disappear.

These aren't emergency services. They're the kind of steady infrastructure that works best when you reach for it before you need it urgently.

What's Next

The holiday season runs through January 1, which means the stretch we're entering this week is a long one. The practical value of building even two or three deliberate self-care habits now — sleep, movement, one real conversation a week — is that they don't have to be rebuilt from scratch in December when the volume goes up further.

D11, D20, and D49 schools return November 30. Until then, Colorado Springs has a genuinely full week of options for families to get outside, move, and enjoy the city. That's a good starting point.

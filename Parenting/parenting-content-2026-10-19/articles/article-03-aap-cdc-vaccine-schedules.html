Two Different Vaccine Schedules: What the AAP–CDC Split Means for Your Family

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | October 19, 2026

COLORADO SPRINGS — NATIONAL PARENTING

In January 2026, the CDC revised its routine childhood immunization schedule, narrowing or removing recommendations for several vaccines. The American Academy of Pediatrics reviewed the changes and declined to follow them, maintaining its own broader schedule for protecting children against 18 diseases.

The result is something that hasn't happened before: families are now navigating two official recommendations from two major health authorities — and they don't always agree.

What Changed

The CDC's revised 2026 schedule reorganized vaccine recommendations into three tiers: vaccines recommended for all children, vaccines recommended only for high-risk children, and vaccines left to shared clinical decision-making between families and doctors.

Among the most significant changes affecting young children, the CDC now categorizes COVID-19 vaccination for children ages 6 to 23 months as a high-risk-only recommendation rather than a routine one. RSV vaccine recommendations were also narrowed. The CDC moved hepatitis A and hepatitis B, rotavirus, influenza, and meningococcal vaccines off the routine schedule for some age groups.

The AAP's response was direct: the organization maintained its existing guidance and issued its 2026 schedule recommending protection against 18 diseases for all children from birth through age 18. The AAP schedule is endorsed by 12 major health organizations, including the American Medical Association, the American Academy of Family Physicians, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the Infectious Diseases Society of America.

What This Means for Families in Colorado Springs

Most pediatric practices in Colorado follow the AAP schedule. That means if your child's doctor has been recommending a vaccine, that recommendation is likely grounded in the AAP guidance — not the narrowed CDC list. Nothing has changed in how most local pediatricians practice.

Where it does matter is in conversations about specific vaccines. If a family declines a vaccine because they heard "the government removed it from the schedule," that may be referring to the CDC change — while their own pediatrician is still recommending it under the AAP guidelines. The distinction is worth knowing.

There's no requirement for families to choose one schedule over the other. The conversation to have is with your child's doctor about what they recommend and why.

What's Next

The AAP maintains its immunization schedule at healthychildren.org. The CDC's current schedule is at cdc.gov/vaccines. If you have questions about which vaccines your child has received or still needs, your pediatrician's office can pull your child's vaccine record and walk through what's due at the next visit.

If you're new to Colorado Springs and looking for a pediatrician, El Paso County Health (elpasocountyhealth.org) maintains a list of local providers, including federally qualified health centers with no-cost or low-cost options for families without insurance coverage.

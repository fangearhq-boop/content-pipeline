New AAP Iron Guidelines: What Colorado Springs Parents Should Ask at the Next Well Visit

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | October 19, 2026

COLORADO SPRINGS — NATIONAL PARENTING

The American Academy of Pediatrics published new iron deficiency guidelines in the July 2026 issue of Pediatrics, replacing guidance that had been in place since 2010. The update expands screening requirements for infants and toddlers and changes both when and how testing should happen.

Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in children. It can affect brain development and energy even before a child becomes anemic, which is why the AAP moved toward earlier and more precise testing.

When Screening Should Happen

The new guidance ties screening timing to how a baby is fed. Breastfed babies should be screened between 9 and 12 months. Formula-fed babies who have transitioned to cow's milk should be screened between 15 and 18 months. Previously, timing was less differentiated.

The recommended test has also changed. The AAP now calls for a complete blood count combined with a serum ferritin test — not a hemoglobin check alone. Ferritin measures iron stores in the body and can detect deficiency before anemia develops. If a full blood draw isn't possible, hemoglobin remains the minimum acceptable test.

Children who are at continued risk — for example, those drinking large amounts of cow's milk, eating few iron-rich foods, or with other risk factors — should be rechecked at each well visit through age four.

Iron Supplementation Guidelines

For breastfed term infants, the AAP recommends starting iron supplementation at 1 milligram per kilogram per day by four months of age. Families who prefer to wait may do so until six months, when iron-containing complementary foods are introduced — but the supplement should start no later than when iron-rich solids begin.

For premature infants on full feeds, iron supplementation should begin by two weeks of life.

Cow's milk before 12 months remains off the table. After 12 months, too much cow's milk can crowd out iron-rich foods and becomes a documented risk factor for iron deficiency. If your toddler is drinking more than 24 ounces of milk per day, it's worth discussing with your pediatrician.

Why the Update Matters

The 2010 guidelines focused mainly on anemia, which is the later stage of iron deficiency. The new guidance targets the earlier phase — iron deficiency without anemia — because that stage already affects cognitive development and behavior. Catching it earlier means treatment can start before the effects accumulate.

For families in Colorado, altitude is an additional consideration. Colorado Springs sits at approximately 6,035 feet elevation. Some research suggests altitude can affect hemoglobin reference ranges, which is another reason the AAP's shift toward ferritin testing alongside hemoglobin is meaningful for high-altitude populations. Your child's pediatrician can interpret results in the context of local norms.

What's Next

If your child has had a recent well visit, check with your pediatrician about whether the new screening approach was used. If your breastfed baby is approaching nine months or your formula-fed toddler recently transitioned to cow's milk, the next well visit is the right time to ask specifically about iron screening.

The full AAP clinical report is available through the July 2026 issue of Pediatrics. A parent-facing summary is available at healthychildren.org, the AAP's public resource for families.

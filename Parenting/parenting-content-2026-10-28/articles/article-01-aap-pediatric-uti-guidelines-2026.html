AAP Updates Pediatric UTI Guidelines — What Colorado Springs Parents Need to Know

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | October 28, 2026

The American Academy of Pediatrics published new clinical practice guidelines for urinary tract infections in young children in the October 2026 issue of Pediatrics. If your child is under five, the update is worth understanding — not because you need to change anything today, but because it shapes what your pediatrician will do the next time your child has an unexplained fever or seems off in a way you can't quite place.

What Changed

The most significant structural change in the new guidelines is the expanded age range. The updated guidance covers children from 8 days old through age 5, broader than what the prior version addressed. That lower boundary matters: infants in the first weeks of life can develop UTIs, and the symptoms can be easy to miss or attribute to something else entirely.

The guidelines also drop race as a clinical risk factor. That's a meaningful departure from older guidance that some families may have encountered with previous pediatricians. The prior framework included race as part of the risk assessment; the new version does not. The AAP's position is that the evidence doesn't support it as a useful clinical variable, and removing it avoids both inaccurate risk stratification and the harms that come with race-based clinical assumptions.

Three technical reports accompany the main guidelines, covering risk factor identification, diagnostic approaches, treatment protocols, imaging decisions, and prevention strategies. These are the working documents that inform how pediatricians make decisions at each stage — from recognizing a possible UTI to deciding whether imaging is needed after treatment.

Committee chair Dr. Brian K. Alverson, MD, FAAP, said the update aims to give clinicians clearer direction across the full diagnostic and treatment process.

Why UTIs in Young Children Are Easy to Miss

UTIs are one of the more common bacterial infections in young children, but they're also among the most frequently misdiagnosed — particularly in infants who can't describe what's wrong. An infant with a UTI may present with nothing more than a fever and unusual fussiness. Without a urine culture, that fever could be attributed to a dozen other causes.

In older toddlers, the picture is clearer: a child may complain of pain when urinating, seem to need to go more frequently, or have urine that looks or smells different than usual. But in a child under one year, those signals aren't available. The guidelines' extension down to 8 days of age reflects how early in life this becomes a relevant clinical concern.

Untreated UTIs in young children can progress to kidney infections. Repeated UTIs may indicate underlying structural issues in the urinary tract. Early, accurate diagnosis matters — and these guidelines are designed to help clinicians get there faster.

Quick Reference: UTI Symptoms to Watch in Young Children

Age Group Symptoms to Watch Action
Infants (under 12 months) Unexplained fever, unusual fussiness, poor feeding, vomiting Contact pediatrician; urine culture may be needed
Toddlers (1–2 years) Fever, irritability, changes in urine smell or appearance Contact pediatrician for evaluation
Preschool (3–5 years) Pain or burning when urinating, increased frequency, urgency, lower abdominal pain Describe specific symptoms to pediatrician; urine sample likely needed

This table is a general reference, not a diagnostic tool. Contact your child's healthcare provider with any concerns.

What This Means for Your Next Pediatrician Visit

You don't need to bring a printout of the new guidelines to your child's next appointment. Pediatricians who follow AAP guidance will be working from the updated framework as it becomes integrated into clinical practice. What's useful for parents is knowing that if your child under five presents with an unexplained fever — especially a recurring one — a UTI is on the differential, and a urine culture is the way to rule it in or out.

If your child has had UTIs before, the new guidelines also update the imaging recommendations, which affect whether and when a kidney ultrasound or other imaging is recommended after a first infection. If that's relevant to your family's history, it's worth asking your pediatrician about current recommendations at your next visit.

The full guidelines are available through aap.org and publications.aap.org/pediatrics for families who want the primary source.

What's Next

The new UTI guidelines take effect immediately for pediatricians following AAP clinical practice. If you have questions about how the updated guidance applies to your child — particularly if your child has had prior UTIs or is in the newborn-to-one-year range — raise it at your next well-child visit. The full guidelines and accompanying technical reports are published in the October 2026 issue of Pediatrics and are available at aap.org.

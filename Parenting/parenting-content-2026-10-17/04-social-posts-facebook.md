### STORY 3: Papablic Bedside Sleeper Recall

**Long-Form Post (12:00 PM MT)**

If you have a Papablic convertible bedside sleeper/play yard at home, stop using it.

The CPSC issued a recall on October 15 for about 248 units. The hazard: the mattress can shift and create a gap that traps an infant — a suffocation risk. The latch mechanism can also fail and cause the product to collapse.

What to do: Stop using the sleeper immediately. Disassemble it, cut the mattress and fabric in half so it can't be used again, and email photos to support@papablic.com. The company will provide a full refund.

The products were sold on Amazon and through other baby retailers. If you're not sure whether you have one, check cpsc.gov/Recalls for the full product description and model numbers.

Infant sleep safety recalls are worth taking seriously even when they cover a small number of units — the risk is real and the remedy is straightforward. If you know a family who might have one of these, share this post.

### STORY 4: Bellabu Bear Robes Recall

**Long-Form Post (3:00 PM MT)**

A children's clothing recall that parents should know about, especially if you bought a cozy robe for your kids in the past year or two.

The CPSC recalled about 1,300 Bellabu Bear bamboo sherpa robes for children (CPSC recall #26-013) because they don't meet the mandatory flammability standard for children's sleepwear — meaning they pose a burn hazard.

Details: Sizes S/M and M/L, sold at Saks Fifth Avenue and boutiques from January 2024 through July 2025, price approximately $60. No injuries have been reported.

What to do: Stop using the robe and return it for a full refund. Visit bellabubear.com/pages/recall or cpsc.gov/Recalls for return instructions.

Because these were sold through specialty retailers rather than mass-market chains, this recall may have lower visibility than larger-brand recalls. If you know another family who shops at Saks or children's boutiques, it's worth mentioning.

### STORY 5: AAP Screen Time Update

**Long-Form Post (5:00 PM MT)**

The American Academy of Pediatrics updated its screen time guidance in early 2026, and the change is worth understanding — especially if you've been trying to follow the old "two hours max" rule and wondering why it never quite felt workable.

The new framework is called the 5 C's:

Child — Who is using screens, and what's developmentally appropriate for them? A 7-year-old and a 14-year-old don't have the same needs or risks.

Content — What are they watching or playing? Quality, context, and whether it's something you can engage with together matters more than screen type.

Calm — Are screens being used to self-soothe or avoid uncomfortable feelings? This applies to kids and parents alike.

Crowding Out — Is screen time displacing sleep, physical activity, homework, or family connection? That's where it becomes a problem.

Communication — Are you having ongoing conversations about media use as a family, rather than just setting rules and enforcing them?

For children 6 and older, the AAP recommends building family-agreed limits using their Family Media Plan tool at healthychildren.org rather than setting a fixed daily minute cap.

The shift isn't permissive — it's practical. Minutes were never the whole picture. What's your family's approach to managing screen time?

D11 Bond Vote Nov 3: What the $775 Million Would Build in Colorado Springs Schools

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | October 20, 2026

D11 voters go to the polls on November 3 to decide on a $775 million bond measure. We've covered what it would cost property owners in a previous story. This one is about the other side of that question: if the bond passes, what actually gets built?

The bond covers five main categories, and they're the kind of infrastructure projects that tend not to make headlines until they fail. Here's what each one involves.

Safety Upgrades

Security vestibule upgrades are at the top of the D11 bond's project list. A security vestibule is the controlled-entry area at a school's main entrance — a visitor buzzes in, the outer door closes, and only then does the inner door open. Many D11 buildings don't have them, or have versions that don't meet current standards. The bond would fund retrofits at schools across the district.

Camera system updates are also included. Aging camera infrastructure means poor image quality, gaps in coverage, and systems that aren't integrated with current security protocols. The bond allocates funding to update both hardware and the software systems that school security staff and administrators use.

HVAC Replacement

D11 has buildings with HVAC systems that are 30 or more years old. HVAC failure in a Colorado school isn't just a comfort issue — cold weather school closures due to heating system breakdowns are a real operational risk, and indoor air quality affects both student health and the ability to concentrate. This category of the bond is less visible than security improvements but arguably more immediately felt by anyone who spends a school day inside.

COVID-era attention to air quality in schools raised the standard for what adequate ventilation looks like. Updated systems would address both the heating reliability issue and air filtration quality.

Asbestos Abatement

Several D11 buildings were constructed during an era when asbestos was a standard building material. Asbestos that's properly contained and undisturbed isn't an immediate health risk, but aging buildings undergoing renovation or deterioration can disturb it. The bond includes dedicated funding for professional asbestos abatement in buildings where it's present, addressing a liability that grows with each deferred renovation cycle.

CTE Lab Expansions

Career and Technical Education programs — welding, medical careers, automotive technology, culinary arts, computer science, construction trades — have been an area of growth across D11. The bond would expand dedicated CTE lab spaces, which require specialized equipment, ventilation, and square footage that can't simply be retrofitted into a standard classroom. Demand for CTE courses at the high school level has outpaced available space in some programs.

Classroom Renovations

General classroom renovations cover a range of work: updated electrical systems for modern device-heavy classrooms, improved acoustics, new furniture designed for collaborative learning arrangements, and repairs to building systems that are past their service life. This is the most distributed category in the bond — it applies across the district rather than targeting specific facilities.

The Five Categories at a Glance

Category Examples
Safety upgrades Security vestibules, updated camera systems
HVAC replacement 30+ year-old heating and ventilation systems
Asbestos abatement Professional removal in older buildings
CTE lab expansions Welding, medical, automotive, culinary, CS programs
Classroom renovations Electrical, acoustics, furniture, general repairs

What's Next

The vote is November 3. D11's bond information page has project lists by school and facility category — if you want to see which specific buildings are targeted in your neighborhood, that's where to look. For voters who want both sides of the debate, the D11 bond information page presents the district's case, and local civic groups have organized informational forums ahead of the vote. The question of whether $775 million is the right number is for voters to decide. The question of what that number would actually produce is what we've tried to answer here.

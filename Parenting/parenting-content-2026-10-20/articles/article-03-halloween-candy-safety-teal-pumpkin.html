Halloween With a Food Allergy: What Families Need to Know Before Trick-or-Treat

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | October 20, 2026

Halloween is 11 days away, and for the one in 13 children in the United States who live with a food allergy, October 31 requires more planning than a costume and a pillowcase. The good news: there's a well-established system that makes trick-or-treating safer, and most of it takes about five minutes to set up.

What's Actually in That Candy

The eight most common allergens in the United States — peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, fish, and shellfish — show up in a surprising number of Halloween candy staples. Chocolate bars almost universally contain milk, and many contain peanuts or are processed in facilities that handle tree nuts. Even candies that seem safe can have "may contain" cross-contamination warnings on the label.

For families managing a serious allergy, the habit of checking every piece of candy before it's eaten is worth building before Halloween night rather than discovering on the sidewalk. Older kids can learn to check for themselves. For younger children, running the bucket through a quick sort at home before the eating begins is the standard approach.

The Teal Pumpkin Project

The Teal Pumpkin Project, run by FARE (Food Allergy Research and Education), is a simple signal system for trick-or-treaters and families handing out treats. A teal pumpkin on a porch means that household has non-food treats available alongside — or instead of — candy. It doesn't mean there's no candy; it means allergy families know there's a safe option at that door.

Non-food options that work well: stickers, small toys, pencils, bouncy balls, temporary tattoos, mini Play-Doh containers. Dollar Tree and Target both carry bulk Halloween non-food treat assortments in October.

If you want to participate, you can register your address on FARE's map at foodallergy.org — families searching for teal pumpkin households in COS can find you there. You can also simply put out the teal pumpkin without registering; the visual cue alone communicates the message.

EpiPen and Emergency Preparedness

If your child has been prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector, it goes with them on Halloween. Not in the car. Not in the bag of supplies you carry for the group. On them, accessible, or on the adult walking closest to them at all times.

A fanny pack or small belt pouch keeps it accessible for older kids who can help manage their own emergency response. For younger children, a parent or guardian carries it in a pocket or small crossbody bag. The EpiPen's job is to be reachable in under 30 seconds — a bag buried in a stroller doesn't qualify.

Know the signs of anaphylaxis. Hives and swelling alone are concerning but rarely life-threatening; difficulty breathing, a drop in blood pressure, or a feeling of impending doom are emergencies. Use epinephrine first, then call 911. Antihistamines alone are not adequate treatment for anaphylaxis.

What's Next

FARE's full Halloween resource guide is at foodallergy.org. It includes a school toolkit for classroom parties, a candy guide showing common allergens by brand, and the Teal Pumpkin Project registration. For COS families who want to connect with others managing food allergies, Colorado Springs has a local FARE affiliate — search "FARE Colorado Springs" for current contact information. Halloween works for food-allergy families when the rest of the neighborhood is paying attention too. A teal pumpkin is a low-effort way to be part of that.

New Study: Common ADHD Classroom Accommodations Show No Measurable Benefit for Kids

By Jamie Rivera | November 25, 2026 | COS Parenting

If your child has ADHD and you've been through an IEP or 504 meeting in the past few years, you've probably heard about classroom accommodations. Extended time on tests. Frequent breaks. Standing desks. Low-distraction workspaces. These are the accommodations that schools offer most often, and most parents accept them in good faith — the assumption being that they help.

A study published in JAMA Pediatrics on November 13, 2026 challenges that assumption. After studying 255 children across K–6, researchers found that none of the four most common accommodations produced measurable improvement in ADHD outcomes. Some of them actually made academic productivity worse.

What the Study Found

The research team enrolled 255 children in grades K through 6 in a structured summer treatment program between 2022 and 2025. The program is a well-established research environment for studying ADHD interventions — it uses consistent conditions, trained observers, and tracked outcomes across multiple domains.

They tested four accommodations individually and in combination: extra time on assignments, scheduled short breaks, access to a standing desk, and placement in a low-distraction workspace. The findings for each were the same: no meaningful improvement in behavior, academic accuracy, or task completion. Some children in the extended time and low-distraction workspace conditions actually showed reduced academic productivity — they completed fewer correct assignments than the control group.

The two things that did work were behavioral interventions and stimulant medication. Behavioral interventions reduced rule violations by 37%. Stimulant medication reduced violations by 61% and increased correct completed assignments by 22%. The gap between what the accommodations produced and what the interventions produced is not subtle.

The Caveat Worth Knowing

The researchers are careful about one limitation: this study was conducted in a summer treatment program, not in a traditional school-year classroom. The summer program is a controlled environment with trained staff, consistent routines, and closer monitoring than most classrooms can offer. It's possible that some accommodations work better — or worse — in a regular school setting.

That caveat doesn't erase the findings, but it means they should be treated as a starting point for conversation, not a final verdict on every accommodation for every child. ADHD presents differently across kids, and what this study measured is outcomes at a group level. Individual children may respond differently.

What This Means for Colorado Springs Families

If your child has an IEP or 504 plan that includes any of these four accommodations, it doesn't mean you need to cancel them today. But it does mean it's worth asking your child's school team a harder question: how are we measuring whether these accommodations are actually helping?

Many accommodation plans include things like extended time as a kind of insurance — something to have available. The problem this study identifies is that having it available doesn't mean it's producing the outcomes everyone hopes for. Accommodations can substitute for the two things that the evidence suggests do work: structured behavioral support and appropriate medical treatment when that's the right path for your child.

Local resources for Colorado Springs families navigating ADHD support include D11, D20, and D49 school district special education teams, which are required to offer regular IEP reviews. El Paso County Behavioral Health and Pikes Peak Behavioral Health Group both offer evaluation and support services for children with ADHD.

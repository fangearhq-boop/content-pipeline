15 Minutes of Tablet Time Before Bed Didn't Affect Toddler Sleep, Study Finds — What It Means for Families

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting

October 31, 2026

A study published October 27 in Contemporary Pediatrics found that 15 minutes of tablet video in the hour before bed did not suppress melatonin or measurably alter sleep in toddlers aged 15 to 24 months. The research, conducted by Ruhr University Bochum in Germany using a home-based crossover design, is among the more direct tests of short-duration bedtime screen use in very young children — and its finding is more reassuring than older research suggested.

That said, the study's authors were careful to note what the finding doesn't cover. It doesn't apply to habitual, longer, or unsupervised screen use. One crossover study of 15-minute video sessions is not the same thing as a child going to sleep with a tablet running every night.

What the Study Found

The researchers measured melatonin levels and sleep quality in toddlers on nights when they watched 15 minutes of video on a tablet before bed, compared to nights without screen exposure. The study took place in families' homes to capture real-world conditions rather than a clinical setting.

The finding was that melatonin — the hormone that signals the body to prepare for sleep — was not suppressed by the brief tablet session. Sleep duration, sleep onset timing, and sleep efficiency were also not significantly affected.

The study design controlled for ambient light by asking families to use a night-light, which eliminates one known variable in earlier research that tested brighter light sources rather than tablet screens directly.

Why This Matters — and What It Doesn't Change

For years, the default advice to parents was clear: no screens in the hour before bed for young children. That guidance was based on studies showing that light from screens could suppress melatonin and delay sleep onset. The new study doesn't overturn that concern — it narrows the conditions under which the effect appears.

The AAP has been shifting its screen time guidance throughout 2026, moving away from blanket time limits and toward recommendations based on content quality, context, and parental engagement. The Ruhr University finding fits into that shift: short, supervised tablet use in a dim setting may not trigger the same biological response as older research suggested.

Older research found that everyday touchscreen use was associated with shorter total sleep time and longer time to fall asleep in toddlers — but those studies covered habitual use patterns, not brief, controlled sessions. A 2022 study from the University of Colorado Boulder found significant melatonin suppression from a bright illuminated table, but that's a fundamentally different light intensity than a tablet held at arm's length in a dim room.

What This Means for Bedtime Routines

If your family's bedtime routine already includes a short winding-down show for a toddler, this study suggests you don't need to panic about the melatonin effect of that specific habit. The biological alarm that older guidance warned about may be more dependent on duration, brightness, and context than a simple on/off rule.

What it doesn't suggest is that screens before bed are interchangeable with calming pre-sleep activities like reading, singing, or quiet play. Sleep routines that don't involve screens are still the most consistent recommendation for toddler sleep hygiene — this study just adds nuance to the why behind the older rule.

Talk to your pediatrician if you're concerned about your child's sleep or screen habits. Bedtime is one of the more common topics in the first few years, and individualized guidance is worth more than any single study's finding.

What's Next

This finding will likely be followed by larger studies testing habitual use patterns, longer durations, and different age ranges. For now, the most current guidance from the AAP focuses on the content children are watching and whether a parent is present — rather than strict time-based limits.

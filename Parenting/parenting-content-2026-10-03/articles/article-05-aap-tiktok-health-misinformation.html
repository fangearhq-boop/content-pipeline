AAP Conference Research: TikTok Misinformation About Kids' Health Gets Far More Views Than Accurate Videos

By Sarah Morales October 3, 2026

What the Research Found

Research presented this week at the American Academy of Pediatrics 2026 National Conference in San Diego found that TikTok videos containing health misinformation about children receive disproportionately more views than videos with accurate medical information on the same topics.

The study focused particularly on videos making false claims about the relationship between acetaminophen — the active ingredient in Tylenol — and autism. These claims, which are not supported by medical research, circulate widely on the platform and consistently outperform accurate content in view counts and engagement. The pattern holds across other child health topics as well, with correct information representing only a minority of the total views generated by any given health topic.

The implication is straightforward: if a parent searches TikTok for health guidance about their child, the content they're most likely to encounter is statistically more likely to be inaccurate than accurate. The algorithm that determines what gets shown doesn't favor reliability — it favors engagement, and misinformation tends to be more emotionally resonant and therefore more engaging than measured, evidence-based advice.

Why Pediatricians Are Paying Attention

The AAP is the largest professional organization of pediatricians in the United States, and its annual conference draws thousands of clinicians presenting research on children's health. The inclusion of social media health misinformation as a conference topic reflects how widespread the clinical impact has become.

Pediatricians across the country have reported an increase in patients and families asking questions during well-child visits that were prompted by TikTok content — including questions based on inaccurate claims. This is now happening frequently enough that many physicians are proactively incorporating social media health literacy into their conversations with parents, addressing the TikTok claim before the parent even raises it.

The acetaminophen-autism connection is one of the most persistent examples. Medical research has not found a causal link between acetaminophen use and autism spectrum disorder. The claim, which often circulates via dramatic or emotionally charged videos, can lead parents to avoid or delay using a safe and effective fever medication for their children — a decision that carries real health risk.

A Framework for Colorado Springs Families

None of this means parents should avoid social media, or that every health claim on TikTok is wrong. There are credentialed healthcare providers who create accurate, helpful content on the platform. The issue is that there's no reliable way to sort the good from the bad by watching the video itself — follower counts, production quality, and confident delivery have no relationship to medical accuracy.

A few practical principles from the pediatric community:

First, treat TikTok health content as a starting point for a question, not an answer. If a video raises a concern, write it down and bring it to your child's next appointment. Second, check credentials. The AAP's HealthyChildren.org, the CDC, and your child's pediatrician practice are authoritative sources. Third, be especially cautious about videos that recommend stopping, starting, or changing a medication without consulting a doctor. That's the category where misinformation does the most direct harm.

In Colorado Springs, families can also reach pediatricians through UCHealth, Children's Hospital Colorado's southern Colorado network, and numerous private practices across D11, D20, and D49 communities. Most practices now offer nurse lines and online messaging for questions that don't require an appointment.

What's Next

The AAP 2026 National Conference runs through October 6 in San Diego, with additional research presentations expected on digital health literacy, children's screen time, and social media's effect on adolescent mental health. Full study findings from the conference will be published in AAP journals and available at publications.aap.org.

If you want to talk to your child's pediatrician about health information you've seen on social media, there's no need to wait for a well-child visit — most practices have online messaging systems designed for exactly this kind of question.

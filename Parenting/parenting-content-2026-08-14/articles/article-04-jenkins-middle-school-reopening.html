Jenkins Middle School Reopens After 18 Months — D11 Students Return to New 20-Classroom Wing

By Sarah Morales | August 14, 2026 | COS Parenting

On August 12, 2026 — the same morning as D11's first day of school — Jenkins Middle School held a ribbon cutting for its fully rebuilt wing. For the first time in roughly 18 months, 6th, 7th, and 8th graders walked back onto their own campus.

It was the kind of milestone that doesn't look dramatic from the outside but means everything to the families who lived through the time it took to get there.

Why Jenkins Closed

The original Jenkins building had never been properly anchored to its foundation. Over time, that structural shortcoming caused the floors and walls of the 7th and 8th grade wing to crack and separate. By the time the issues became serious enough to require action, the wing was structurally unsafe for students and staff.

D11 made the call to close the affected wing and relocate students while a replacement was built. It wasn't a decision anyone wanted to make, but it was the right one.

Where Students Went

During the 2025–26 school year, the Jenkins community was split across two locations.

Sixth and seventh graders stayed on the Jenkins campus — but in portable classrooms set up in the school's parking lot. They had proximity to their school, their friends, and their familiar routines, but they were in temporary structures rather than the building they knew.

Eighth graders had a more significant change. They attended classes at Doherty High School, sharing that building with high school students. The arrangement was workable, and D11 staff worked to maintain community and support for the 8th graders in an unfamiliar setting — but it was an 8th grade year unlike any other for the Jenkins Wolves.

What Was Built

D11 crews broke ground on the new wing in October 2025. The project involved demolishing the original 7th and 8th grade wing and constructing a replacement on stable ground, properly anchored to a new foundation from the start.

The new wing covers 20,000 square feet and includes 20 new classrooms — 10 on each floor. The building is built to modern construction standards, and the anchoring issue that created the original problem has been corrected.

Less than 10 months from groundbreaking to ribbon cutting is a fast turnaround for a project of this scope, and it meant the new wing was ready for students on the very first day of the 2026–27 school year.

What the Ribbon Cutting Meant

Ribbon cuttings are a ritual, but this one had real weight behind it. For Jenkins families, the 2025–26 school year was a test of patience and adaptability. Parents navigated split drop-off locations, students adjusted to temporary spaces, and 8th graders spent their final year of middle school off-campus entirely.

Coming back to a rebuilt, permanent home on the first day of a new school year — rather than in the middle of one — gave the Jenkins community a clean start. Every student at Jenkins this year begins their school year in the same building, in a new structure built specifically for them.

D11's investment in this project is part of a broader pattern of capital work across the district's aging school buildings. Jenkins is among the district's older campuses, and the foundation issue that triggered the closure was a known category of problem in schools built during certain eras of construction. The new wing addresses that problem permanently.

What's Next

For the Jenkins community, the next step is simply school. The Wolves have their building back, and the 2026–27 year is underway.

For D11 families at other campuses, the Jenkins project is worth watching as a model for how the district approaches capital investment in aging facilities. D11 is also in the early stages of the major Palmer High School renovation — a $100 million project that will reshape another significant piece of the district's infrastructure over the coming years.

Families with students at Jenkins can find school-specific updates at the D11 website and through the school's direct communications. The campus is fully operational and the ribbon cutting marked the official completion of the construction phase.

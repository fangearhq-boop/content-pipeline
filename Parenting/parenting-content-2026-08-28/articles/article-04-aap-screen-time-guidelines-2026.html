The AAP Changed Its Screen Time Guidelines in 2026. Here's What Actually Changed — and What Didn't.

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting

August 28, 2026

If you've been tracking your kids' screen time by the clock and feeling like you're always losing, here's some news from January 2026: the American Academy of Pediatrics officially retired the "two hours or less per day" rule for children ages 2 and up.

That doesn't mean screens are suddenly fine in unlimited amounts. It means the research had moved past simple time caps, and the AAP updated its guidance to reflect that. The focus has shifted from how long to what, how, and alongside whom.

What Didn't Change

Before getting into the new framework, it's worth noting what the AAP kept in place:

  • No screens under 18 months (with one exception: video chatting with family is still allowed and encouraged)
  • Ages 2-5: no more than one hour of high-quality content daily — the cap stayed for this age group, but the emphasis shifted to quality of that hour
  • Device-free bedrooms and mealtimes — still recommended for all ages
  • Screens shouldn't replace sleep, physical activity, family time, or free play

What Changed: The "5 C's" Framework

For children ages 6 and up, the new AAP guidance centers on a "Family Media Plan" that families build together — rather than a single universal time limit. The centerpiece of the new approach is what the AAP calls the "5 C's":

  • Child: Consider your individual child's age, development, and specific needs. An 8-year-old and a 13-year-old shouldn't necessarily follow the same media plan.
  • Content: What are they actually watching or doing? High-quality educational content, active games, and creative tools are different from passive, endless autoplay.
  • Calm: Is screen time being used to help a child wind down, or to help them fall asleep? The context matters. Using a tablet right before bed as part of a wind-down routine has different implications than handing over a device during an afternoon meltdown.
  • Crowding Out: Is screen time cutting into sleep, physical activity, homework, family meals, or outdoor play? That's the real measure.
  • Communication: Are you watching together and talking about what you're seeing? Co-viewing and conversation dramatically change how kids process screen content.

Building a Family Media Plan

The AAP's recommendation for ages 6 and up is to build a Family Media Plan together — involving kids in the conversation about what rules make sense. Healthychildren.org has a free, interactive Family Media Plan tool that walks families through the process step by step.

The basic elements of a good plan include: agreed-upon times and locations where devices are used, a clear rule about device-free bedrooms, a shared understanding of what content is and isn't okay, and check-ins over time as the plan needs updating.

The Bottom Line for COS Families

School-year routines are a natural time to revisit media habits. With D49, D11, and D20 kids now back in the classroom, after-school screen time tends to expand — homework on devices, gaming with friends, winding down with streaming. The new AAP guidance gives families more flexibility to set rules that fit their household, while keeping the focus on what matters: sleep, movement, family connection, and conversations about what kids are consuming.

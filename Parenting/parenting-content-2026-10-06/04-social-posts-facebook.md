### STORY 1: ZMC Group Light-Up Toy Recall

**Long-Form Post (8:30 AM MT)**

If your kids have light-up toys from dollar stores or party supply shops, this recall is worth checking today.

The CPSC announced an expanded recall of 245,124 battery-powered light-up toys sold by ZMC Group. The products include light-up yo-yos, maracas, wands, and LED headbands — the kind of toys that often show up in goodie bags, party favors, and holiday bins.

The hazard is the button cell battery (a small lithium coin cell) inside the toy. If a child swallows one, it can become lodged in the throat or stomach. The battery then creates an electrical current that causes severe internal burns, serious injury, or death — sometimes within two hours. It's a well-documented hazard, and the CPSC has issued multiple recalls for button-cell-containing products over the past few years.

What to do if you have one of these toys:
- Stop use immediately
- Destroy the toy (so other children can't access it)
- Email recallzmctoy@gmail.com for a full refund

The toys were sold at dollar stores, party supply retailers, and online. If you're not sure whether you have one, look for small light-up toys with a tiny battery compartment that requires a coin or screwdriver to open — that's a sign of a button cell battery.

### STORY 4: AAP Annual Conference Wraps Up — New AI Guidance and What the Research Says

**Long-Form Post (3:30 PM MT)**

The American Academy of Pediatrics wrapped up its annual conference today (October 2-6), and there are a few takeaways worth knowing as a parent.

The big headline: the AAP released new guidance on artificial intelligence in pediatric care. As AI tools increasingly enter doctor's offices — from AI-assisted diagnosis to automated screening tools — the AAP is laying out how pediatricians should use them and what their limitations are. If you've ever wondered whether your child's doctor is using AI in any part of their care, this guidance is the starting point for that conversation.

The October 2026 issue of Pediatrics (the AAP's flagship journal) also published a study tracking trends in parent-reported developmental disabilities from 2019 to 2024. The data covers conditions like ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, and developmental delays — and how diagnosis patterns shifted across that five-year window, which included COVID-era disruptions to routine pediatric care.

On the research side, the conference featured studies linking smartphone ownership in young people to higher rates of depression, obesity, and insufficient sleep. None of these findings are entirely new, but having them consolidated in one conference is a signal that the AAP is treating screen-related health outcomes as a serious clinical issue, not just a lifestyle concern.

If you want to explore the AAP's resources on any of these topics, aap.org is the best starting point.

### STORY 5: The Gazette Endorses D11 Bond 4A

**Long-Form Post (5:30 PM MT)**

Colorado Springs families, the Gazette editorial board came out in support of D11 Bond 4A today.

If you're just catching up: D11 has a $775 million bond measure on the November ballot — Ballot Question 4A. The district's board voted 6-1 to put it there back in August. It would fund repairs at every D11 school: new HVAC systems, asbestos removal, modernized classrooms, a full rebuild of one school, and — finally — air conditioning at Coronado High School.

It would be D11's first voter-approved bond since 2004.

The Gazette's endorsement is significant. It's the largest newspaper in Colorado Springs, and an editorial endorsement from them carries weight with a lot of voters who are still weighing the measure.

The cost to the average homeowner: approximately $260 per year. That's about $22 per month.

Ballots go out in about three weeks. If you want to review the full plan before yours arrives, d11.org/bond has the detailed breakdown.

AAP Annual Conference Wraps Up With New AI Guidance and Developmental Health Research — What Parents Should Know

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting

October 6, 2026

The American Academy of Pediatrics concluded its 2026 Annual Conference today in Washington, D.C., after five days of presentations, research sessions, and guideline updates. For Colorado Springs parents, three things from this year's conference are worth knowing: new guidance on artificial intelligence in pediatric care, a large-scale study on developmental disability trends published in the October 2026 issue of Pediatrics, and research linking smartphone ownership to measurable health outcomes in young people.

AI in Pediatric Care: What the AAP Says

Artificial intelligence is already showing up in pediatric settings — from AI-assisted screening tools at well-child visits to automated risk scoring in electronic health records. The AAP's new guidance addresses how these tools should be used responsibly by pediatric providers and what their limitations are.

The guidance doesn't tell parents to distrust AI-assisted care. Rather, it establishes standards for pediatricians: AI tools should be used as a supplement to clinical judgment, not a replacement for it; providers should be transparent with families when AI is part of a diagnostic or screening process; and AI systems used in pediatric settings should be tested on diverse patient populations to reduce bias.

For parents, this guidance is a starting point for a conversation with your child's pediatrician. Reasonable questions to ask: Does this practice use any AI-assisted screening or risk-assessment tools? What do those tools do, and how do you use the results? You don't need to be a technology expert to ask those questions — they're the same questions you'd ask about any new diagnostic approach.

Developmental Disability Trends: A Five-Year Look

The October 2026 issue of Pediatrics published a study titled "Trends in Parent-Reported Developmental Disabilities 2019-2024." The study tracks how parent-reported rates of ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, and other developmental delays shifted across a five-year window that included the COVID-19 pandemic and its aftermath.

The findings reflect what many families and pediatricians already suspected: the disruptions of 2020 and 2021 — reduced access to routine well-child care, early intervention services, and school-based screenings — affected how and when children received developmental diagnoses. Some diagnoses were delayed; others emerged later as children returned to structured environments. The study gives clinicians and families better data on where those gaps fall and which age groups were most affected.

If you have concerns about your child's development that went unaddressed during the pandemic years, this is a good moment to revisit them with your pediatrician. Early intervention services remain available through D11, D20, and D49 for children from birth through age five, and referrals for older children can be made through your child's school or primary care provider.

Smartphones and Youth Health: The Research Picture

Research presented at the conference reinforced a pattern that's been building across multiple studies: smartphone ownership in young people is associated with higher rates of depression, obesity, and insufficient sleep. These associations don't establish that smartphones cause those outcomes — the research is correlational — but the pattern is consistent enough that the AAP is treating it as a clinical concern rather than a lifestyle question.

Practically, the AAP's guidance on screen time for children and adolescents encourages parents to think about context as much as quantity. When is screen time happening (bedtime use is associated with worse sleep outcomes than afternoon use)? What kind of content? Are devices in bedrooms overnight? These are more useful questions than trying to hit a specific daily hour count.

What's Next

The full text of the AAP's AI guidance and the developmental disability trends study are available through aap.org. The AAP's HealthyChildren.org website (the parent-facing arm of the AAP) translates many of the conference findings into practical guidance for families and is regularly updated after major conferences. If you have specific questions about your child's developmental health or screentime habits, your pediatrician is the best first stop — and now a good time to bring either topic up at the next well-child visit.

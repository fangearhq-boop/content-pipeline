Screen Time and Sleep: What COS Parents Should Know a Month Into the School Year

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | September 14, 2026

A month into the 2026-27 school year, most Colorado Springs families are out of the "fresh start" energy and into the actual work of establishing fall routines. And if screen time has quietly expanded — on tablets after school, on phones during the homework hour, on TVs before bed — that's a common pattern worth paying attention to right now.

New research and updated guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics offer a clearer picture of what the data actually shows about screens and sleep, and what families can realistically do about it.

What the Research Shows

Studies published in major pediatrics journals in 2026 found a consistent and significant link between screen use before bedtime and delayed sleep onset. On average, screens in the hour before bed extended how long it took children to fall asleep by about 23 minutes — an 84% increase in sleep latency compared to nights without pre-bed screen use.

Over a school week, that's nearly two hours of lost sleep. Over a month, it adds up to a pattern that affects attention, mood, and academic performance.

The research also found that screens used in the sleep environment — even with the screen off — were associated with shorter sleep duration. The presence of a device in the bedroom changed behavior.

What the AAP Recommends

The American Academy of Pediatrics has updated its screen time approach to focus on quality and context rather than strict time limits. Here's the current guidance:

AAP Screen Time Recommendations — 2026
Age Group Recommendation
Under 18 months No screens, except video chatting with family
Ages 2–5 No more than 1 hour per day of high-quality programming; watch together when possible
Ages 6 and up Prioritize quality and conversation; screens should not replace sleep, movement, or family time

The AAP's shift away from strict hourly limits reflects an understanding that context matters as much as quantity. An hour of educational content watched with a parent is different from an hour of passive scrolling alone before bed.

Where COS Families Are in the Year

In Colorado Springs, all three major school districts are now past the first month of school. D49 families started August 3 and are in week 6. D11 started August 12 and is in week 5. D20 started August 17 and is in week 4.

This is the window when routines harden. The habits children establish in September tend to carry through the semester. That includes whether devices go into the bedroom at night, whether the hour before bed involves a screen, and whether homework is done with a phone nearby or in another room.

Practical Starting Points

A few approaches that research supports and that fit real family schedules:

  • Charge devices outside the bedroom. This removes the late-night temptation and the blue-light exposure close to sleep.
  • Set a screen-off time 30–60 minutes before bed. Even a 30-minute buffer helps sleep onset.
  • Use device time as a homework transition, not a wind-down. Homework often ends with screens; the transition to bedtime works better with a non-screen activity in between.
  • Watch content together when you can. The AAP emphasis on co-viewing applies to school-age kids too, not just toddlers.

What's Next

The American Academy of Pediatrics publishes its guidance through healthychildren.org — it's worth a bookmark for any family navigating these questions. The AAP's Center of Excellence on Social Media and Youth Mental Health also has a searchable Q&A portal that covers specific scenarios families run into. There's no single right answer for every household, but the research provides a clear direction: the sleep environment matters, and late-night screen access is one of the easier variables to change.

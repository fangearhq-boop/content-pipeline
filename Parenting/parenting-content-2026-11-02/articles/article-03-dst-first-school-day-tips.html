Day 1 After DST: Practical Tips for Getting Your Tired Kids Through the Week

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting

November 2, 2026

Today is the first Monday back at school after Daylight Saving Time ended Sunday morning. The clocks say it's the usual time, but your kid's body thinks it's an hour earlier than the clock says — and their body is the one running the show at 6 AM.

School start times didn't shift. The calendar didn't move. Only the clock did. That disconnect between the internal body clock and the external schedule is what makes the first few days after a time change harder than they look on paper.

Why This Week Feels Different

Circadian rhythms are controlled by light cues, not clock hands. When the clocks "fall back," the body keeps signaling sleep and wake times based on the light patterns it learned over months of summer and fall. Shifting the clock one hour doesn't immediately shift those signals.

School-age children typically take five to seven days to adjust fully. Toddlers and babies take longer — seven to ten days is the common range for infants. This week is the adjustment window, not a permanent new normal. It will get easier by Thursday or Friday for most school-age kids.

What Actually Helps This Week

Keep Bedtime the Same

The instinct to put kids to bed earlier because it's dark by 5 PM can backfire. Moving bedtime earlier before the body clock has adjusted can lead to earlier wake-ups and make the week harder, not easier. Keep the established bedtime consistent. Let the adjustment happen gradually rather than trying to force it ahead of schedule.

Get Morning Sunlight

Natural light is the most effective signal the body uses to reset its internal clock. Even ten minutes of natural light in the morning — walking to the bus stop, standing outside while the car warms up, opening blinds for breakfast — accelerates the adjustment. This works for kids and adults. It's the most evidence-backed practical step available.

Limit Screens After Dinner

This week more than usual, the blue light from phones, tablets, and TVs in the hour before bed competes with the melatonin signals that signal sleep. It's not that screens are suddenly toxic — it's that the body is already working against a shifted schedule, and removing one additional obstacle matters more during the adjustment window.

Expect a Rougher Commute

Monday and Tuesday are usually the hardest mornings. Kids are groggy, slower to respond, more likely to forget things, and more likely to be emotionally reactive than usual. Build in a few extra minutes if you can, and lower the bar on small things this week. It's not a discipline problem. It's a circadian rhythm problem.

A Note on Younger Children

If you have toddlers or babies at home, the adjustment window is longer. For infants, shifting one of the day's naps or feeding windows by 10-15 minutes each day for several days — rather than jumping to the new time all at once — tends to produce a smoother adjustment. Pediatric sleep researchers call this a "gradual shift" approach, and it typically works better for younger children than cold-turkey schedule changes.

What's Next

By Friday of this week, most school-age kids will be largely adjusted to the new schedule. The harder part of the fall-back transition is often the weeks that follow, when early darkness after school disrupts outdoor activity and kids spend more time inside. Getting into the habit of outdoor time right after school before dark — even briefly — makes a noticeable difference in mood and sleep for the next several months. DST officially ends for the season, and standard time runs through March 8, 2027, when clocks spring forward again.

When the Holidays Feel Heavy: What the AAP Says About Kids and Seasonal Stress

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | November 27, 2026

The holiday season brings a lot of things into a family's life at once: disrupted schedules, travel, gatherings with extended family, sugar-heavy meals, and a cultural pressure to feel festive that doesn't always match how everyone actually feels. For adults, the stress of this season is well-documented. For children, it's less often discussed — but just as real.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has published guidance on recognizing and managing stress in children, and it applies directly to what many families experience between late November and early January. The core message is worth knowing before the season is fully underway.

What Holiday Stress Actually Looks Like in Kids

Children don't always name their stress as stress. What they produce instead are symptoms: headaches, stomachaches, changes in sleep patterns, irritability, clinginess, or a sudden drop in appetite. These are the same signals that show up with physical illness, which is part of why parents sometimes miss them.

The AAP notes that behavioral changes lasting more than two weeks — especially when no clear physical cause is present — warrant a conversation with your pediatrician. That two-week threshold is a useful one. Some disruption during the holidays is normal and expected. What you're watching for is a pattern that persists and intensifies rather than resolving as the routine returns.

Common behavioral signals in school-age children include:

  • Increased withdrawal from activities they usually enjoy
  • Difficulty falling asleep or waking up more frequently at night
  • More frequent meltdowns than usual for the child's age and baseline
  • Complaints of physical symptoms (stomachache, headache) with no identified cause
  • Regressive behavior — habits or patterns they had outgrown returning briefly

Younger children, particularly those under five, may not be able to articulate that they feel overwhelmed. What they produce is behavior that parents sometimes attribute to sugar, tiredness, or general toddler unpredictability — all of which are real, but can also be a stress response in disguise.

The Routine Factor

Pediatric guidance consistently points to routine as one of the most effective buffers against seasonal stress. Children's nervous systems calibrate around predictability — consistent mealtimes, consistent bedtimes, consistent expectations. When all of those shift simultaneously, the dysregulation that follows isn't misbehavior. It's a physiological response to novelty and unpredictability.

This doesn't mean keeping the holidays rigid or forgoing special events. It means being intentional about anchoring the parts of the routine that matter most. Bedtime is typically the most high-leverage one: children who maintain consistent sleep schedules through the holiday season tend to handle the other disruptions better. If you're going to let one thing slide, the AAP would suggest it not be sleep.

Mealtimes are also worth protecting where possible. Holiday meals and special treats are part of the season, and no one is suggesting you skip them. The question is whether the meals that aren't special occasions still happen roughly on schedule, with reasonably balanced food. That consistency is stabilizing in a way that matters to kids even when they can't tell you why.

The Role of Participation and Agency

One of the more counterintuitive pieces of AAP guidance is that children who are actively involved in holiday preparations tend to handle the season better than those who experience it as something happening around them. Participation gives children a sense of agency and contribution — they're part of the holiday, not just along for the ride.

This can be as simple as letting a six-year-old help decorate cookies, involving a ten-year-old in selecting a gift for a grandparent, or asking a teenager to help plan one aspect of a family outing. The task itself is less important than the genuine involvement. Children who feel like participants in something tend to have lower stress responses than children who feel like passengers.

Talking About Your Own Stress

Parents who openly acknowledge their own holiday stress — in age-appropriate terms — give children important permission: to feel something other than pure joy during a season that's culturally presented as magical. A parent who says "I'm feeling a little overwhelmed today, so I'm going to take a few minutes to calm down before we start cooking" is modeling emotional regulation in real time.

This doesn't mean processing your full adult anxiety inventory with your seven-year-old. It means naming emotions honestly, showing what a healthy coping strategy looks like, and not performing a cheerfulness that makes a child wonder why they don't feel that way too.

When to Call the Pediatrician

Most holiday-season stress in children resolves naturally as routines return in January. The cases worth a call are those where behavioral or physical changes are significant, persist beyond two weeks, or are qualitatively different from what you've seen before — changes that feel like more than the ordinary disruption of a busy month.

Colorado Springs families have access to a range of pediatric resources, and many practices have after-hours lines or nurse hotlines for questions that feel urgent but aren't necessarily emergency-room material. If your child's symptoms feel like a pattern rather than a moment, trust that instinct. A quick call to your pediatrician is a low-stakes way to get clarity and either rule out something or catch it early.

What's Next

The American Academy of Pediatrics maintains a consumer-facing resource site at healthychildren.org with guidance on stress, sleep, nutrition, and behavioral health that's written directly for parents. For Colorado Springs families, Primary Children's Hospital and UCHealth both maintain pediatric nurse lines for general questions. If your child's pattern continues into the new year, your pediatrician is the right next call. Most holiday stress resolves on its own — but knowing what to watch for means you'll catch it faster if it doesn't.

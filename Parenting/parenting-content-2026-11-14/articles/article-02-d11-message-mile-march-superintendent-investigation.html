D11 Protesters Walk a "Message Mile" Demanding Release of Superintendent Investigation

By Jamie Rivera | November 14, 2026 | COS Parenting

Four days ago, parents and community members in Colorado Springs organized a "Message Mile" march through the city to demand transparency from District 11 leadership. The timing — just weeks after voters approved a historic $775 million bond measure — made the march a signal about more than just the superintendent controversy. It was a statement about what accountability looks like when a school district is about to manage the largest construction program in El Paso County history.

What the March Was About

The core demand is straightforward: protesters want D11 to publicly release the findings of an independent third-party investigation into Superintendent Michael Gaal and Deputy Superintendent Terry Comfort. The district has consistently declined to do so, saying the matter is in active litigation and can't be commented on publicly. D11 did tell FOX21 that the investigation found no wrongdoing — but protesters aren't accepting the district's characterization as a substitute for the report itself.

The "Message Mile" framing was deliberate. Marchers walked a symbolic route intended to draw attention to the gap between what the community is being told and what it's being shown. The demand isn't for the district to admit wrongdoing — it's for independent verification that the conclusion matches the evidence.

The Bond Connection

Colorado Springs School District 11 voters approved a $775 million bond measure in the November 2026 election — the largest bond in El Paso County history. The bond includes an $80 million rebuild of Holmes Middle School and significant investments across the district's aging facilities. It's real money, with real consequences for how D11 schools look and function for decades.

That scale is exactly why protesters and advocacy groups have pushed for a citizen oversight committee to monitor how bond funds are spent. The school board proposed such a committee in the days after the vote — a structure some community members say should have been built into the ballot measure from the start rather than added afterward.

The two issues are distinct: the superintendent investigation is a personnel matter, and the bond is a capital spending program. But they've become intertwined in the community conversation because both hinge on the same underlying question — whether D11 leadership can be trusted to operate transparently when the stakes are high.

What's Known and What Isn't

What's known: an independent investigation was conducted. D11 says it found no wrongdoing. The district won't release the report. The matter is in litigation. Protesters don't accept the district's summary as sufficient.

What isn't known: the scope and methodology of the investigation, what specific allegations were reviewed, who conducted it, and what "no wrongdoing" means precisely in context. Those are the gaps the march was designed to highlight.

For families with children in D11, this matters regardless of whether you have a strong opinion on the superintendent's tenure. The oversight structures that exist for bond spending, personnel decisions, and administrative investigations are the same structures your family will interact with over the next several years as construction projects proceed.

What's Next

The school board's proposed citizen oversight committee is still in the proposal stage. Whether it gets established, what authority it's given, and how it's structured will be determined through the board's public process over the coming months. D11 board meetings are open to the public and listed on the district's website — if this issue matters to your family, that's the right venue for it. The litigation surrounding the superintendent investigation will continue on its own timeline. As a practical matter, the bond construction timeline and the oversight structure are the near-term decisions that families will feel most directly.

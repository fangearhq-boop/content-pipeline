1 in 5 Teens Asked an AI Chatbot for Mental Health Advice, AAP Survey Finds

By Jamie Rivera | August 22, 2026

National Parenting

With school back in session across Colorado Springs, a new survey from the American Academy of Pediatrics puts some numbers to something a lot of parents may have already suspected: teenagers are turning to AI chatbots when they're struggling.

The AAP survey, published in August 2026, polled more than 1,000 adolescents and young adults between the ages of 12 and 21. Nearly 1 in 5 said they had asked an AI chatbot — such as ChatGPT — for mental health advice. That's a significant number, and it's worth understanding why before deciding how to respond.

Why Teens Are Turning to AI

The reasons are actually pretty understandable when you think through them from a teenager's perspective. AI chatbots are available at midnight when anxiety is worst. They don't report back to parents or counselors. They don't judge. There's no awkward pause when you say something you're embarrassed about. For a 14-year-old who's stressed about starting a new school year and doesn't want to make it a whole thing, typing into a chatbot feels manageable in a way that calling a counselor does not.

The AAP's emphasis is not that this is a crisis — it's that parents should be aware it's happening, because most aren't. The typical assumption is still that a teen struggling will either say something to a parent or suffer in silence. The reality is that a third path has emerged, and a meaningful share of teens are already using it.

What AI Chatbots Can and Can't Do

AI chatbots can do some things genuinely well in this context. They can offer a non-judgmental space to process feelings. They can explain coping strategies. They can reflect language back in a way that feels heard. For mild stress and everyday anxiety — the kind that comes with every school year — that's not nothing.

But there are real limits, and they matter. AI chatbots can't detect crisis signals the way a trained counselor can. They can offer generic reassurance that feels supportive but doesn't address what's actually happening. They can't follow up. They can't coordinate with a school counselor or a therapist. And if a teen is in genuine distress, the chatbot interaction may be the only contact point — because the teen chose it specifically to avoid human involvement.

The AAP's concern is not that AI chatbots are being used, but that they're being used as a substitute for human connection and professional support rather than a bridge toward it.

How to Start the Conversation

The good news is that this doesn't require a difficult confrontation. The AAP's guidance — and what most adolescent mental health professionals echo — is to approach it with curiosity rather than concern.

A question like "I read that a lot of kids are turning to AI chatbots when they're stressed — have you ever done that?" is a curiosity question, not an accusation. It opens a door rather than closing one. It signals that you know this is happening, that you're not alarmed by it, and that you're interested in your teenager's actual experience.

The goal isn't to take the chatbot away. The goal is to make sure your teen knows that you're also available — and that human support, whether from a parent, a counselor, or a professional, is something they can actually use.

Local Mental Health Resources in Colorado Springs

If your teen needs more support than a conversation at home, there are real options in Colorado Springs:

  • Pikes Peak Mental Health Center: 719-572-6100. They offer services for children and adolescents, including crisis support.
  • School counselors: All three major districts — D11, D20, and D49 — have counselors at the middle and high school level. They're a good first step and a good referral source for additional services.
  • Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741 for free, confidential support any time.

What's Next

The AAP's recommendation is that pediatricians start asking teens directly about their AI chatbot use as part of routine mental health screening — the same way they now ask about social media use. That shift hasn't fully arrived in most clinical settings yet, but it's coming.

For Colorado Springs parents right now, the most useful next step is simple: have the conversation this weekend. Not as an intervention, just as a check-in. "How are you feeling about this school year?" is a good starting point. If your teen mentions stress, you'll have a natural opening to follow up with how they're handling it — and to mention that you're available if an AI chatbot isn't cutting it.

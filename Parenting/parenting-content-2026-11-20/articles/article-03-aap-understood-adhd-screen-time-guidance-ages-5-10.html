First Expert Guidance for ADHD Screen Time (Ages 5–10): What AAP and Understood.org Want Parents to Know

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | November 20, 2026

If you've got a kid with ADHD and you've been counting screen time minutes and feeling like you're doing it wrong, there's a new resource worth reading. Understood.org and the American Academy of Pediatrics released a paper on November 16 that offers the first expert-backed screen time guidance written specifically for parents of children ages five to ten with ADHD.

It's the first document of its kind. The AAP's existing screen time guidance is designed for the general pediatric population — kids with and without ADHD, across different developmental stages. What parents of kids with ADHD have been missing is guidance that accounts for how ADHD actually affects the way their kids engage with screens. This paper fills that gap.

The Core Message: Context Over Minutes

The paper's most practically useful shift is away from minute-counting. For neurotypical kids, general guidelines — like two hours of recreational screen time per day for school-age children — give families a reasonable benchmark. For kids with ADHD, the guidance argues that this metric misses the point.

What matters more for kids with ADHD is context and content. A child with ADHD hyperfocusing on a creative project, an educational game that's genuinely engaging their attention, or a building activity in a game environment is using their screen time differently than a child passively scrolling or watching content that doesn't hold their focus. The guidance is explicit: not all screen time is the same, and treating it as a uniform block of minutes leads to the wrong conversations.

That's not a license for unlimited screens. It's a reframe that encourages parents to think about what their child is doing, how engaged they are, and whether the screen is functioning as a helpful tool in that moment — rather than watching the clock.

Busting the Biggest Myth

The paper directly addresses one of the most persistent myths about ADHD and screens: that screens cause ADHD. They don't. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition with a strong genetic component. Screen exposure does not cause it, and removing screens does not treat it. The guidance is clear and direct about this, and it's worth having that statement from the AAP on hand for conversations with family members, teachers, or anyone else who's suggested your child's screen use is to blame for their diagnosis.

Addressing Parental Guilt

One of the most distinctive parts of the paper is that it directly names parental guilt as a problem worth addressing. Many families raising kids with ADHD feel that they've somehow failed when their child needs more screen time than their peers, or when screens are a more effective tool for regulation than other approaches. The guidance is explicit that this guilt is both common and unwarranted.

Kids with ADHD have different neurological needs around stimulation, focus, and reward. Screens often meet those needs effectively in ways that other activities don't. Using them as a tool — even more extensively than general guidelines might suggest — doesn't mean you're making a mistake. It means you're responding to your child's actual needs.

What the Guidance Covers

Topic What the Paper Says
Age group Children ages 5–10 with ADHD
Minute limits Not the primary focus; context and content matter more
Do screens cause ADHD? No. This is explicitly addressed and refuted

Who created it Understood.org in partnership with the American Academy of Pediatrics
Where to find it Understood.org (free, publicly available)

What's Next

The paper is freely available on Understood.org and is a useful reference for conversations with teachers, therapists, or pediatricians. If you've been given general screen time guidance that doesn't seem to fit your child's experience with ADHD, this is the resource to bring to those conversations. Understood.org also offers additional tools, articles, and community resources for families navigating ADHD alongside everyday parenting decisions.

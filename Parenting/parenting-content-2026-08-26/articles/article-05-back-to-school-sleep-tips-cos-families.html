Back-to-School Sleep Tips for Colorado Springs Families

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | August 26, 2026

Colorado Springs families are now two to four weeks into the new school year depending on which district their children attend. For many households, one persistent challenge at this stage is the same one that shows up every fall: children are still not sleeping on a school-appropriate schedule.

Summer sleep patterns tend to shift later — bedtimes get pushed back, mornings start slower, and the 10 PM-to-9 AM rhythm that worked in July becomes a problem when the school bus arrives at 7:15. Resetting that rhythm once school starts is one of the most common parenting challenges of the back-to-school period.

Quick Reference

  • Ages 6–12: AAP recommends 9–12 hours of sleep per night
  • Ages 13–18: AAP recommends 8–10 hours of sleep per night
  • Ages 3–5: AAP recommends 10–13 hours per night
  • Screen cutoff: Limit screens 30–60 minutes before bedtime (AAP guidance)
  • COS elevation: Approximately 6,035 feet — altitude can affect sleep quality
  • Resource: healthychildren.org (AAP's parent-facing resource)

What the Recommendations Say

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that school-age children between the ages of 6 and 12 get 9 to 12 hours of sleep each night. Teenagers between 13 and 18 need 8 to 10 hours. Preschoolers between 3 and 5 years old need 10 to 13 hours, including naps.

These are not aspirational targets. Research consistently links insufficient sleep in children to attention and concentration problems, mood instability, weakened immune function, and lower academic performance. Children who are sleep-deprived can appear hyperactive rather than tired, which sometimes leads parents to underestimate how much the lack of sleep is affecting behavior.

Practical Strategies for the Adjustment Period

Consistent bedtime is the single most effective intervention. The body's internal clock — the circadian rhythm — responds to routine. Moving bedtime earlier by 15 minutes every two to three days, rather than all at once, is one approach that sleep researchers recommend for children who have significantly shifted their schedule over the summer.

Limiting screen time 30 to 60 minutes before bed is a specific AAP recommendation. Screens — phones, tablets, televisions — emit blue light that suppresses melatonin production and delays the onset of sleep. For families where screens are part of the evening routine, shifting that activity earlier rather than eliminating it entirely is a realistic middle approach.

Morning light exposure helps. Opening curtains or taking children outside briefly after waking signals to the body that the day has started, which in turn makes it easier to feel tired at the right time in the evening. This is particularly relevant for children who need to wake earlier than they did all summer.

Altitude and Sleep in Colorado Springs

Colorado Springs sits at approximately 6,035 feet above sea level. Altitude affects sleep in ways that are worth understanding for local families — particularly for those who have recently moved to the area or who travel down to lower elevations and notice their children sleep differently there.

At higher altitudes, oxygen levels in the air are lower. During sleep, breathing naturally slows, which can create mild disruptions in sleep quality for some people, particularly during the first weeks or months of adjustment. Children may wake more often, experience lighter sleep, or seem tired despite logging enough hours in bed.

For most Colorado Springs families who have lived at elevation for some time, the body has largely adapted. But if a child is experiencing persistent sleep disruptions that do not improve with consistent scheduling, it may be worth mentioning elevation as a factor when consulting with a pediatrician.

What's Next

Most children's sleep schedules stabilize within four to six weeks of school starting — which means D11 and D49 families may be close to that window now. If sleep problems persist beyond the first month of school, the AAP's parent resource at healthychildren.org includes a detailed sleep guide organized by age. A pediatrician can also assess whether anything beyond routine adjustment is contributing to the problem.

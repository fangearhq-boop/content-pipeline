### STORY 1: CPSC Recalls — Helmets and Light-Up Toys

**Long-Form Post [8:00 AM MT]**
Two product recalls issued this week affect families with young children in Colorado Springs.

First: 5Color Children's Bicycle Helmet and Pads Sets have been recalled because they fail the mandatory federal bicycle helmet safety standard and pose a serious head injury risk. If you have these helmets at home, stop using them and contact the seller for a refund.

Second: ABC Trading Light-Up Children's Toys have been recalled because the button/coin battery compartments are too easy for children to access. This one is critical — swallowed button batteries can cause severe internal chemical burns and are fatal if not caught quickly. Stop use immediately.

Both recalls were issued September 24, 2026. You can search for either recall (and sign up for free recall alerts by product category) at cpsc.gov/Recalls.

Do you subscribe to CPSC recall alerts? It's a free email service — takes 2 minutes to set up and it's been a lifesaver for a lot of families.

### STORY 4: D49 ERBOCES Dissolution

**Long-Form Post [12:00 PM MT]**
D49 parents — something significant happened in District 49 earlier this month that we want to make sure you're aware of.

The D49 Board of Education voted to dissolve the Education ReEnvisioned Board of Cooperative Education Services (ERBOCES), a partnering organization that had been providing charter and specialized education programs for the district. Under direction from the Colorado Department of Education, D49 will now absorb approximately $53 million in funding that had been managed through ERBOCES.

If your family uses D49's specialized programs or charter schools, it's worth understanding what this restructuring means for your kids' schools. The Colorado Springs Gazette first reported this on September 11.

For the most current information on how this affects specific programs, d49.org is your best source.

D49 parents — have you heard about this change? Has it affected your family's school choices or programs?

### STORY 5: AAP Screen Time Update

**Long-Form Post [5:00 PM MT]**
We've all been living by the "2 hours of screen time per day" rule for school-age kids. The American Academy of Pediatrics has officially moved on from it.

The AAP's updated 2026 framework is more nuanced than a clock. For school-age children and teenagers, there's no longer a universal hour cap. Instead, the guidance asks a different question: what is screen time replacing?

If screens are replacing sleep, physical activity, homework, or face-to-face time with family and friends, that's the problem — not the clock. If they're not replacing those things, the calculus changes.

A few things haven't changed. Under 18 months: still no screens (outside of video chatting). Ages 2–5: up to an hour per day of quality content, with parents co-viewing. And one rule holds firm across all ages: no screens in bedrooms, and a screen-free buffer before bed. The research on sleep disruption from screens is consistent and significant.

The shift in language from "how long?" to "what is it replacing?" is something we've found genuinely useful as a framing tool for conversations with kids.

What's your approach to screen time at home — are you a timer household, or do you take a more conversation-based approach?

The AAP Updated Its Screen Time Guidance: Here's What Changed (and What Didn't)

By Sarah Morales | September 29, 2026

National Parenting | Health & Wellness

For years, the two-hour rule was the standard answer. How much screen time is okay for school-age kids? Two hours a day. It was a clean number, easy to remember, and endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics.

The AAP no longer recommends it for school-age children and teens. Their updated 2026 framework takes a more nuanced position — and honestly, one that many parents will find more practical to use at home.

What Changed

For school-age children and teenagers, the AAP has moved away from a universal daily hour cap. The reasoning: research shows that the specific harm from screen time comes not from the hours themselves but from what those hours replace.

The updated framework asks a different question: what is screen time displacing? If it's cutting into sleep, physical activity, homework, or face-to-face interaction with family and friends — that's where the harm lives. A teenager who finishes their homework, gets outside for an hour, eats dinner with their family, and then watches 90 minutes of TV before bed is doing something meaningfully different from a teenager who substitutes screens for all of those activities.

The AAP's updated guidance acknowledges that quality, context, and conversation matter more than raw minutes on a clock.

What Didn't Change

The youngest age groups still have clear limits, and they haven't budged:

  • Under 18 months: No screens, other than video chatting with family.
  • Ages 2–5: Up to 1 hour per day of high-quality programming, with parents co-viewing and talking with children about what they're watching.

And one rule holds firm across all ages: no screens in bedrooms, and a screen-free buffer before bedtime.

This one has the strongest research support. Screens in the hour before bed consistently delay sleep onset and reduce sleep quality in children and adults. Blue light suppresses melatonin production, making it harder for the brain to wind down. Kids who use screens in bed or right before sleep take longer to fall asleep and sleep less deeply. The AAP is firm: bedrooms should be screen-free zones, and devices should be charged outside the bedroom overnight.

The New Questions to Ask

The practical shift the AAP is recommending is a change in the conversation you have with your kids. Rather than asking "how much time have you been on your phone?" the more useful questions are:

  • Did you sleep enough last night?
  • Did you get some physical activity today?
  • Have you spent time with people face-to-face?
  • Is your schoolwork handled?

If those boxes are checked, the screen time discussion changes character. If they're not, that's where the conversation belongs — not on the clock.

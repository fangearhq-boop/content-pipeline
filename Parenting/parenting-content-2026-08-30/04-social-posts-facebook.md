### STORY 1: Chillife Montessori Baby Toy Recall

**FB Long-Form — 8:30 AM MT**

STOP. If you bought a Chillife 5-in-1 Montessori Baby Toy Set on Amazon, stop using it right now.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission has recalled more than 20,000 of these popular sets due to a serious choking hazard. The problem is with the pull-string teether included in the set — its silicone strings are longer and thinner than federal safety standards allow, and they can reach the back of a child's throat. The CPSC has documented 32 incidents related to this type of hazard.

The recalled sets include five pieces: a pull-string teether, circular stacking blocks, a sensory toy, a shape sorting cube with blocks, and a suction cup spinner. They were sold exclusively on Amazon from July 2023 through April 2026 for about $27.

Here's what to do if you have one:
1. Stop using it immediately — especially the pull-string teether component.
2. Cut all silicone strings off the teether.
3. Write "DESTROYED" on the toy in permanent marker.
4. Take a photo of the destroyed toy.
5. Email the photo to the company to receive a partial refund.

Do not donate it. Do not pass it to another family. The remedy requires proof of destruction.

For full recall details, visit cpsc.gov and search "Chillife Montessori."

Has anyone seen this toy in their home or gifted it recently? Share this post — it could matter.

### STORY 3: Back-to-School Anxiety

**FB Long-Form — 12:30 PM MT**

Most Colorado Springs kids are now two to three weeks into the 2026-27 school year. For D11 families, that's week three. D20 is in week two. D49 students are heading into week four.

At this point in the school year, the first-day nerves have had time to settle — or they haven't. And that matters.

Experts from the Child Mind Institute, Johns Hopkins Medicine, and Mass General Brigham all say the same thing: some back-to-school anxiety is completely normal. Kids are adjusting to new teachers, new routines, and new social dynamics. For most, the anxiety fades naturally as the weeks pass and familiar rhythms take hold.

But there are signs that something more is going on. If your child:
— Still has sleep problems after two or more weeks
— Shows major changes in behavior (more withdrawn, more irritable, more clingy)
— Refuses to eat breakfast or says they feel sick on school mornings
— Is avoiding school activities or friends they were previously close to

...those are worth paying attention to. These patterns, if they persist beyond two to three weeks and start interfering with daily life, can indicate anxiety that benefits from more support than a parent pep talk alone.

What you can do: Create predictable routines at home. Listen without minimizing ("I know you're nervous, and that makes sense"). Teach simple calming techniques like slow deep breaths. And if the symptoms don't ease by week four or five, talk to your pediatrician or school counselor.

### STORY 5: Week 3 School Routine Check-In

**FB Long-Form — 8:00 PM MT**

The honeymoon phase of the new school year is officially over.

D49 families are heading into week four. D11 is in week three. D20 is wrapping up week two. That means the novelty of new supplies, new backpacks, and excited first-day energy has worn off — and what's left is the actual work of sustaining a school-year routine.

This is the point where things often start to slip. Bedtime pushes back a little later each night. The morning scramble returns. Homework sits until 9 PM. None of this is unusual — it's just the second phase of the adjustment, and it's worth addressing before it compounds.

A few things that help reset routines at this point:

Sleep first. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 9-12 hours for kids ages 6-12, and 8-10 hours for teens. If your child's bedtime has drifted 30-60 minutes later than the first week of school, this week is a good time to walk it back.

Afternoon decompression. Kids need some downtime after school before they're ready to do homework. A short outdoor break or a quiet snack time before homework often produces better results than jumping straight in.

Keep asking open questions. "How was school?" gets a one-word answer. "What was the most annoying part of today?" often gets a real conversation.

Nothing here is complicated — but it all requires showing up consistently. You've got this.

Most Colorado Springs kids are two to three weeks into the 2026-27 school year. D11 families are in week three. D20 is in week two. D49 students are heading into week four. At this point in the calendar, the first-day nerves have had time to settle — or they haven't. And that distinction matters more than parents sometimes realize.

Some back-to-school anxiety is completely normal. The Child Mind Institute, Johns Hopkins Medicine, and Mass General Brigham all say the same thing: kids are adjusting to new teachers, new social dynamics, and new routines, and for most children the anxiety fades naturally as those patterns become familiar. But weeks two and three are also when it becomes clearer whether the anxiety is doing that — or something else is happening.

Normal vs. Persistent Anxiety

The line isn't sharp, but the timing and severity give parents useful information. First-week nerves — mild stomachaches, some reluctance on Monday mornings, asking extra questions about school — are typical. They usually ease as the child builds familiarity with their teacher, their classroom, and the social landscape of the year.

When the anxiety hasn't eased by week two or three, and especially when it's affecting daily life, that's worth paying closer attention to. According to Mass General Brigham, the signals to watch for include:

  • Sleep problems that have persisted for two or more weeks
  • Major changes in behavior — more withdrawn, more irritable, or more clingy than usual
  • Refusing to eat breakfast or frequently saying they feel sick on school mornings
  • Avoiding school activities or friends they were previously close to

Any one of these, on its own, might have a simple explanation. A cluster of them, persisting for more than two weeks, is a clearer signal that something more than first-day jitters is going on.

What Parents Can Do at Home

The research on what helps points consistently toward a few practical things.

Create predictable routines. The Child Mind Institute identifies predictability as one of the strongest antidotes to anxiety. When a child knows what to expect — what time school starts, what happens when they get home, what bedtime looks like — the brain has less to worry about. Consistent meal times, a reliable after-school sequence, and a bedtime routine all contribute.

Listen without minimizing. The instinct to reassure ("You'll be fine, it's just school") is understandable but can backfire. Acknowledging the feeling first — "I know you're nervous, and that makes sense" — before moving into problem-solving gives the child space to feel heard rather than dismissed.

Teach simple calming techniques. Geisinger health recommends teaching children slow deep breaths and simple muscle squeeze techniques. These aren't just for in-the-moment use — practicing them at home, when things are calm, makes them more accessible when anxiety peaks at the school door.

When to Loop in a Professional

If the symptoms haven't eased by weeks four or five and continue to interfere with daily life, it's time to talk to someone beyond the parent-child conversation. A pediatrician can rule out physical causes and, if needed, refer families to a mental health professional. The school counselor is also a natural first contact — they see many children navigating the same adjustment and can offer in-school support while the family figures out next steps.

The Child Mind Institute notes that anxiety that goes unaddressed tends to compound. The earlier a family gets support, the better the outcomes typically look — and that support doesn't have to mean therapy from day one. A conversation with a pediatrician or school counselor is a low-stakes first step.

What's Next

For most Colorado Springs families, the next few weeks will tell the story. For children whose anxiety is fading — more smiles at pickup, less Monday-morning resistance, more talking about school as a place with friends rather than a place to dread — the calendar is working. For those where the picture isn't clearing up by weeks four or five, the first call is to the pediatrician or school counselor. The Child Mind Institute's childmind.org has a parent resource section with guides specific to school anxiety that are worth bookmarking regardless of where your child is in the adjustment arc.

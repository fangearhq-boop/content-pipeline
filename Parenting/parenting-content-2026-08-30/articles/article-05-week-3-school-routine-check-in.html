The honeymoon phase of the new school year is officially over. D49 families are heading into week four. D11 is in week three. D20 is wrapping up week two. That means the novelty of new supplies, new backpacks, and first-day energy has worn off — and what's left is the actual work of sustaining a school-year routine through September, October, and beyond.

This is the point where things often start to slip, and it happens to nearly every family. It's not a parenting failure — it's a predictable phase of the adjustment, and it's worth getting ahead of before the slide compounds.

What Slips First

The patterns are consistent enough that most parents who've been through a few school years can see them coming. Bedtime pushes back a little later each night, usually starting with a reasonable "just this once" reason — a late soccer practice, a homework crunch, a slow dinner. The morning scramble returns when kids who were waking up easily in week one now need three attempts. Homework that used to be done by 7 PM sits until 9 PM because decompression ran long.

None of this is unusual. But left unaddressed through September, the slide compounds. A bedtime that drifts 30 minutes in week three becomes an hour by week six. Sleep deprivation makes emotional regulation harder, which makes mornings worse, which makes after-school behavior worse — a loop that's harder to break the deeper it runs.

Sleep First

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends nine to 12 hours of sleep per night for children ages six to 12, and eight to 10 hours for teens. Those numbers are worth knowing concretely, because families often underestimate how far the actual bedtime has drifted from what meets those requirements.

If your child's bedtime has crept back 30 to 60 minutes later than it was in week one of school, this week is a practical time to walk it back. A gradual reset — 15 minutes earlier every few nights rather than a sudden hard cutoff — tends to produce less resistance and stick better than an abrupt change.

Afternoon Decompression

School is cognitively and socially demanding in ways that are easy to underestimate. By 3 or 4 PM, most kids have been performing — paying attention, managing social dynamics, following rules — for six to seven hours. Asking them to pivot directly to homework the moment they walk through the door often produces worse results than giving them 20 to 30 minutes to decompress first.

A short outdoor break, a quiet snack, or even just some unstructured time before the homework push tends to produce better focus once homework does start. The total time to complete homework is often shorter when kids have had that buffer — counterintuitive, but consistent with what Hopkins Medicine and other sources note about cognitive recovery time.

Keep Asking Open Questions

"How was school?" gets a one-word answer almost every time. The question is too broad and easy to close off with "fine." Open-ended questions that invite specifics work better: "What was the most annoying part of today?" or "Who did you sit next to at lunch?" or "Tell me one weird thing that happened." These aren't magic — some days the answer is still one word — but they create more openings for real conversation than a closed question does.

Staying connected to what's actually happening in a child's school day matters more at weeks two and three than it did at week one. The first week everyone was paying close attention. The second and third weeks, routines can blur into background noise. A question or two at pickup or dinner keeps the lines open.

What's Next

None of the resets above require a major overhaul — they're incremental adjustments to timing and habit. The goal is a school-year rhythm that's sustainable through June, not just perfect in week one. If the routine check-in this week reveals something bigger — a child who seems significantly more withdrawn, is consistently refusing school, or is struggling academically — that warrants a conversation with the school counselor or pediatrician.

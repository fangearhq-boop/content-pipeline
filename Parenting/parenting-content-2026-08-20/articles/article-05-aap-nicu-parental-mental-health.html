New AAP Research: Parental Mental Health After the NICU Matters as Much as Your Baby's

By Sarah Morales | August 20, 2026 | COS Parenting

If you've been through the NICU, you know that the hardest part isn't always the weeks inside the hospital. It's the months and sometimes years afterward — the anxiety that doesn't go away, the vigilance that becomes your default, the weight of everything you witnessed that you never quite put down.

New research from the American Academy of Pediatrics says that experience is more common than most parents realize — and that the pediatric medical system needs to do more about it.

What the AAP Found

The AAP published a paper in August 2026 in its journal Pediatrics (Volume 158, Issue 2) titled "The Importance of Addressing Parental Mental Health for Children Born Preterm." The research highlights a consistent pattern: parents of preterm babies experience elevated rates of anxiety, stress, and depression — both during the NICU stay and for a significant time afterward. These mental health challenges often go unaddressed because the focus, understandably, is on the baby.

What the research makes clear is that this is a mistake — not just for the parent, but for the child. Parental mental health is a meaningful predictor of a preterm child's developmental outcomes. A parent who is struggling with untreated anxiety or PTSD after a NICU experience is less able to engage in the kind of responsive caregiving that supports the child's growth. The two are connected.

What the AAP Is Calling For

The AAP recommends that pediatric medical practices — including the regular well-child visits you bring your baby to — actively screen for parental mental health, not just the child's development. That means the doctor asking how you're doing, not just how the baby's growth chart looks.

If your pediatrician isn't asking, you have every reason to bring it up yourself. Saying "I've been struggling since the NICU and I think I need support" is a legitimate medical conversation to have in a pediatrician's office — and should result in a referral, not a brush-off.

Resources for COS Families

Colorado Springs families with NICU experience have local options to explore.

  • UCHealth Memorial Hospital's NICU has follow-up programs that can connect families with support resources. If your child was born there, call the NICU follow-up clinic to ask what's available.
  • Centura Health's St. Francis Medical Center also provides NICU services and has family support programs.
  • Your child's pediatrician can provide a referral to a therapist or counselor who specializes in parental anxiety, perinatal mental health, or medical trauma.
  • The Postpartum Support International helpline (1-800-944-4773) includes support for NICU-related parental mental health — not just postpartum depression.

What's Next

If this article landed with you — if you recognized something in that first paragraph — that recognition is enough of a reason to make a call. Parental mental health after the NICU is a real, documented, treatable issue. The AAP is finally saying so loudly enough that it can't be ignored.

You took care of your baby through the hardest weeks of both your lives. Taking care of yourself afterward isn't weakness — it's the same instinct, applied where it's needed.

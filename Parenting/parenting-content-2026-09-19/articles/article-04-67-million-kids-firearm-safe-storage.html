6.7 Million Kids Live in Homes with Loaded, Unlocked Guns — What Colorado Springs Parents Need to Know

By Sarah Morales | September 19, 2026

National Parenting

New research published in the American Academy of Pediatrics' journal Pediatrics estimates that approximately 6.7 million children in the United States live in homes with a loaded, unlocked firearm. That's not a risk estimate or a projection — it's a current snapshot, and pediatric safety experts say the number is entirely preventable with proper safe storage practices.

Colorado Springs has a significant gun-owning population. Military families, hunting families, and homeowners who keep firearms for personal protection are all part of the community here. This research isn't about gun ownership. It's about what happens when loaded, accessible guns and young children share the same space.

What the Research Found

The AAP Pediatrics study used national survey data to estimate how many children are living in homes where a gun is not only present, but loaded and accessible without a lock. The 6.7 million figure represents children who are at elevated risk of unintentional firearm injury — and in many cases, intentional injury as well — simply because of how firearms are stored in their household.

Pediatricians have been tracking these numbers for years, and the data consistently shows that safe storage is the most effective single intervention for reducing child firearm injuries and deaths. When a gun is unloaded and locked, the window between impulse and outcome closes. That gap matters enormously with young children, who can't reliably predict consequences or assess risk the way adults can.

Why This Matters in Colorado Springs

Colorado has child access prevention laws on the books, which create legal liability for gun owners when a child accesses an improperly stored firearm. But law aside, the practical case for safe storage is straightforward: a locked, unloaded gun cannot be picked up and accidentally discharged. An unlocked, loaded one can be, and children's natural curiosity doesn't distinguish between a toy and a firearm.

This is a conversation that cuts across political lines. Whether you're a gun owner who keeps a firearm for home protection or a hunting family with rifles in the closet, the safe storage guidance applies equally. The goal is simply making sure young hands can't reach a loaded weapon.

Free Resources for Safe Storage

Two organizations offer free safe storage resources specifically for families:

  • BeSmartForKids.org — A parent-focused safe storage campaign with guidance tailored to different household situations, including families with young children and teenagers.
  • ProjectChildSafe.org — Offers a storage option finder and distributes free cable gun locks. If you don't currently have a lock for your firearm, this is a fast, no-cost way to get one.

Both sites are straightforward and non-partisan. They don't advocate for or against gun ownership — they help families who already own guns store them safely around children.

Your child's pediatrician is also a reliable resource. Safe storage of firearms is now a standard topic at many well-child visits, and pediatricians can walk through specific recommendations based on your household situation — including guidance for families with teenagers, who present different considerations than families with toddlers.

Starting the Conversation

If your family doesn't own a gun but your children spend time in homes that do — at a friend's house, at grandparents' homes — it's a reasonable conversation to have with other parents. Many parents find it uncomfortable to ask, but the question "is your firearm stored safely?" is the same category as "do you have a pool fence?" or "does your child use a car seat?" It's a safety check, not an accusation.

Children who grow up in gun-owning homes can also be taught safe firearm behavior as part of normal family education. Organizations like the Eddie Eagle GunSafe program teach young children a simple set of rules for what to do if they encounter a firearm. That education works best alongside — not instead of — safe storage.

What's Next

The AAP Pediatrics research will continue to be cited by pediatric safety advocates throughout the fall as part of broader child firearm safety awareness efforts. Your child's next well-child visit is a natural moment to bring up safe storage with your pediatrician if you haven't already.

For free resources, start with BeSmartForKids.org or ProjectChildSafe.org. A cable lock takes about 30 seconds to install and costs nothing through Project Child Safe's distribution program.

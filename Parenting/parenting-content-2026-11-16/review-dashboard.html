CPSC Recalls — Infant Walkers, Safety Gates, Soother Clips
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post Three new CPSC recalls came through this week, and if you have any of these in your home, it's time to act. The first is the Uuoeebb infant walker, sold on Amazon by BaoD. It's been recalled for entrapment and fall hazards and violates the mandatory safety standard for infant walkers. The second is the BabyBond retractable safety gate — a product many families use at stairs and doorways. It's been recalled for entrapment risk. The third is the Primark Little Bear 2-Pack Soother Clip, recalled for choking and laceration hazards. For all three: stop using the product immediately. Visit cpsc.gov/Recalls to look up your specific item and find out what the remedy is — most are either a full refund or a free repair kit. It takes about five minutes to check. We know it can feel overwhelming to keep up with every recall. But infant walkers and safety gates in particular are products that give parents a false sense of security — and when the product itself is the hazard, it's worth pausing to check. Has your family dealt with a product recall before? Share your experience below.

D11 Bond — What Families at Holmes and MacLaren Should Expect
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post The D11 bond is certified. Now comes the part that actually affects families: what happens next. The $775M bond measure passed with about 51.68% of the vote — the first D11 bond since 2006. Holmes Middle School is slated for an $80M rebuild, and MacLaren Elementary is set for $24.18M in improvements. Those are the two biggest school-level projects in the bond. Here's what the timeline typically looks like: first comes the planning phase, where D11 works with architects and project managers to scope each project. Then community design meetings, where families at the affected schools can weigh in on how the new buildings and spaces should be used. Then construction. For Holmes and MacLaren families: you won't be displaced overnight. Projects of this size usually move through the planning phase over the first year before any ground breaks. A required citizen oversight committee will be formed to track how bond funds are spent. The best way to stay informed is to watch d11.org for updates as the planning process begins. Board meetings are open to the public — that's where the timeline decisions happen. What questions do you have about the D11 bond projects?

ADHD + Screen Time — New AAP-Supported Guide for COS Families
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post If you have a kid with ADHD, you already know that screen-time transitions are a different animal than they are for other kids. A new AAP-backed guide published today helps explain why — and gives parents practical strategies. The core insight: ADHD brains are wired to seek dopamine, and screens deliver it in a steady stream. When it's time to stop, the brain doesn't just resist — it actively protests, because it's losing something it was chemically craving. That's not a behavior problem. It's neurology. The strategies that help are ones that give the brain a runway. A 5-minute warning, then a 2-minute warning. A visual timer the child can see (not just hear). Co-regulation — meaning a parent who stays calm and present during the transition instead of escalating. And, when possible, framing screen time as "earned and time-limited" rather than something that gets taken away. The full guide is available through understood.org. If screen-time conflict is a recurring issue in your home, it's worth 15 minutes to read. What strategies have worked in your family for screen-time transitions?

Thanksgiving Baking with Kids — Age-by-Age Kitchen Guide
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post Thanksgiving is 10 days away, and one of the best things you can do with your kids between now and then is get them in the kitchen with you. Here's an age-by-age guide for COS families. Ages 2–4: Washing produce, stirring batters (with help holding the bowl), and tearing bread for stuffing. The goal here is involvement, not precision. Let them make a mess. They'll eat better because they helped. Ages 5–8: Measuring dry and wet ingredients, rolling out pie dough with a rolling pin, peeling potatoes or apples with a Y-peeler. These ages love responsibility. Give them a "job" and they'll stay focused. Ages 9–12: Chopping soft vegetables with supervision (bananas, mushrooms, cooked potatoes), managing a timer, following a recipe from start to finish. This is the age range where real cooking skills click. A green bean casserole or cranberry sauce is fully within reach. Teens: Full recipe ownership. Let them choose one dish and own the whole thing — shopping list, prep, cooking, and plating. They'll complain and then be quietly proud. The developmental research on cooking with kids is clear: it builds math skills (fractions, measurement), scientific reasoning (heat + time = change), and food confidence that lasts into adulthood. It also makes the meal taste better, because everyone had a hand in it. What's your family's kitchen tradition at Thanksgiving?

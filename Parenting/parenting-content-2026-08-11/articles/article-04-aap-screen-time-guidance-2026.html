The AAP Says Screen Time Limits Aren't Enough Anymore — Here's the New Framework

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | August 11, 2026

For years, the dominant advice to parents about children and screens has centered on limits: two hours a day, no screens before bed, no devices at the dinner table. The American Academy of Pediatrics has now shifted its position in a meaningful way, and the new framework looks quite different from the old one.

The updated guidance, backed by a review of hundreds of studies spanning 20 years of research, says that counting screen hours is no longer the right lens. What matters more, according to the new framework, is the type of content children are consuming and the context in which they're consuming it.

What Changed — and Why

The core shift is this: the AAP's updated position moves away from treating all screen time as equivalent and toward asking what's actually happening on screen and whether a parent is present for it.

Dr. Tiffany Munzer, a pediatric behavioral specialist at the University of Michigan Hospital, explained the change plainly in coverage of the update: "Simply taking devices away or enforcing rigid rules can backfire for parents." The research underlying the new guidance found that the two-hour rule and similar limit-based approaches miss important distinctions between different kinds of screen use.

Passive scrolling, solo gaming for escape, and content designed to keep children occupied without engagement fall into one category. Co-viewing with a parent, educational content that sparks conversation, and relational use — video calls with family, creative projects, age-appropriate learning tools — fall into another. The guidance treats these very differently.

The Practical Framework

The new AAP-backed guidance asks families to consider four things instead of watching the clock:

  • Watch alongside your children. Co-viewing makes screen time more interactive and gives parents real-time context for what their children are seeing.
  • Ask about what they're watching. Questions during and after screen time help children process content and signal that what they watch is worth talking about.
  • Choose content that promotes connection. Content that sparks imagination, teaches something, or gives kids and parents something to discuss together is substantively different from content designed purely to hold attention.
  • Model the behavior you want to see. Children who see adults manage their own screen use thoughtfully are more likely to develop those habits themselves.

None of this means unlimited screens with no structure. The guidance doesn't discard boundaries — it reframes them around quality and context rather than a daily hour count.

What This Means at the Start of the School Year

Back-to-school season tends to bring screen time into focus in ways that other times of year don't. Homework happens on devices. Communication with teachers and classmates happens on devices. Extracurricular signups, district notifications, and school calendars all land on devices. And at the end of a long school day, downtime on a device is often exactly what an exhausted kid reaches for.

In that environment, a rigid two-hour limit can create friction in ways that don't actually protect children — because a child using a device for 45 minutes of homework and 45 minutes of a co-viewed documentary has had a meaningfully different screen experience than a child spending the same 90 minutes scrolling solo through short-form video.

The new framework gives families a more useful tool for those conversations: not "how long" but "what are you doing and are we doing it together."

A Note on the Research

The guidance is described as drawing on hundreds of peer-reviewed studies across 20 years of screen time research. That breadth matters because it allows the analysis to track how children's screen environments have changed — from early television research through the introduction of smartphones and tablets and into the current era of short-form video and algorithm-driven feeds.

The AAP has not yet published a full formal updated policy document, but the position shift has been reported and attributed to AAP-affiliated researchers including Dr. Munzer. COS Parenting will continue to track any formal policy publications as they become available.

What's Next

This is a developing area of research, and the AAP's formal updated policy document is expected to follow the current guidance statements. COS Parenting will flag the formal release when it arrives. In the meantime, the practical framework above — co-viewing, asking questions, modeling behavior, choosing connective content — represents the current best guidance from pediatric behavioral research. For families navigating the new school year's screen dynamics, it's a more workable starting point than counting hours.

### STORY 1: Liuqibax Bed Rail Recall — 12,000 Units, Risk of Death [TIER 1]
**Headline:** CPSC Recalls 12,000 Liuqibax Bed Rails Sold on Amazon — Entrapment and Asphyxiation Risk
**Pillar:** National Parenting
**Who:** U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission; Liuqibax/Luqiba (seller)
**What:** About 12,000 Liuqibax adult portable bed rails (Model BR-11) have been recalled because users can become entrapped within the rail or between the rail and mattress — posing a risk of serious injury or death by asphyxiation. The rails also lack required warning labels. Sold on Amazon.com from July 2024 through May 2026 for about $60. No injuries reported. Consumers must stop use immediately and contact Luqiba for a full refund.
**Why it matters:** Many Colorado Springs families use portable bed rails for children transitioning from cribs, grandparents visiting, or elderly family members. This recall covers a widely distributed Amazon product and carries one of the most serious hazard classifications (entrapment/asphyxiation).
**Refund contact:** Email Liuqibax@outlook.com with photo of destroyed product. Destruction required: dismantle rail, cut safety strap and storage bag, write "RECALLED" in permanent marker on upper and lower rails.
**Angle:** CPSC recalls don't always describe a hazard this severe. Entrapment and asphyxiation risk on a product sold to families for years warrants immediate attention for anyone who purchased it on Amazon.
**Byline:** Sarah Morales

### STORY 4: AAP Says Screen Time Limits Alone Are No Longer Enough — What to Do Instead [TIER 2]
**Headline:** The AAP Has Changed Its Screen Time Advice. It's No Longer About the Clock.
**Pillar:** National Parenting
**Who:** American Academy of Pediatrics; Dr. Tiffany Munzer (Univ. of Michigan Hospital)
**What:** A new report citing hundreds of studies over 20 years marks a departure from the AAP's previous guidance: screen time limits alone are no longer sufficient for protecting children's health. The review finds that rigidly enforcing device limits or taking devices away can backfire. Instead, the focus shifts to the type and context of screen use — watching together, choosing educational or relational content, and modeling healthy habits. Dr. Tiffany Munzer, a pediatric behavioral specialist, states: "Simply taking devices away or enforcing rigid rules can backfire for parents."
**Why it matters:** As COS kids return to school this week, the pressure to manage screen time intensifies. This new framework gives parents a more nuanced and practical tool than a simple hour limit.
**Angle:** Parents have been told "2 hours maximum" for years. The new guidance says the clock was never the point — it's what your kids are doing and who they're doing it with.
**Byline:** Jamie Rivera

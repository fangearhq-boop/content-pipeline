AAP's 2026 Screen Time Update: Why Quality Matters More Than the Clock

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | September 21, 2026

National Parenting

If you've ever felt guilty about a longer-than-ideal screen session or stressed about counting your child's daily minutes, the American Academy of Pediatrics' latest update to its screen time guidelines offers a somewhat different framework. Published in the September 2026 issue of Pediatrics, the updated guidelines shift the focus from strict time limits to quality, context, and conversation — particularly for young children.

The AAP has maintained age-based recommendations, but the emphasis has changed. The question isn't just "how long" — it's "what are they watching, are you watching with them, and what are you doing about it afterward."

Quick Reference: AAP 2026 Screen Time Recommendations

Age Group Recommendation
Under 18 months No screens, except video calls (FaceTime, video chat with family)
Ages 2 to 5 Up to 1 hour per day of high-quality programming; co-viewing recommended
Ages 6 and up Consistent limits that protect sleep, physical activity, homework, and family time
All ages Quality and context matter; watch together and discuss what you're seeing

What Changed

Earlier AAP guidance put more weight on hitting specific time targets — the two-hour-a-day limit for older children was a familiar benchmark. The 2026 update doesn't abandon age-based limits, but it positions them differently. The new framing treats strict time counting as less important than what children are actually consuming and how families are engaging with it.

The update also responds to the reality that screens in 2026 are used for a huge range of purposes — video calls with grandparents, learning apps, homework research, creative tools, entertainment. Treating all screen time identically, the AAP acknowledges, doesn't match how families actually live.

The Age-by-Age Guide

For children under 18 months, the recommendation remains essentially the same: no screens, with one exception. Video calls — think FaceTime, Google Meet, or similar — are fine, because the interactive and social nature of those calls is meaningfully different from passive viewing. A baby seeing and responding to a grandparent over video is a social experience. A toddler watching a YouTube loop is not.

For children ages 2 to 5, the guideline is up to one hour per day of high-quality programming. What counts as "high-quality" is intentional. The AAP points to educational programming from trusted sources over passive entertainment or user-generated content with no developmental value. Within that hour, the new guidelines particularly emphasize co-viewing — watching alongside your child rather than handing them a device.

For children ages 6 and up, the recommendation is consistent limits — not necessarily a fixed number of hours — that ensure screens don't displace sleep, physical activity, homework, or family interaction. The goal is that screen time fits into a healthy daily routine rather than replacing parts of it.

Co-Viewing: The Core Strategy

Across all age groups, the AAP is putting co-viewing front and center. Sitting with your child, watching what they're watching, and talking about it afterward makes a measurable difference in how children process media content. It turns passive viewing into an active shared experience.

For COS families heading into fall — when shorter days and cooler temperatures mean more indoor time — this is a practical shift in mindset. It's less about policing a timer and more about being present when the screen is on. That could mean watching a nature documentary together and talking about it over dinner, or helping your kid navigate an educational app rather than leaving them to it alone.

The AAP isn't saying screen time doesn't matter or that limits aren't useful. They're saying that a parent watching alongside a five-year-old for 45 minutes of quality programming is a better outcome than a child watching 30 minutes of low-quality content in isolation.

What's Next

The full 2026 AAP screen time guidelines are published in Pediatrics, Volume 158, Issue 3 (September 2026). The AAP's official news releases are available at aap.org. For a parent-friendly summary of the recommendations, CHOC (Children's Health of Orange County) has published a readable breakdown at health.choc.org.

As fall settles in and Colorado Springs families spend more time indoors, this is a good moment to revisit what's on the screen in your household — not to audit every minute, but to ask whether what your kids are watching is worth their time and whether you're watching it with them.

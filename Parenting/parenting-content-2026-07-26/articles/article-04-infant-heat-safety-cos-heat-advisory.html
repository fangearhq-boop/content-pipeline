What Pediatricians Are Saying About Infants and Extreme Heat

Pediatricians nationwide issued detailed heat safety guidance in July 2026, timed to the heat patterns affecting much of the country. The guidance covers several practices that are either poorly understood or counterintuitive. For families with infants and toddlers in Colorado Springs today, the key rules are as follows.

Do Not Cover a Stroller With a Blanket

This is the rule that most consistently surprises parents. Draping a thin cloth or lightweight blanket over a stroller to block the sun feels protective, but it does the opposite: it prevents air from circulating inside the stroller and traps heat around the child. Pediatricians are explicit on this point. The stroller's built-in canopy is the right tool for shading a child outdoors. If a stroller lacks a canopy, a properly designed mesh stroller cover that allows airflow is an acceptable alternative, but a standard blanket or cloth is not.

Check Metal Parts Before They Touch a Child's Skin

Metal stroller frames, car seat buckles, and playground equipment all absorb heat rapidly in direct sun. These surfaces can reach temperatures that cause burns even when they look or feel only moderately warm. Pediatricians recommend testing metal stroller parts and buckles with the back of an adult hand before positioning a child in the stroller. The same applies to playground equipment -- metal slides and metal bars on playgrounds can cause contact burns and are best avoided during the heat of the day.

Prepare the Car Before Putting a Child Inside

Car interiors can heat to dangerous temperatures quickly, even in partial shade and even with windows cracked. Before placing an infant or toddler in a car seat, run the air conditioning or open all doors and windows for 10 to 20 seconds to allow hot air to escape. This step matters even for short stops.

Heat Is Now on the Standard Safety List

Pediatricians noted in July 2026 guidance that they are increasingly adding extreme heat to the standard safety discussion at well-child visits, alongside drowning prevention, choking hazards, and car seat safety. Climate patterns have made extended heat events more common in places that historically saw them rarely, and pediatricians are adjusting their safety guidance accordingly.

The AAP Replaced the 2-Hour Screen Time Rule — Here's the New Framework for COS Families

By Sarah Morales | October 24, 2026 | COS Parenting

If you've been quietly ignoring the "no more than two hours of screen time per day" rule for your school-age kids, you're not alone — and you're also now in the clear. The American Academy of Pediatrics replaced that guidance in early 2026 with a new framework that focuses on the quality and context of screen use rather than the clock.

What Changed

The AAP's updated guidance, issued in February 2026, was the first major revision to its screen time recommendations in approximately 10 years. The old framework set specific time limits — no screens for children under two, and no more than one to two hours per day for children ages two to five. The new framework drops those limits for school-age children entirely.

In their place, the AAP introduced the "5 Cs" framework for evaluating screen time:

  • Content: What is your child watching or engaging with? Educational, creative, and age-appropriate content is different from passive consumption of unregulated videos.
  • Context: Is the screen time happening during family time, as a reward, to fill downtime, or instead of sleep and physical activity?
  • Child: Every child is different. A child who uses screens to connect socially or creatively has different needs than one using screens to avoid anxiety.
  • Connections: Is screen time connecting your child to others — family video calls, shared gaming — or isolating them?
  • Choices: Is screen time a deliberate family choice, or is it happening by default?

What Hasn't Changed

The AAP still recommends avoiding screens for children under 18 to 24 months, with an exception for video chatting with family members. The research on early brain development in that age range is consistent: passive screen exposure in infancy does not support language or cognitive development the way direct interaction does.

For all ages, the AAP continues to recommend keeping screens out of the bedroom and avoiding them for at least one hour before bedtime. Screen light — particularly blue light — disrupts melatonin production and can interfere with sleep onset, especially in children whose sleep needs are greater than adults'.

What This Means for COS Families

As Colorado Springs heads into fall and winter — shorter days, colder temperatures, more indoor time — screen time naturally increases for most families. The 5 Cs framework gives parents a practical tool for evaluating those choices rather than tracking minutes.

A few practical applications: co-viewing a documentary with your kids counts differently than unsupervised scrolling. A child playing a creative building game counts differently than watching passive content for hours. Family movie night on a Friday is a different context than a screen as a default babysitter on a Tuesday afternoon.

What's Next

The AAP's full 2026 screen time guidance is available at healthychildren.org. For Colorado Springs families looking for more context, Common Sense Media (commonsensemedia.org) offers age-by-age content guides that align well with the 5 Cs framework. As the holiday season approaches — with more family streaming, gaming gifts, and winter break — it's a good time to talk as a family about how you want to handle screens together rather than setting rules that create conflict.

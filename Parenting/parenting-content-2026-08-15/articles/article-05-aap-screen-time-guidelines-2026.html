AAP Screen Time Guidelines 2026: What Colorado Springs Parents Need to Know

By Sarah Morales | August 15, 2026 | COS Parenting

The American Academy of Pediatrics has updated its guidance on children's screen time, and the new framing is more nuanced than the simple time limits most parents grew up hearing. The focus has shifted from "how many minutes" to "how does screen time fit into the rest of your child's day" — a distinction that matters for Colorado Springs families trying to navigate devices in the household.

Here's what the AAP currently recommends, by age group, and what it means in practice.

The Guidelines by Age

For children under 18 months, the AAP recommends avoiding screen use entirely — with one exception. Video calls with family members (grandparents, relatives who live at a distance) are specifically carved out as beneficial because they involve real interaction, eye contact, and back-and-forth engagement rather than passive consumption.

For children ages 2 to 5, the AAP recommends no more than one hour per day of high-quality programming. "High-quality" is a meaningful qualifier here. The AAP points to programming that is specifically designed for young learners — slow-paced, interactive, educational content — rather than adult entertainment or fast-cut social media content not designed with child development in mind.

For children ages 6 and older, the guidelines shift away from a strict time limit and toward the "balance over burnout" framework. The AAP recommends ensuring that screen time doesn't crowd out the things children need: adequate sleep, regular physical activity, family connection time, and unstructured free play. There's no single number that works across all families and situations.

Quick Reference: AAP Screen Time Recommendations

Age Group AAP Recommendation
Under 18 months No screens, except video calls with family
18-24 months High-quality programming only; watch together with a parent
2-5 years Up to 1 hour daily of high-quality content; co-view when possible
6 years and older Consistent limits; screens should not replace sleep, activity, or family time
All ages Device-free mealtimes and bedrooms; model healthy screen habits

Two Habits the AAP Now Emphasizes

Beyond the time recommendations, two practical household practices have become more central to AAP guidance in recent years.

The first is device-free environments. The AAP recommends keeping screens out of children's bedrooms — particularly during sleep hours — and keeping mealtimes screen-free for the whole family, including parents. Research on family meals consistently links device-free mealtimes to better communication, stronger family relationships, and healthier eating habits. The guidance applies to adults in the household as much as it does to children.

The second is co-viewing and active engagement. When young children watch content, the AAP recommends watching alongside them and talking about what they're seeing: asking questions, connecting what's on screen to real-world experiences, and making the viewing experience interactive rather than passive. This approach supports language development and helps children make sense of what they're consuming.

What "Balance Over Burnout" Actually Means

The shift in language from strict limits to balance reflects an evolving understanding of how screens function in modern family life. Most families are not going to reach zero screen time, and the AAP's updated framing acknowledges that reality. Screens are part of education, communication, creativity, and entertainment — the goal is integration, not elimination.

For Colorado Springs families, this might look like using screens for educational content and video calls during the day, maintaining device-free mealtimes, and setting a consistent rule around devices in bedrooms at night. It doesn't have to mean tracking minutes on a spreadsheet or feeling guilty about a movie night.

The AAP's emphasis on parental modeling is also worth taking seriously. Children's screen habits are influenced by what they observe in the adults around them. If phones are constantly present at meals, in bedrooms, or during family conversations, the habit takes root early — regardless of the rules in place for the children themselves.

What's Next

The AAP updates its guidance periodically as research on child development and technology continues to develop. Families looking for more detailed guidance — including resources for specific situations like gaming, social media, or children with developmental differences — can find current recommendations at HealthyChildren.org, the AAP's official consumer-facing resource site.

For Colorado Springs parents navigating screens in the household, the most useful near-term step is often a straightforward family conversation about which spaces and times are device-free, and why. Kids who understand the reasoning behind a rule are more likely to follow it — and the AAP's current language gives parents a clear, non-punitive framework to work from: screens are fine in balance, and balance means they don't replace the things that matter most.

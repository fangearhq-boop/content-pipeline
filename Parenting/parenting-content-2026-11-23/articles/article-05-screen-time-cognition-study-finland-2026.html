New Study: More Screen Time in Childhood Linked to Faster Teen Cognition — With an Important Catch

By Jamie Rivera November 23, 2026

A new study from Finland is likely to generate significant attention in parenting circles over the coming days, so it's worth reading past the headline before drawing conclusions.

Researchers at the University of Jyväskylä and the University of Eastern Finland followed 260 children for eight years as part of the PANIC (Physical Activity and Nutrition in Children) project, tracking their screen time from childhood through adolescence. The finding getting attention: children with higher cumulative screen time from childhood showed better cognitive processing speeds at around age 15.8 years. That runs counter to what many parents have been told to expect.

What the Research Actually Found

The finding is real, but the nuance is important. The children with more screen time scored better on cognitive processing speed — meaning they responded to stimuli more quickly. But those same children also showed lower accuracy on certain cognitive tasks. Faster, yes. But not necessarily better across the board.

The study was also observational by design. It tracked what children did and what their cognitive outcomes were — it didn't control for or even measure what those children were doing on screens. Were they playing educational games, watching videos, doing something interactive, or some mix of all three? The research design can't answer that question.

Lead researcher Petri Jalanko was explicit about this limit: "It depends on what kind of activities children are doing on screen."

That quote is the most useful thing in the entire study for practical parenting purposes. It points toward what other research has also suggested: the content and context of screen use matters far more than raw time.

What This Doesn't Mean

This study doesn't mean that more screen time produces smarter kids. It doesn't overturn the established evidence that sedentary screen time displacing sleep, physical activity, and face-to-face interaction carries real costs. And it doesn't change the AAP's guidance on healthy media use for children and adolescents, which continues to emphasize balance, content quality, and parental involvement in screen choices.

One study, even a well-designed longitudinal one, doesn't rewrite the literature. What it does do is contribute to a more complex picture — one where the dominant "screens are always harmful" narrative doesn't fully capture what's happening for all children in all contexts.

What It Might Mean for COS Families

The practical implication is a reframe rather than a rule change. If the question is "how many hours of screen time is too many," this study doesn't give a useful answer. If the question is "what should my kids be doing on screens," it supports the emphasis on active, engaged, interactive use over passive consumption.

Colorado Springs families navigating screen time decisions during a holiday week when kids are home from school and normal schedules are disrupted might find that framing more useful than a hard-hour limit. What are they doing? Are they creating, problem-solving, communicating, or just consuming? The difference matters more than the clock.

What's Next

The study was published in a peer-reviewed journal and covered by ScienceDaily and the University of Jyväskylä. It's one piece of a large and ongoing body of research on children's media use and development. Parents who want to review the AAP's current media use guidelines for children can find them at HealthyChildren.org.

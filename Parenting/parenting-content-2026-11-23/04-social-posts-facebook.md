### STORY 1: SHEIN Baby Rattle and Teether Recall — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 1
**Scheduled:** 8:00 AM MT

🚨 If you've bought anything from SHEIN for a baby or toddler, check this recall before the holiday season gets into full swing.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission announced on November 12 that approximately 2,657 SHEIN Baby Rattle and Teether Toy Sets are being recalled because the purple and orange knobs can detach — creating a choking hazard for children under age 3. The sets were sold on SHEIN.com from November 2025 through July 2026 at around $10. No injuries have been reported, which is exactly why acting now matters.

If you have this product at home (look for SKU sk25070936134444119 on the packaging), stop use immediately and contact SHEIN for a full refund. Their recall hotline is 833-853-8668, available 9 AM to 6 PM.

With holiday shopping in full swing, it's worth making CPSC.gov a quick stop before buying any baby or toddler toy as a gift. Choking hazards are among the most common — and most preventable — toy-related injuries for children under 3.

Did you know CPSC.gov has a free recall alert system you can sign up for? It delivers new recalls directly to your inbox.

### STORY 2: CHE-US Magnetic Robot Toys Recall — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 1
**Scheduled:** 10:00 AM MT

⚠️ Heads up for anyone with kids on the younger side — this is the second magnetic toy safety action from the CPSC in one week, and the scale of this one is significant.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission has recalled approximately 101,282 CHE-US magnetic robot and stick figure toy sets (Recall #27-096), sold on Amazon from April 2025 through August 2026 for $9 to $18. The sets come in packs of 5 or 10 and have packaging code X004N3MGCB. The issue: the magnets are high-powered and violate the mandatory toy safety standard. If a child swallows two or more of them, the magnets can attract each other through the walls of the intestines — causing perforations that require emergency surgery and can be fatal.

If you have these at home, stop use immediately and contact CHE-US for a full refund. And if you bought magnetic toy sets from Amazon in the last year and a half, it's worth looking up the product on CPSC.gov to confirm it isn't affected.

This follows Wednesday's CPSC warning on FZChenrry Magnetic Fidget Ball toys. Two actions in one week is a pattern — magnetic toys are having a rough November. When shopping for the holidays, any toy with high-powered magnets marketed toward kids deserves an extra check.

Have you ever looked up a toy on CPSC.gov before buying? It takes about 30 seconds and it's worth making it a habit.

### STORY 3: D11 Underused Schools — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 2
**Scheduled:** 12:00 PM MT

📚 Some news out of D11 this week that's worth understanding — especially if you have kids in the district.

At a November 18 board work session, D11 presented enrollment and building capacity data for the current school year. The findings: 11 district campuses meet state criteria for being "underused." If you're thinking that sounds like a prelude to school closures, the district has been explicit that closures are not being pursued. Instead, D11 is looking at co-location — bringing in charter schools or programs to share building space.

Here's the enrollment context: D11 had 23,458 students in 2025-26, up from 22,265 the year before — but still more than 3,000 below the district's peak enrollment in 2001-02. Elementary schools are running at 81% capacity right now, and projections show that could fall to 73% over the next decade.

For most D11 families, this is background information rather than urgent news. No specific schools were named for action, and the co-location strategy is still in the planning stages. But it's worth knowing that the district is managing a real long-term enrollment challenge — and that the approach they're choosing is to fill buildings with new programs, not close them.

### STORY 4: AAP 2026-27 Flu Guidance — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 2
**Scheduled:** 3:00 PM MT

🤧 With Thanksgiving travel coming up and flu season arriving, here's the guidance that matters for Colorado Springs families right now.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has published its 2026-27 influenza recommendations, and the core guidance hasn't changed: every child 6 months and older should receive an annual flu vaccine, unless there's a medical reason not to. The recommendation comes after a severe 2025-26 season — nationally, there were 190 pediatric deaths from influenza last year.

A few updates worth knowing about this season: all US-licensed flu vaccines are now trivalent (the Afluria formulation has been discontinued), and antiviral guidance has been updated. There's also a notable wrinkle — the AAP and CDC now have differing guidance in some areas following changes at the federal level in 2026. If you ask your pediatrician about the flu shot and they reference AAP recommendations specifically, that's why.

If your kids haven't had their flu shot yet, Thanksgiving week — with travel and family gatherings — is exactly the wrong time to skip it. Local pharmacies and pediatric offices across Colorado Springs are offering vaccines. It's not too late to schedule before the holiday.

### STORY 5: Screen Time / Cognition Study — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 2
**Scheduled:** 5:00 PM MT

📱 This research is going to get a lot of headlines over the next few days — and the nuance matters.

A study published last week followed 260 Finnish children for eight years, tracking their screen time from childhood through adolescence. The finding that's getting attention: kids with more cumulative screen time actually showed better cognitive processing speeds as teenagers. If your first reaction is "wait, that can't be right" — that's the right instinct to interrogate.

Here's what the research actually found, and what it didn't. Faster cognitive processing is a real result from the data. But the same group also showed lower accuracy on certain tasks — faster, but less careful. The study was observational, meaning it tracked what happened, but didn't control for what kids were doing on screens. Were they playing educational games? Watching videos? Doing something interactive? The researchers explicitly noted that their data couldn't distinguish between those uses.

The lead researcher put it plainly: "It depends on what kind of activities children are doing on screen."

The practical takeaway for COS families isn't "more screens equals smarter kids." It's something more useful: the content and context of screen use matters more than the total hours. That's consistent with what most pediatric guidance already suggests — quality over quantity.

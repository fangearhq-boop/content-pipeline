44% of Kids Aren't Getting Enough Sleep — What the 2026 NSF Poll Found, and What Helps

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | August 27, 2026

Nearly half of all children in the United States are not consistently getting the sleep they need. That's the headline finding of the National Sleep Foundation's 2026 Sleep in America Poll, and it lands at a moment when Colorado Springs families are three weeks into a new school year and the early-morning grind is already real.

The poll found that 44% of children don't consistently receive the recommended amount of sleep for their age. And it found something equally important: parents are regularly underestimating how much sleep their children actually need — by more than an hour per day, across every age group.

Why the Gap Matters

The consequences of insufficient sleep in children aren't subtle. Nearly 70% of parents who responded to the poll said poor sleep visibly affects their child's mood and daytime functioning. Eighty-six percent said that when their child does get good sleep, behavior and mood improve noticeably.

Parents know sleep matters. The problem is the gap between that knowledge and the actual hours. When parents consistently underestimate sleep needs, the family's bedtime routine gets built around an incorrect target — and children go to school chronically under-rested without anyone necessarily recognizing it as a sleep problem.

Children ages 6 to 12 need nine to twelve hours of sleep per night. Teenagers need eight to ten. Preschoolers ages three to five need ten to thirteen hours, including naps. These recommendations come from the American Academy of Pediatrics and are reflected in the NSF guidance. If your family's current bedtime math doesn't reach these ranges, that's worth adjusting.

What Actually Helps

The poll's findings point to a few evidence-based strategies, and recent research adds one that's particularly relevant for COS families heading into fall sports season.

Regular exercise improves children's sleep quality. A study examining the relationship found that after eight weeks of consistent physical activity, children's sleep habits measurably improved. Moderate-to-vigorous exercise was the key variable — and that stress-reduction effect also translated into better sleep duration. Fall sports registration is open in most D11, D20, and D49 schools and programs right now. Building physical activity into a child's routine has compounding benefits that go beyond fitness.

Other practices the research supports:

  • Consistent bedtime and wake time — even on weekends — helps anchor the body's natural sleep rhythm.
  • Screen-free wind-down in the thirty to sixty minutes before bed reduces sleep-onset difficulty.
  • Dark, cool sleeping environment — our high-altitude climate makes cool nights a natural advantage here in COS.
  • Gradual shifts — if your child's bedtime needs to move earlier, shift it by fifteen minutes every two to three days rather than all at once.

What's Next

The four-to-six-week window after school starts is often when sleep patterns stabilize — for better or worse. If your child is consistently tired, irritable in the morning, or struggling to focus after three weeks back, the sleep schedule is worth examining before looking at other explanations.

The American Academy of Pediatrics maintains sleep resources for families at healthychildren.org. The National Sleep Foundation's full 2026 poll results are available at thensf.org. Both are solid, evidence-based starting points if you want to dig into the specifics for your child's age group.

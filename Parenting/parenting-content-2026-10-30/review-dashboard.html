Harkla Sensory Swing Recall — Stop Using Immediately
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post If you have a Harkla compression sensory swing in your home, we need you to stop and check something right now. CPSC announced a recall on October 29 covering approximately 31,075 Harkla children's compression sensory swings. The issue is the ceiling mounting plate — specifically the 4-hole version. That plate can separate from the mount during use, sending the swing and the child inside it to the floor. CPSC has recorded 29 incidents and 14 injuries, including 2 concussions and a broken arm. The good news: it's only the 4-hole ceiling hook plate version that's affected. If yours has a 2-hole plate, it's safe to keep using. And Harkla is offering a free hardware replacement kit — you don't have to toss the whole thing. To get your fix kit, go to harkla.co/pages/ceiling-hook-recall-2026 or call 844-442-7552. The affected SKUs are LS-COMP-1BLU and LS-COMP-1BLU-SW. Swings sold between March 2020 and December 2025 for roughly $100–$130. For families with sensory-seeking or neurodivergent kids, these swings often get daily use — which is exactly why this one needs immediate attention. Stop using it until the fix is in place. Does your household have one of these swings? US Measles Cases Hit 4,588 — Highest Since Elimination, COS Schools Should Know
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post Here's a number that should prompt you to pull out your kids' vaccination records this weekend: 4,588. That's the confirmed US measles case count as of October 22 — the highest annual total since measles was declared eliminated from the US in 2000. Three people have died. Roughly double last year's already-troubling count. And 96% of the 2026 cases were in people who were unvaccinated or had unknown vaccination status. Here's the piece that matters most for parents of school-age kids: kindergarten MMR vaccination coverage has dropped to 92.4% nationally. That's down from 95.2% just five years ago, and well below the roughly 95% threshold needed to protect the kids who can't be vaccinated. Children 5–19 make up the largest share of 2026 cases (42%). Children under 5 make up 17%, and 15% of those young cases required hospitalization. MMR is a two-dose vaccine. Most kids get the first dose at 12–15 months and the second at 4–6 years. If you're not sure your kids are current, a quick call to your pediatrician's office or a check of your state's immunization registry can confirm it. You don't need an appointment just to check records. Do your kids have both MMR doses on file? AAP Says Pediatricians Should Check Your Teen's Teeth at Every Well Visit
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post You probably expect your teen's annual well visit to cover height, weight, blood pressure, and maybe a mental health screen. But according to a new clinical report from the AAP, there's something that's been slipping through the cracks: oral health. The AAP published "Oral Health for Adolescents" in Pediatrics on October 19 with a clear recommendation: every teen well visit should include a dental history and a basic oral exam — checking for pain, bleeding gums, dry mouth, and any changes in how your teen eats or drinks. Not instead of going to the dentist. In addition to it. Why the attention now? A few reasons. Tooth decay is still the most common chronic disease in teens, often because they've aged out of routine pediatric dental care but haven't built the habit of making their own appointments. And the report specifically flags vaping, cannabis, and alcohol as oral health risk factors that should come up during well visits — things a dentist might not ask about but a pediatrician is already screening for. If your teen's doctor doesn't currently do this, you can ask for it at the next visit. It takes a few minutes and catches problems early. Does your teen see a dentist regularly alongside their yearly physical? We're curious where people land on this 👇 ### Image Post Caption New AAP guidance: your teen's pediatrician should assess oral health at every well visit — including screening for vaping and dry mouth. Post-Halloween Candy Guide — What to Do With All That Loot
NOT VERIFIEDFACEBOOK
### Long-Form Post Tomorrow is the big night — and by Saturday morning, there's going to be a lot of candy in your house. Here's what the dentists actually say about navigating the post-Halloween haul, and it's more manageable than you might expect. The most effective thing you can do isn't to limit how much candy they eat on any given day. It's when they eat it. Candy consumed with a meal is less harmful than candy eaten as a standalone snack, because saliva production is highest during meals and helps neutralize the acid that causes decay. That's a real thing, not a parenting cop-out. A few other practical guidelines: brush teeth the same night as trick-or-treating, not just "before bed" — the sugar has been sitting on teeth for hours. Avoid hard candies and toffee for kids under 6; they're the worst offenders for dental damage and choking. And children under 2 shouldn't have any candy at all. For the surplus candy (and there will be surplus), Operation Gratitude accepts Halloween candy donations for military care packages. Given that COS is home to Fort Carson and Peterson SFB, it's a donation that hits close to home. One last thing: ration. Not as punishment, but as pacing. A little every day over a few weeks is genuinely better than a candy free-for-all over a weekend. What's your family's Halloween candy strategy — strict limits, let them go nuts the first night, something else? 👇 ### Image Post Caption After tomorrow's haul: eat candy with meals, brush that night, skip the toffee and jawbreakers for little ones, and consider donating extras to Operation Gratitude (they send it to military care packages).

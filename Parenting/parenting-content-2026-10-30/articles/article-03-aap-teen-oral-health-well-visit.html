AAP: Your Teen's Pediatrician Should Be Checking Their Teeth at Every Well Visit

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting

October 30, 2026

Most parents expect their teenager's annual well visit to cover the standard checklist: height, weight, blood pressure, vision, maybe a mental health screen. A new clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics says there's something that's been left off too often: oral health.

The AAP published "Oral Health for Adolescents" in its journal Pediatrics on October 19, 2026. The guidance calls on pediatricians to take a brief dental history and perform a basic oral exam at every teen well visit — assessing for dental pain, bleeding gums, dry mouth, difficulty eating, and any changes in oral function. It's meant to be a bridge, not a replacement for regular dental care.

Why Teens Are Falling Through the Gap

Dental care is often most consistent in early childhood, when parents schedule and supervise everything. As kids move into adolescence, they're increasingly responsible for their own appointments — and that transition doesn't always go smoothly. Many teens quietly age out of regular dental visits without anyone noticing until a problem is already established.

Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease among adolescents, and it's largely preventable. The new AAP guidance recognizes that pediatricians are often the most consistent point of contact with teenagers during this gap period, even when dental care has slipped.

New Risk Factors on the Screening List

The clinical report specifically calls on pediatricians to ask about behaviors that affect oral health in ways previous generations didn't deal with at the same scale: vaping, cannabis, and alcohol. All three affect saliva production, gum health, and the protective tissue in the mouth. Vaping in particular has been linked to dry mouth and gum inflammation even in teenagers who don't consider it a significant habit.

Dry mouth is worth flagging because it's easy to miss. Saliva isn't just for digestion — it constantly neutralizes acid from food and bacteria and helps remineralize enamel. Without it, decay progresses faster. Teens who experience dry mouth from any cause should know to tell their dentist, not just wait for a toothache.

What This Means for Your Teen's Next Visit

You can ask your teen's pediatrician to add oral health to the well-visit checklist. If their practice has already adopted the AAP guidance, it may already be happening. If not, a simple "can you take a look at their gums and ask about their dental history?" is a reasonable request and takes only a few minutes.

In parallel, confirm your teen has an established dental provider and that their last exam was within the past year. Many families with solid dental routines through early childhood find those routines drifting around middle school. The AAP's goal with this report is to catch that drift before a problem develops.

What's Next

At your teen's next annual physical, ask whether oral health is part of the well-visit check. If your teen hasn't seen a dentist in the past year, schedule an appointment. And if dry mouth, gum bleeding, or dental pain has come up and been dismissed as minor, the new AAP guidance supports bringing it up directly with both their dentist and their pediatrician.

The full AAP report is available at aap.org. A parent-friendly summary is published at healthychildren.org.

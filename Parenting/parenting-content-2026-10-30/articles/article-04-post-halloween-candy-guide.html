After the Haul: What to Do With All That Halloween Candy

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting

October 30, 2026

Tomorrow night, somewhere between the first house and the last, your kids are going to collect more sugar than most adults eat in a month. The average trick-or-treater hauls approximately three cups of sugar worth of candy on Halloween night. That pile will still be sitting on your counter on Sunday morning, probably sorted by category and fiercely negotiated over.

Here's the practical guidance for what comes after — from what dentists actually recommend to what to do with the candy nobody wants.

The Dentist-Approved Approach to the First Few Days

Counterintuitive as it sounds, dentists don't necessarily say to strictly limit how much candy kids eat. What they say is to pay attention to when they eat it. Candy consumed with a meal produces less dental damage than candy eaten as a standalone snack. The reason is saliva: during meals, saliva production is highest and works to neutralize the acid that causes decay. A handful of candy after dinner is genuinely less harmful, in dental terms, than the same candy eaten alone mid-afternoon.

Two exceptions to this logic: hard candies that stay in the mouth for long stretches (like lollipops or jawbreakers) and sticky candy that clings to teeth (like toffee, caramels, and gummies). Both are among the worst offenders for dental damage regardless of when they're eaten, because they prolong contact time with teeth. For kids under 6, these should be set aside or parents should eat them themselves.

The other absolute: brush teeth on Halloween night. Not "before bed tomorrow" — actually the same night the candy is consumed. The sugar will have been sitting on teeth for hours by the time kids get home. Waiting until morning gives it that many more hours to work.

Age-Specific Guidelines

Children under 2 should not have any Halloween candy. This isn't about sugar alone — many candies are choking hazards for toddlers, and young children's teeth are especially vulnerable to early decay. If you have a very young child trick-or-treating for the first time, their job is the costume and the experience. The candy comes home with a parent.

Children ages 2 through 4 can participate in the haul but should have candy only in small amounts, with supervision, and only soft candies with no small parts or hard pieces. Skip the lollipops, hard candies, gum, and anything with a chewy center for this age group.

For school-age kids, the goal isn't restriction — it's pacing and timing. A little candy every day for a few weeks is a better pattern than a weekend-long candy free-for-all followed by nothing. Rationing doesn't need to be punitive; framing it as "let's make it last" often works better than hard limits.

What to Do With the Surplus

Every household ends up with candy nobody wants. For COS families, there's a straightforward option: Operation Gratitude accepts Halloween candy donations for inclusion in care packages sent to active-duty military members. Given that Colorado Springs is home to Fort Carson, Peterson Space Force Base, Schriever SFB, and NORAD, this donation has a direct connection to people in our community.

Dentist offices often run their own Halloween candy buyback programs as well, purchasing candy by the pound and donating it. Check with your family's dental provider — many practices in COS run these programs in the first week of November.

What's Next

By November 1, most of the Halloween planning is behind you. The candy haul is a week-long project, not a one-night event. Keep the candy somewhere inconvenient (out of a kitchen bowl), stick to meal-time treats when possible, and brush every night. The dentist visit can wait for its usual schedule — there's no need for an emergency sugar-damage check unless your kids had a particularly hard-candy-heavy night.

If you're donating to Operation Gratitude, their candy collection information is at operationgratitude.com. Most local drop-off programs run through early November.

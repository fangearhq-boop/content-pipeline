How to Set Holiday Expectations with Kids So the Season Doesn't Disappoint Anyone

By Sarah Morales | November 13, 2026

Evergreen

The holiday season looks very different from a 5-year-old's perspective than it does from an adult's. And a lot of holiday-season stress in families comes from that gap — not from bad behavior or ungrateful kids, but from a mismatch in what everyone was expecting.

Child development research is fairly consistent on this: kids under 7 understand giving and receiving concretely, not abstractly. They experience the holidays as a series of specific events — a specific gift, a specific cookie, a specific moment. The idea of "family togetherness" or "the spirit of the season" isn't something a 4-year-old can hold onto. That's not a character flaw; it's a developmental stage.

Understanding that gap is probably the most useful thing you can do before Thanksgiving kicks the season into full gear.

Set Expectations Out Loud, Before the Event

The most reliable way to reduce meltdowns and conflict around holiday gatherings is to set expectations explicitly before you go. This sounds obvious, but most of us don't actually do it.

"We're going to Grandma's house. There will be cousins you haven't seen in a while. We'll eat a big meal. After lunch, we'll open gifts. It might feel long." That takes 30 seconds and dramatically improves outcomes. Kids who know what to expect are far better equipped to handle situations that feel overwhelming or unfamiliar.

Specific concrete information works better than general encouragement. "Be good" doesn't tell a kid anything. "We're going to be there for about three hours, and then we'll come home" does.

Gratitude Practices That Actually Work

Gratitude practices are well-supported in developmental psychology as genuinely good for kids' wellbeing — not just as a nicety, but as something that builds measurable resilience over time. The holiday season is a natural opportunity to practice them, but the approach matters.

A few that work for young kids:

  • Before an event: "What are you most excited about today?" Simple, concrete, positive. Gets kids focused on what they're anticipating rather than anxious about what's unfamiliar.
  • After receiving gifts: Practice the response before the gathering, not in the moment when emotions are running high. "What do you say when someone gives you something?" is a reasonable question at home on a Tuesday. It's a terrible question when a 5-year-old is disappointed by what they opened in front of Grandma.
  • End-of-day reflection: "What was your favorite part?" for young kids, or "Tell me one thing that went well" for older kids. Brief and specific.

Experiences vs. Gifts: What Research Says

Family experiences tend to create stronger long-term memories than material gifts — this is consistent across developmental and psychological research. Kids often don't feel that way in the moment. The gift they wanted feels more important right now than the ice skating trip you're planning. That's normal.

But the ice skating trip will be remembered more vividly than most gifts from the same season, often within a year. That's worth knowing as you're allocating the holiday budget and calendar.

When the Season Genuinely Feels Like Too Much

Some kids find the holidays genuinely overwhelming. Increased stimulation, disrupted schedules, unfamiliar environments, more social demands than usual — these are real stressors, not excuses. Kids who struggle with sensory processing, anxiety, or transitions are more likely to have a hard time during the holiday season, and knowing that in advance lets you plan for it rather than be blindsided by it.

Having a low-stimulation exit option at family gatherings — a quiet room, a walk outside, an agreed-upon signal between parent and kid — can be the difference between a difficult afternoon and a genuinely manageable one.

**Long-Form Post (10:00 AM MT)**

Colorado Springs School District 11 just passed the largest bond in El Paso County history — $775 million approved by voters last week. But the conversation didn't stop at the ballot box.

Protesters gathered outside D11 offices this week demanding the public release of an independent investigation into Superintendent Michael Gaal and Deputy Superintendent Terry Comfort. Now the school board is proposing a citizen oversight committee to monitor how bond money gets spent — a structure some community members say should've been built into the ballot measure from the start.

For families in D11, this is worth watching. An $80 million rebuild of Holmes Middle School is planned, and bond spending decisions will shape district facilities for a generation.

### STORY 3: Fall 2026 Baby and Toddler Toy Recalls: What Colorado Springs Parents Need to Check Right Now

**Long-Form Post (12:00 PM MT)**

Holiday shopping season is starting — and before you buy, wrap, or receive baby and toddler gifts this year, there are a few recalls you need to know about.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission has issued multiple recalls for infant and toddler products this fall. The highest priority: SHEIN Distribution Corporation's Pull and Chew Montessori Teething Toys (recall #26-782, September 17, 2026 — roughly 644 units sold on SHEIN.com between December 2025 and April 2026 for about $10). The silicone strings can reach a baby's throat and cause choking or respiratory distress. Full refund available from SHEIN.

HABA USA also recalled Rainbow Rattle Grasping and Teething Toys for choking and ingestion hazards. Costway's Babyjoy High Chairs were recalled for entrapment and suffocation hazards.

If you've bought any of these — or think you might have one from a baby shower or secondhand — stop use immediately and check cpsc.gov/Recalls for remedy instructions.

### STORY 4: The AAP Just Changed Screen Time Guidelines — Here's What Colorado Springs Families Actually Need to Know

**Long-Form Post (3:00 PM MT)**

The American Academy of Pediatrics updated its screen time guidance in 2026, and if you've been trying to enforce strict hour limits with school-age kids, the new framework has some good news for you.

For kids under 18 months, the guidance still recommends avoiding screens except video chat. For ages 2–5, up to one hour of high-quality content daily still applies. But for school-age children and older, the AAP has moved away from counting minutes entirely.

The new "5 Cs" framework — Child, Content, Calm, Crowding out, and Communication — asks a different question: what are screens replacing? If your kid's screen time is cutting into sleep, outdoor play, movement, or face-to-face connection, that's the problem. If it isn't, counting minutes was never the real issue.

That's a meaningful shift for families who've been having daily battles over arbitrary limits. The focus is now on context and quality, not the clock.

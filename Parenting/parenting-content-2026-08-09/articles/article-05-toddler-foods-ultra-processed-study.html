81% of Toddler Grocery Foods Are Ultra-Processed, Study Finds

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | August 9, 2026

If you've ever felt vaguely suspicious of the toddler food aisle, a new study from the journal Pediatrics gives that instinct some numbers to stand behind.

Researchers audited nearly 2,800 toddler and baby food products sold in U.S. grocery stores and found that 81% of toddler food products qualified as ultra-processed. The finding was published in Pediatrics, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics, and it adds a significant data point to a growing conversation about what young children are actually eating.

What "Ultra-Processed" Means

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations that go well beyond basic cooking or preservation. They typically contain few recognizable whole-food ingredients and often include additives — emulsifiers, flavor enhancers, colorings, and preservatives — that wouldn't appear in a home kitchen. They tend to be high in added sugar and sodium and low in the fiber, protein, and nutrients found in minimally processed foods.

The classification system researchers use — known as NOVA — groups foods into four categories based on how much industrial processing they've undergone. Ultra-processed is the top tier: think shelf-stable snack pouches, puffed grain products, and sweetened yogurt drinks formulated specifically for young children.

What makes the toddler food finding particularly striking is that many of these products are marketed in ways that make them appear nutritious. The study specifically noted that words like "organic," "natural," and "made with real vegetables" frequently appeared on products that researchers classified as ultra-processed. The front of the package and the actual ingredient list can be telling very different stories.

Why It Matters for Toddlers

The toddler years are when children are building their earliest food preferences. Research consistently shows that what children eat in this developmental window shapes their palates and eating habits for years, sometimes decades, later. Repeated exposure to highly processed flavors — ultra-sweet, ultra-salty, intensely flavored — can make whole foods harder to accept over time.

The American Academy of Pediatrics has been moving its guidance toward whole foods and away from heavily processed options for young children, and this study reinforces why. The concern isn't limited to any single nutrient — it's the overall pattern of what a child's diet looks like during a period when it's still highly malleable.

At the same time, the study's authors weren't calling for parents to achieve perfection. Convenience matters. Not every family has time to prepare every meal from scratch, and access to whole foods varies. What the data does is provide a clearer picture of what's in the market — so families can make better-informed decisions about the products they choose.

What You Can Actually Do With This

A few practical things that follow from this research:

The most reliable check is the ingredient list, not the front of the package. If the first few ingredients aren't recognizable whole foods — oats, apples, sweet potatoes, chicken — that's worth noting. A long list of additives, modified starches, or sweeteners in various forms (including fruit concentrate and juice) is a signal that the product is more processed than it looks.

This doesn't mean throwing out everything in the pantry. But it does suggest that the toddler food market has a significant gap between how products are presented and what's actually in them. A product marketed specifically for toddlers isn't necessarily designed with their nutritional needs as the primary priority.

Whole foods — fruit, soft-cooked vegetables, legumes, eggs, plain yogurt, soft grains — don't require label scrutiny. They're also often cheaper than packaged toddler foods, which tend to carry a premium for the convenience and branding.

What's Next

The Pediatrics study is a useful data point for any family thinking about what their toddler is eating — not a reason to panic, but a reason to read labels. If you want to go deeper, the AAP's nutrition resources for toddlers at healthychildren.org are updated regularly and reflect current guidance. And if you're shopping with a specific question about a product, flipping to the ingredient list is still the most direct answer. The front of a package is marketing; the ingredient list is the truth.

### STORY 1: Liam Stewart School Zone Act

#### Long-Form Post

If you drive anywhere near a school in Colorado Springs, there are some new rules you need to know about — and they take effect this Wednesday.

The Liam Stewart School Zone Act (HB26-1318) is a new Colorado traffic safety law that goes into effect on August 12, 2026 — the same morning D11 students walk through their doors for the first day of school.

Here's what changes:

School zones are now defined as roadways within at least 1,000 feet of a school's property boundary. That's a significant expansion from current zones. Required signage will notify drivers that fines are doubled within these areas.

There's also a new "school street" designation — roadways immediately adjacent to school property where the speed limit is 10 mph, and drivers must yield to all pedestrians and cyclists.

The law is named for Liam Stewart, a Littleton 8th grader who was struck and killed while biking to school in 2023. His death prompted advocacy by families and state legislators, and the bill passed the House 42-21 before being signed by the Governor on May 29.

For Colorado Springs families, Wednesday morning is a double milestone: it's the first day of school for D11, and it's the day every driver near a school faces the new legal standard.

While you're reviewing the rules: since May 2025, ALL drivers in ALL directions must stop at least 20 feet from a school bus with flashing red lights, regardless of whether there's a painted median.

#### Long-Form Post

D11 holds a Jumpstart Day on August 11 specifically for students entering a new building level — kindergarteners, incoming middle schoolers, and new high schoolers. A quick reminder: D11 is also handing out free Edukit school supply bags for K-8 students on the first day, August 12. No need to scramble for supplies if your student is in that grade range.

### STORY 3: CPSC Recalls — PIXLABBY Magnets + Wnttmt Walkers

#### Long-Form Post

There are two product recalls this week that Colorado Springs parents need to know about, and one of them involves a confirmed hospitalization.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission has recalled PIXLABBY Magnetic Fidget Sliders. About 860 units were sold on Amazon between October 2025 and May 2026 for approximately $10. The hazard: high-powered magnets can be separated from the silicone casing. If a child swallows two or more of these magnets, they can attract each other through intestinal walls — causing perforations, blockage, blood poisoning, or death. The CPSC confirmed that one child removed the silicone casing, ingested two magnets, and was hospitalized.

If you have this product: stop use immediately. Email ah1998zxc@gmail.com with a photo of the disposed toy to receive a full refund.

The second recall involves Wnttmt Baby Walkers — approximately 1,310 units sold on Amazon. The walker is narrow enough to pass through a standard doorway and fails to stop at the edge of a step. That creates a serious fall and entrapment risk. Contact wbabywalker-recall@outlook.com to arrange a refund.

Both of these products were sold on Amazon and may still be in homes. If you have them — or if you know someone who does — please share this.

Do you keep track of CPSC recalls? It's worth bookmarking cpsc.gov/Recalls — they've had 17 recalls in a single day recently.

### STORY 5: Toddler Foods Ultra-Processed

#### Long-Form Post

Here's a number that might make you look twice at the toddler food aisle on your next grocery run: 81%.

That's the share of toddler food products that researchers found to be ultra-processed in a new study published in Pediatrics, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics. The study audited nearly 2,800 toddler food products sold in grocery stores.

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations that typically contain little in the way of whole food. They're often high in added sugar, sodium, and additives — and the front-of-package labeling frequently makes them look far healthier than they are. Words like "organic," "natural," and "made with real vegetables" can appear on products that the researchers would classify as ultra-processed.

Toddlers are building eating habits that can last a lifetime. Research consistently shows that early food environment shapes long-term preferences. That's part of why the AAP has been shifting its guidance toward whole foods and away from heavily processed options for young children.

This doesn't mean every packaged food is off the table — convenience matters, and not everyone has time to cook from scratch at every meal. But it's worth knowing that the marketing on many of these products doesn't always match what's actually in them.

The best check: flip the package over and read the ingredient list. If the first few ingredients aren't recognizable whole foods, that's a signal.

What's your approach to food shopping for your toddler? Any brands you trust — or ones you've stopped buying?

AAP Screen Time Guidelines: What COS Families Need to Know as School Starts

By Sarah Morales | August 19, 2026 | COS Parenting

The school year is underway across all three Colorado Springs school districts, and that means the summer screen time situation has collided with homework, earlier bedtimes, and a different daily routine. It's one of the better moments of the year to revisit what pediatricians actually recommend — and to understand how that guidance has shifted.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) has updated its approach to screen time in recent years, moving away from strict minute-per-day limits toward a framework that emphasizes quality, context, and conversation. Here's what families need to know.

The Current AAP Recommendations by Age

Age Group AAP Guidance
Under 18 months Avoid screen use other than video chatting with family
18-24 months If introducing media, choose high-quality programming; watch together to help them understand what they see
Ages 2-5 Up to 1 hour per day of high-quality content; co-view when possible
Ages 6 and up Set consistent limits on time and type; prioritize sleep, physical activity, and homework first

What's Changed: Quality Over the Clock

The older AAP guidance leaned heavily on time limits — two hours per day was the widely-cited threshold for older children. The updated framework still acknowledges time as one factor, but it now places more emphasis on what children are watching and how they're watching it.

Co-viewing — watching together and talking about what's on screen — is one of the most protective behaviors families can adopt. It turns passive consumption into an active conversation. A child watching an educational program with a parent asking questions is having a very different experience than the same child watching alone with autoplay running.

The AAP also notes that not all screen time is equivalent. Video chatting with grandparents, using an interactive educational app, and watching hours of algorithmically-served short videos are not the same activity — even if they're all happening on the same device.

Making It Work in the School Year

A few practical approaches that fit the current AAP thinking:

Create screen-free zones and times. The dinner table and the hour before bed are natural starting points. The AAP's updated guidance aligns with existing research showing screens before sleep interfere with both falling asleep and sleep quality.

Treat screens as one activity among many, not the default. When the school day is done and homework is finished, if a child chooses a screen over playing outside or reading, that's one thing. But if screens are the automatic answer to any unstructured moment, it's worth building in some variety.

Have the conversation. The AAP's shift toward quality and context assumes families are actually talking about what kids are watching and why. For older kids especially — middle schoolers and high schoolers — that conversation matters more than the strict time count.

What's Next

The full AAP screen time guidance is available at HealthyChildren.org, the AAP's parent-facing resource site. If you're working with a pediatrician in the Colorado Springs area on specific screen-time concerns — particularly for kids with ADHD, anxiety, or sleep challenges — they can help tailor these guidelines to your family's situation. The AAP general guidance is a starting point, not a one-size prescription.

CDC: Teen Mental Health Is Improving — But Colorado Springs Parents Should Still Pay Attention

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | September 30, 2026

There is genuine good news in the latest data on teen mental health — and genuine reason to stay engaged. The CDC released its 2025 Youth Risk Behavior Survey on September 21, and the headline numbers represent the most meaningful improvement in years.

Among high school students nationally, 33% reported persistent feelings of sadness or hopelessness in 2025. That's down from 40% in 2023 and from a peak of 42% in 2021. The percentage who seriously considered suicide also dropped — from 20% in 2023 to 14% in 2025. These are real improvements that represent hundreds of thousands of young people moving in a better direction.

What the Improvement Means — and Doesn't Mean

Mental health researchers and educators who reviewed the CDC data have urged caution before declaring victory. The current rates still exceed pre-pandemic levels — in 2017, approximately 31.5% of high schoolers reported persistent sadness, a number that was itself already concerning. The 2025 improvement brings us close to that mark, but not below it.

More significantly, the improvement is not evenly distributed. Girls continue to report sadness and hopelessness at substantially higher rates than boys. LGBTQ+ youth face rates significantly higher than their heterosexual peers. For families with daughters or with LGBTQ+ teens, the overall positive trend does not reflect their children's likely experience.

EdWeek, which analyzed the report in depth, framed the expert concern clearly: the trend is encouraging, but this is not the time to ease up on support, investment in school mental health services, or attention at home.

The Screen Time Connection

The same survey tracked digital behavior. Thirty-six percent of students reported using social media every hour or more — statistically similar to the 37% reported in 2023. If the mental health metrics are improving while social media use stays roughly constant, that challenges simple narratives about social media as the singular driver of teen distress — though the research on that relationship remains complex and ongoing.

What This Means for Colorado Springs Families

For parents with middle or high schoolers, the CDC data offers context rather than prescription. The numbers tell you that the overall trend is moving in the right direction — which is worth acknowledging with your teen. But the data also tells you that struggles remain real and widespread, particularly for specific groups.

The most practical use of this information is as a conversation starter. "I read some data about how teens are doing nationally — how are you doing?" is a low-stakes opening that many teenagers respond to better than direct questions about feelings.

What's Next

The AAP Retired the 2-Hour Screen Time Rule: What the New 5 C's Framework Means for Your Family

By Jamie Rivera | COS Parenting | August 25, 2026

In February 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics officially retired the two-hour-per-day screen time limit for children ages 2 and up. The change reflects years of pediatric research showing that time-counting is too blunt a tool to capture what actually matters about children's media use. What replaced it is more nuanced — and in some ways more demanding — than a clock.

Quick Reference

  • Change: Two-hour daily limit for ages 2+ officially retired, February 2026
  • New framework: 5 C's — Child, Content, Calm, Crowding Out, Communication
  • Still in place: No screens before 18 months (video calls a limited exception)
  • Ages 2–5: No more than 1 hour of high-quality content per day
  • Key finding: Co-viewing changes outcomes significantly vs. solo screen use
  • Family tool: healthychildren.org/mediauseplan

What the 5 C's Mean

The 5 C's framework shifts the question from "how long?" to "how, what, and with whom?" Each C addresses a different dimension of a child's media experience.

Child means considering the individual child — their age, developmental stage, temperament, and any vulnerabilities. A 7-year-old processing a family stress and a 7-year-old with no particular challenges may be affected very differently by the same amount of screen time.

Content means evaluating what the child is actually watching or doing. Educational programming that a caregiver has previewed is different from algorithmically served entertainment designed to maximize watch time.

Calm refers to the context and the child's state — is screen time being used as a calming tool for a dysregulated child, or as part of a structured routine? The AAP notes that using screens to calm a distressed child can interfere with developing other self-regulation strategies.

Crowding Out asks what screens are displacing. Sleep, physical activity, creative play, and face-to-face conversation should not be consistently lost to screen time. This C is especially relevant for school-age children whose days are already structured.

Communication means talking about media — asking what a child watched, discussing what happened, pointing out what is real and what is not. Co-viewing and conversation change the developmental impact of screen time significantly.

What Has Not Changed

Several rules remain in place from the AAP's prior guidance. Children under 18 months should still avoid screen media other than video calls with family. Children ages 2 through 5 should still be limited to no more than one hour of high-quality content per day, watched with a caregiver whenever possible. For older children, the AAP recommends a consistent Family Media Plan — a family-agreed set of rules that considers all five C's rather than a universal time cap.

The Finding That Stands Out

A March 2026 study found that for children with developmental vulnerabilities — including autism spectrum disorder and attention-related differences — even 10 to 30 minutes of solo screen time per day showed measurable negative effects on certain outcomes. Crucially, the study found that co-viewing with an engaged caregiver significantly changed those outcomes. The implication is not that screen time is uniformly harmful for these children, but that how it is used matters enormously, and that caregivers sitting alongside their child changes what screen time does to that child's development.

What's Next

The AAP's Family Media Plan tool is available at healthychildren.org. It walks families through building a plan that fits their specific household — considering the ages of children, the types of devices in the home, and the contexts in which media gets used. Colorado Springs families can also bring questions about screen time and child development to their pediatrician during a well-child visit. The new framework does not make the answers simpler — but it does make them more honest.

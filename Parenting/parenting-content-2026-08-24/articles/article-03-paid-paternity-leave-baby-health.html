Paid Paternity Leave Benefits Babies: What the 2026 Research Shows and Why It Matters for Colorado Springs Families

By Sarah Morales | COS Parenting | August 24, 2026

Most discussions about parental leave focus on the mother — the recovery time, the bonding time, the logistics of returning to work. But a growing body of research in 2026 is making a different argument: paid paternity leave matters for babies, not just for fathers.

A report from STAT News published August 17, 2026 reviewed the latest pediatric evidence on the subject, finding that access to paid paternity leave is linked to better health outcomes for newborns and to lower rates of anxiety and depression in fathers during the critical postpartum period. Fathers who are present, mentally healthy, and engaged in those first weeks make a measurable difference to infant outcomes. That presence depends heavily on whether they can afford to take the time.

What the Research Found

A June 2026 study from Northwestern University and the Ann and Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago analyzed data from the Ohio Fatherhood Survey (2022–2023), one of the most comprehensive sources on fathers' experiences during the perinatal period. The researchers found that fathers who do not have access to paid leave face significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression after birth. Those outcomes affect how present and engaged they are with their newborns in the weeks that follow.

Separately, a study published in AAP Pediatrics analyzed 11.4 million live singleton births using 2016–2022 birth certificate data, comparing perinatal outcomes in jurisdictions with and without paid family leave policies. The study found that paid leave policies improve perinatal health outcomes at scale. A separate review found that longer paternity leave was also associated with improved breastfeeding duration.

The picture that emerges from these studies is consistent: when fathers can take meaningful time after birth — not just a few days, but weeks — the benefits extend beyond father wellbeing to the health of their babies and to family stability.

The Gap Between What Research Recommends and What Fathers Actually Take

Despite this evidence, the 2026 National Parent Survey from New America found that fathers take an average of just three days of leave after the birth of a child. Mothers average 7.2 weeks. That gap exists for many reasons — workplace culture, financial pressure, limited or no paid leave available — but the gap itself has consequences that the research now makes difficult to ignore.

What This Means for Colorado Springs Families

Colorado Springs is home to a significant military community — active-duty personnel at Peterson Space Force Base, Schriever Space Force Base, and Cheyenne Mountain Space Force Station. Federal law provides military families with parental leave policies that civilian families often do not have access to. For the broader Colorado Springs community, leave policies depend on the employer and the state.

Colorado does not currently mandate paid paternity leave beyond federal requirements. Families navigating this issue can review their employer's leave policies, check eligibility for Colorado's Family and Medical Leave Insurance program (FAMLI), or speak with a benefits coordinator about how to maximize available time.

What's Next

The research on paid paternity leave and infant health is continuing to grow, with more studies expected through the end of 2026 and into 2027. Colorado parents can visit colorado.gov/famli for information on the state's paid family leave program. For Colorado Springs families with military connections, visit military.com/benefits/military-pay/leave for federal military parental leave details. COS Parenting will continue to cover new research on parenting and family health as it emerges.

AI Is Coming to Your Child's Pediatrician — What the AAP Wants Families to Know

By Jamie Rivera | November 22, 2026

Quick Reference

Publisher American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP)
Published October 2026, in Pediatrics journal
Guidance title "Safe and equitable pediatric use of generative AI"
Who it covers Clinicians using AI tools in pediatric settings
Family guide In development — not yet published
Key principle Families deserve transparency when AI is involved in their child's care

The American Academy of Pediatrics published new guidance in October 2026 on the use of generative AI in pediatric clinical settings — the first formal framework from the country's leading pediatric medical organization on how AI tools should and shouldn't be used when it comes to children's health care. The guidance was published in the AAP's journal Pediatrics under the title "Safe and equitable pediatric use of generative AI."

For Colorado Springs parents, the practical takeaway is straightforward: AI tools are already showing up in pediatric offices, and the AAP is now telling clinicians what standards they're expected to meet when using them.

What the Guidance Covers

The October 2026 guidance is directed at clinicians — pediatricians, nurses, and clinical staff — not at families directly. It addresses the AI tools that have been making their way into medical practices in recent years: automated visit note-taking systems that listen to and transcribe appointments, clinical decision-support tools that flag potential diagnoses or drug interactions, and other generative AI applications that assist with the administrative and clinical work of a pediatric practice.

The AAP's framework addresses several key areas: how clinicians should evaluate AI tools before using them, how patient data should be protected, how clinicians should disclose AI use to families, and how to ensure AI tools work equitably across diverse patient populations. That last point reflects a known concern in AI-assisted medicine — that tools trained on non-representative data can produce systematically different recommendations for different patient groups.

Importantly, the guidance explicitly states that it does not cover the direct use of AI tools by children, adolescents, or families. That guidance — aimed at the family side of the equation — is currently in development and has not yet been published.

What This Means for a Pediatric Visit in Colorado Springs

If you've been to a pediatric appointment recently and noticed your child's doctor typing less than usual, or if the doctor mentioned that notes are being captured automatically, you may have already encountered an AI tool in a clinical setting. These note-taking systems — sometimes called ambient clinical documentation tools — record and summarize appointments so clinicians spend less time on paperwork and more time with patients.

The AAP guidance says families deserve transparency about when these tools are in use. That means your provider should tell you if AI is involved in generating your child's visit notes or contributing to clinical decisions — and you have every right to ask.

Beyond note-taking, some pediatric practices use AI-assisted clinical decision support tools that flag clinical patterns or suggest diagnostic considerations. These tools are designed to assist clinicians, not replace their judgment. But understanding that they exist is part of being an informed participant in your child's health care.

Questions Parents Can Ask at Their Next Appointment

Until the AAP's family-specific guide is published, here are three practical questions parents can bring to a pediatric appointment:

  • Are AI tools used in this practice for note-taking or clinical decisions? Any practice using these tools should be able to answer yes or no clearly.
  • How is my child's health data protected when AI tools are in use? HIPAA applies, but the specifics of data handling with third-party AI tools vary by vendor and practice.
  • Can I opt out of AI-assisted documentation? Some practices allow patients to decline automated transcription.

These aren't adversarial questions — they're the kind of informed engagement the AAP's guidance is designed to encourage. Most pediatric providers will appreciate the conversation.

What's Next

The AAP's family-focused guide on generative AI is in development and expected to be published at a later date. When it's available, it will address how families should think about their own use of AI health tools — including consumer AI assistants, symptom checkers, and similar applications — in the context of their children's health. The AAP's full October 2026 guidance is available through the Pediatrics journal and summarized at healthychildren.org, the AAP's family-facing resource site.

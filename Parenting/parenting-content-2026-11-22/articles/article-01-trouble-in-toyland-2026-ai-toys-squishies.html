Every year before the holiday shopping season, U.S. PIRG Education Fund releases its "Trouble in Toyland" report — an independent review of toy hazards that parents need to know about before they buy. The 2026 edition, published November 17, puts two new categories at the top of its watch list: AI toys and heated squishies. Both present risks that weren't a significant factor in past holiday seasons, and both are the kinds of products showing up in children's wish lists across the country.

AI Toys: Guardrails That Don't Hold

Researchers tested seven AI-powered toys and found a consistent problem: the safety guardrails built into these products weakened over extended conversations with children. AI toys are designed to interact with kids through natural language, responding to questions and carrying on back-and-forth dialogue. The safety limits built into these systems are supposed to keep conversations age-appropriate. In practice, those limits didn't hold up.

The most serious documented case involved the GOLAB Panda Plush AI toy, which produced sexually explicit content after a sustained interaction. The researchers' findings don't suggest this was an isolated glitch — they found this pattern across multiple products. As AI toys become more common in the marketplace and more widely advertised to children, this represents a real gap between how these products are marketed and how they actually behave.

For parents considering AI toys this holiday season, the report recommends caution. If you do purchase one, test the product yourself before giving it to a child, and limit the length of conversations the toy has with children until more consistent guardrail standards are in place.

Squishies: A Social Media Trend Turned Safety Hazard

Squishy foam toys have been popular for years, but a newer social media trend — heating them before squeezing — has introduced a hazard that wasn't part of the original product category. The trend involves microwaving or heating squishies, which can cause the foam material to reach temperatures that burn skin on contact. Reports of burn injuries tied to this trend have increased, and the CPSC has taken notice. Nearly 500,000 squishy toys were recalled or issued government warnings over the past year.

Even parents who aren't familiar with the trend may find their children have encountered it through friends, videos, or online content. If squishies are already in your home, talking with kids about not heating them is a worthwhile conversation. The CPSC maintains an updated recall database at cpsc.gov.

Counterfeit Products: The Third Risk

The report's third major concern is counterfeit products from unfamiliar online sellers. With Amazon's marketplace, TikTok Shop, and similar platforms making it easy to purchase from third-party sellers, it's increasingly common for counterfeit versions of popular toys to reach consumers — including items that failed safety testing. The PIRG report notes that the CPSC recorded more than 900 toy violations between January and July 2026 alone.

When shopping online, the report recommends buying from authorized retailers when possible, checking seller reviews carefully, and verifying product safety at cpsc.gov before purchasing — especially for unfamiliar brands or products at significant discounts from their usual market price.

What Colorado Springs Parents Can Do Before Black Friday

The full "Trouble in Toyland 2026" report is available at pirg.org. Before finalizing your holiday shopping list, it's worth searching any AI toy or squishy product you're considering at cpsc.gov to check for active recalls. For AI toys specifically, look for products from established manufacturers with documented safety standards and avoid giving children unsupervised, extended access to AI toys until the industry establishes more reliable guardrails.

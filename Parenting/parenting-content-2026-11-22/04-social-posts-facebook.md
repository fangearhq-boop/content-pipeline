### STORY 2: Qpancy Pajamas Recall — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 1
**Scheduled:** 10:00 AM MT

🚨 If you've ever ordered children's pajamas on Amazon, this recall is worth checking right now.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission announced on November 19 that approximately 1,309 Qpancy children's pajama sets are being recalled (Recall #27-094) because they don't meet the mandatory flammability standard for children's sleepwear. That standard exists for a reason — pajamas that don't pass it pose a risk of serious burn injuries or death. The sets were sold on Amazon from March through August 2026 at around $20.

If you have Qpancy pajamas at home, the CPSC's guidance is simple: stop dressing your children in them immediately and contact Qpancy for a full refund. With the holidays coming and plenty of cozy pajama gifting ahead, it's also a good reminder to check the labels on any children's sleepwear you buy — it should say either "flame resistant" or "snug-fitting." If it doesn't have one of those labels, don't buy it.

This is the third children's sleepwear recall we've covered this fall. The holiday shopping season tends to bring a surge of budget-priced sleepwear from unfamiliar brands — when in doubt, check CPSC.gov before you buy.

### STORY 4: AAP AI Guidance for Pediatricians — LONG-FORM POST
**Type:** FB Long-Form
**Story Tier:** 2
**Scheduled:** 3:00 PM MT

🩺 AI is showing up in your child's pediatrician's office — and now there are formal guidelines for how it should be used.

The American Academy of Pediatrics published new guidance in October 2026 on "safe and equitable pediatric use of generative AI" — the first formal framework covering how AI tools like automated note-taking and clinical decision support should work in pediatric settings. The guidance is directed at clinicians, not families, and it emphasizes that families deserve transparency when AI is involved in their child's care.

What does this mean practically? Your child's doctor may already be using AI to generate visit notes or flag clinical decisions. The AAP's guidance says that's acceptable — with the right safeguards in place. A separate family-focused guide is in development, but it isn't out yet.

As a parent, you have every right to ask your provider what AI tools are in use at their practice and how your child's health data is protected. Pediatric offices are still figuring this out, and your questions help move that conversation in the right direction. Full article at the link.

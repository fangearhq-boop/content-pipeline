Kawhi Investigation: Forbes Warns NBA Ruling Could Create League-Wide Salary-Cap Loophole

By Marcus Cole | Hoop Heroes | August 21, 2026

The Kawhi Leonard investigation has been a slow-moving story all summer — fact-finding completed, sanctions expected, trade on hold. But this week, a Forbes analysis added a new dimension that reframes the entire saga: however the NBA rules, the decision could inadvertently create a salary-cap loophole that benefits teams across the league for years to come.

That's the warning published by Forbes on August 18, and it's one the NBA will need to navigate carefully as it prepares to announce the outcome of its investigation into the Los Angeles Clippers and owner Steve Ballmer.

What the Investigation Covers

The outside law firm Wachtell Lipton was retained by the NBA to investigate allegations of salary-cap circumvention by the Clippers. Two deals are at the center of the probe: a $28 million agreement with Aspiration, a financial company in which Ballmer personally invested, and a deal with Daktronics, the scoreboard manufacturer that supplied the jumbotron for Intuit Dome.

The allegation in both cases is roughly the same: that these companies received payments from the Clippers effectively as a channel for funneling additional compensation to Kawhi Leonard, beyond what the NBA's salary rules permit. If true, it would constitute circumvention — one of the more serious violations a franchise can commit.

The NBA has already cleared Leonard personally. Multiple sources confirmed there is no evidence Ballmer funneled money directly to Leonard. That clears a significant hurdle for the pending trade to the Toronto Raptors, which has been on hold while the investigation runs its course.

The Forbes Warning

The precedent angle is where it gets complicated. Forbes' analysis argues that depending on how the NBA defines "circumvention" in its ruling — specifically, how direct the connection between a sponsor and a player must be to constitute a violation — the decision could leave a meaningful gray area that other teams rush to exploit.

Essentially: if the NBA rules that the Aspiration or Daktronics deals were violations because of Ballmer's personal investment in one and his team's physical relationship with the other, future teams will calibrate their own "creative" deals to stay just on the permissible side of that line. The boundary, once drawn, becomes a roadmap.

Commissioner Adam Silver is acutely aware of this dynamic. The NBA's enforcement of its salary rules has historically been aggressive precisely because cap circumvention, left unchecked, can hollow out competitive balance. The Clippers case is high-profile; the standard it sets will be scrutinized.

Where the Trade Stands

The trade package — Leonard to the Toronto Raptors for Brandon Ingram, Gradey Dick, and future draft picks — remains officially on hold. League sources continue to indicate that the deal will ultimately be cleared once the investigation concludes. The Raptors are preparing as if the trade completes; their training camp opens September 28 in Quebec City at Université Laval.

The Clippers, meanwhile, face the prospect of sanctions — fines, possibly draft pick penalties — that will accompany whatever ruling the league issues. Silver has said he hoped for resolution before the 2026-27 season begins; an ESPN report has flagged the possibility of the timeline extending into 2027 if the parties dispute Wachtell Lipton's findings.

What's Next

Watch for the NBA's formal announcement of sanctions against the Clippers, which sources say is coming. Once that announcement lands, the Leonard trade to Toronto should follow shortly after. Training camp opens for most teams on September 29 — the league has a natural incentive to resolve this before rosters begin formal workouts. The Forbes precedent concern will likely resurface in any future "creative" sponsorship deals — the loophole question won't disappear when the investigation closes.

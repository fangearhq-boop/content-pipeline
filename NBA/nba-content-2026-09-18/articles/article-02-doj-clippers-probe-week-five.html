DOJ Clippers Probe Enters Week Five — No Charges, Investigation Ongoing

By Marcus Cole | Hoop Heroes | September 18, 2026

Los Angeles Clippers

The Department of Justice's criminal investigation into the Los Angeles Clippers has crossed into its fifth week. Federal prosecutors in the Eastern District of New York continue probing whether the organization arranged no-show sponsorship deals worth approximately $18 million for Kawhi Leonard — a scheme, if proven, that would represent a deliberate circumvention of the NBA's salary cap rules. No charges have been filed. The investigation remains in what multiple reports describe as its "earliest stages."

The NBA season opens in 32 days. The Clippers begin a new chapter without Leonard, operating under the league's penalties while a separate federal inquiry runs in the background. Both processes move at their own speed. The DOJ's timeline is entirely its own.

The NBA Penalties — Still in Force

The league's own investigation concluded months ago, and its penalties are in place. The Clippers were fined $30 million and forfeited five first-round draft picks covering 2029 through 2033. Owner Steve Ballmer received a one-year suspension from all league and team activities. President Lawrence Frank was suspended six months. Team executive Gillian Zucker drew a one-year suspension. Governor Peter Robertson received the most severe league sanction, a five-year ban. Leonard was fined $700,000 and his trade to the Toronto Raptors was ratified rather than voided.

Three companies allegedly paid Leonard roughly $18 million in total for endorsement arrangements that NBA investigators determined had little to no actual work performed. The scheme was designed to route money to Leonard outside the official salary cap structure — a direct violation of league rules.

The Federal Track Is Different

The DOJ's inquiry operates under entirely different standards and timelines than the NBA's internal process. Federal criminal investigations at their "earliest stages" can mean months of grand jury work, additional subpoenas, witness interviews, and document review before any charging decision is made. At least one subpoena has been issued — to Daktronics, a scoreboard and display company that was one of the entities involved in payments to Leonard.

Criminal prosecution would require proving intentional fraud beyond a reasonable doubt — a substantially higher bar than the league's internal investigation, which uses a preponderance of evidence standard. Federal investigators may conclude that the conduct, while clearly in violation of NBA rules, does not rise to federal criminal statutes. Or they may conclude it does. The public record gives no indication either way.

The Clippers' Situation

For the franchise itself, the 2026-27 season represents something of a rebuilding year under penalty. The organization has lost five first-round picks in the years ahead, its most prominent executive team has been suspended, and the player at the center of the controversy is now in Toronto. The arena — the Intuit Dome — is modern and formidable, and the team still has talent. But they walk into camp with a shadow that is not entirely of their own making.

What the DOJ ultimately decides will determine whether that shadow has a longer reach than the league's penalties already establish.

What's Next

There is no announced timeline for the DOJ investigation. Federal inquiries at this stage can proceed for months before any public development. The NBA season opener is October 20. The Clippers will take the court while the federal investigation continues. Watch for any new subpoenas, grand jury activity, or DOJ statements as indicators of the probe's direction — but expect silence to be the default for some time. The NBA chapter of this story is finished; the federal one has barely begun.

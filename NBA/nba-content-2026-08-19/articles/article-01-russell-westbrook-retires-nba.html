Russell Westbrook Retires: 209 Triple-Doubles, 2017 MVP, 18 Seasons of Pure Intensity

By Jake Torres | Hoop Heroes | August 19, 2026

Russell Westbrook is retiring from the NBA, ending one of the most singular careers in the history of professional basketball. The nine-time All-Star, 2017 Most Valuable Player, and all-time triple-double record holder has called it a career after 18 seasons — a run defined by relentless effort, breathtaking athleticism, and statistical achievements that may never be replicated.

The numbers alone demand a pause: 209 career triple-doubles. Oscar Robertson, the previous holder of the all-time record, had 181. Westbrook did not approach Robertson's mark — he obliterated it, adding 28 more with the kind of regular-season dominance that had analysts searching for historical precedent and coming up empty.

A Career Unlike Any Other

Westbrook entered the league as the fourth overall pick in the 2008 NBA Draft, selected by the Seattle SuperSonics before the franchise relocated to Oklahoma City. What followed over the next decade in OKC was a partnership with Kevin Durant that produced some of the most electric basketball the league had seen — two young stars pushing each other to their limits in a small market that fell in love with both of them.

When Durant left for Golden State in 2016, most expected Westbrook to fade. Instead, he delivered one of the most extraordinary individual seasons in NBA history. In 2016-17, Westbrook averaged a triple-double — 31.6 points, 10.7 rebounds, and 10.4 assists per game — for an entire 82-game season, becoming only the second player ever to accomplish the feat and winning his first and only MVP award. Then he did it again. And again. And again — averaging a triple-double for four consecutive seasons from 2016 through 2020.

The debate over what those numbers meant — whether he was the game's ultimate winner or a stat-chaser who put himself ahead of team success — never fully resolved. What could not be argued was the work ethic and the athletic gift. Westbrook ran the floor harder than anyone in the league. He attacked every possession with a ferocity that made casual observers stop scrolling.

The OKC Legacy and What Comes Next

Westbrook's retirement lands at a meaningful moment for the Oklahoma City franchise. Last week, Kevin Durant posted a tribute to his former teammate, calling Westbrook's career "iconic" and describing him as "quiet and methodical" — a reframing of the narrative that had surrounded their complicated relationship for a decade. The tribute read like the beginning of a public reconciliation.

More significantly, Jalen Williams — the Thunder's current star — pushed for the organization to retire Westbrook's No. 0 jersey, suggesting the ceremony could happen as soon as Opening Night. The home opener falls on October 22. If OKC moves forward, Westbrook would become only the second player in franchise history to have his number retired, joining Nick Collison — a testament to how deeply the Oklahoma City community embraced him during the franchise's most competitive years.

The Hall of Fame clock starts now. With 209 triple-doubles, a scoring title, nine All-Star appearances, and the 2017 MVP, Westbrook's case for enshrinement is straightforward. The bigger question is how quickly the Basketball Writers association and Hall voters move on the narrative — whether they see him as one of the five most complete statistical players in NBA history, or as a player whose style made his teams harder to build around.

What's Next

The immediate next step for the OKC franchise is making the jersey retirement announcement official. Westbrook's Hall of Fame eligibility begins in three years. For NBA fans, the retirement also brings a natural reckoning: in an era where player movement and roster construction dominate the conversation, Russell Westbrook spent 10 of his 18 seasons in Oklahoma City, becoming the face of a franchise in a way that fewer and fewer stars choose to do. That loyalty, whatever else you think about his game, deserves acknowledgment. The career is complete. The legacy debate is just beginning.

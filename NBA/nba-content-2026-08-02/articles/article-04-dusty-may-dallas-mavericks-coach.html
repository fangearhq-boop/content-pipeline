Dusty May Takes the Leap: Michigan's NCAA Champion HC Is Now the Dallas Mavericks' Coach

By Marcus Cole | Hoop Heroes | August 2, 2026

In April, Dusty May was cutting down nets in the NCAA Tournament. His Michigan Wolverines had just won the national championship, cementing his place among the best coaches in college basketball.

By August, he is in the NBA.

May has been hired as the head coach of the Dallas Mavericks, joining a 2026 coaching class that includes five other new NBA head coaches across the league. The college-to-pro jump is always scrutinized, always debated, and sometimes spectacular. May arrives with more credibility than most college coaches who have made it — he arrives with a championship.

What May Brings to Dallas

May built a reputation at Michigan for player development and offensive creativity. His Wolverines ran a system that maximized spacing, ball movement, and individual skill expression — principles that translate, at least philosophically, to the NBA game. The question for any college-to-pro transition is never whether the Xs and Os carry over. It is whether the coach can build authority in a locker room full of grown men who are paid more than he is.

Championship coaches tend to earn that authority faster. A ring, even a college one, is a universal language in basketball. Players know what it takes to win one. May has demonstrated he can build a program, manage egos, develop talent, and peak at the right moment. Those skills don't have a league boundary.

A League-Wide Coaching Overhaul

May's hire is one of the most dramatic in a coaching offseason that saw six NBA teams turn over their head coaching positions. Taylor Jenkins took over in Milwaukee after a run in Memphis. Jamahl Mosley moved to New Orleans. Sean Sweeney was promoted from the Spurs' staff to lead Orlando. Tiago Splitter accepted the Chicago Bulls job after leading Portland to the playoffs in an interim capacity.

That kind of coaching churn typically signals either organizational dysfunction or aggressive rebuilding — and in some cases, both. Dallas falls into the latter category. The Mavericks are a franchise with history, tradition, and a fan base that expects competitive basketball. They hired May because they believe a championship-caliber mind can accelerate their development timeline.

The Risks Are Real

College coaches have a complicated track record in the NBA. For every Brad Stevens — who arrived in Boston from Butler and built a dynasty — there are coaches who found the translation difficult, the roster management demands overwhelming, or the lack of scholarship leverage disorienting. NBA players have more power than college players. The system has to accommodate the talent, not the other way around.

May will spend his first year learning those dynamics alongside a Dallas roster that is still developing its identity. The Mavericks are not a championship contender entering 2026-27. They are a franchise building toward one. May's first job is to create a culture that makes that building process real.

What's Next

May will formally be introduced to his Dallas roster when training camp opens September 29. His first look at how his offensive and defensive principles translate to an NBA context will come in the preseason, with regular-season games beginning in October. The NBA coaching landscape in 2026-27 is defined by six new voices trying to prove themselves — May's arc will be one of the most interesting to track.

He won a national championship in his final college game. The NBA version of that journey is just beginning.

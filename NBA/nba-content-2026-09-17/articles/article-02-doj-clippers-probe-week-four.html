DOJ Clippers Probe Enters Fourth Week — Subpoenas Out, No Charges Filed

By Marcus Cole | Hoop Heroes | September 17, 2026

Los Angeles Clippers

The NBA's chapter in the Los Angeles Clippers scandal closed on September 2, when Commissioner Adam Silver announced the league's penalties and walked out of the room. The federal government's chapter has not closed. It may be just getting started.

The U.S. Department of Justice's criminal investigation into the Clippers, run out of the Eastern District of New York, is continuing into its fourth week. Prosecutors are examining whether the team arranged for sponsors to secretly pay Kawhi Leonard approximately $18 million through three separate companies for what have been described as no-show endorsement deals. At least one subpoena has been issued. No charges have been filed. Federal investigators describe the probe as being in its "earliest stages."

Two Tracks, Two Very Different Outcomes

Understanding the Clippers' situation requires understanding the difference between what the NBA did and what the DOJ is doing. These are parallel tracks with different powers, different timelines, and different consequences.

The NBA penalties are settled. The league fined the franchise $30 million, stripped five first-round draft picks, suspended owner Steve Ballmer for one year, suspended president of basketball operations Lawrence Frank for six months, and suspended president of business operations Gillian Zucker for one year. Executive Tom Robertson received a five-year suspension. Kawhi Leonard was fined $700,000, and his trade to the Toronto Raptors was ratified — meaning he is no longer a Clipper.

Ballmer issued a compliance statement after the penalties were announced. The organization has said it will cooperate.

The DOJ probe is something else. The NBA can suspend and fine. The DOJ can indict and prosecute. Criminal charges — if they come — would represent a fundamentally different kind of exposure than any league penalty. The Eastern District of New York is not a sleepy prosecutor's office. It has handled some of the highest-profile white-collar cases in modern American legal history.

What "Earliest Stages" Means

When federal investigators describe a probe as being in its "earliest stages," it tells you very little and quite a lot at the same time. It tells you: no charges are imminent. No grand jury is reportedly seated. But it also tells you: the investigation is real, active, and working through the evidence trail.

At least one subpoena has been issued, targeting Daktronics — a company that had dealings with the Clippers. A subpoena is a legally compelled request for documents or testimony. Prosecutors do not issue subpoenas for investigations they do not intend to pursue. The paper is moving. The timeline is unknown.

What Happens to the Clippers Meanwhile

The Clippers are still an NBA franchise. They will still play games this season, open training camp on September 29, and participate in the preseason beginning October 3. The NBA's penalties do not prevent that. What they do is strip the team of significant future draft capital and remove their top executive and owner from day-to-day operations for most of this season.

On the basketball side, the roster that remains is a different Clippers team than the one that entered the scandal. Kawhi Leonard is in Toronto. The draft picks are gone. The organization is under scrutiny in a way no NBA franchise has faced in modern memory.

What's Next

The federal investigation has no public timeline. What to watch: any additional subpoenas, evidence of grand jury activity, or a DOJ announcement about the scope of charges — or a decision not to charge. Federal investigations of this type can take months or years. For now, the Clippers organization navigates a dual reality: playing through an NBA season under unprecedented penalties while a separate federal inquiry continues in the background. Neither track is closed.

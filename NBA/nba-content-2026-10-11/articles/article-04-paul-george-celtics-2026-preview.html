Paul George Is a Celtic Now — Boston's Biggest Bet Since the Big Three

By Jake Torres | October 11, 2026 | Hoop Heroes

The trade was stunning when it happened in July. Boston Celtics president Brad Stevens sent Jaylen Brown — a perennial All-Star, a cornerstone, and one of the best players in the Eastern Conference — to Philadelphia in exchange for Paul George and four future draft picks. The reaction was immediate and divided: a bold swing or a mistake that could haunt the franchise for years?

Nine days before the regular season tips off, that debate is still very much alive. But Paul George is a Celtic, and what he does this season will define whether that trade was genius or a cautionary tale.

The Numbers Make the Case

Strip away the injury history and look at what George produced last season in Philadelphia: 17.3 points per game on 43.9% shooting from the field, 39.2% from three-point range, 5.3 rebounds, 3.6 assists, and 1.7 steals per game. He was an excellent two-way wing who defended elite guards and forwards, stretched the floor at a high level, and made teammates better.

The problem: he only appeared in 37 of Philadelphia's 82 games. Whatever George is when healthy, the Sixers never got the full version of it. Boston is betting they will.

At 36, George arrives in Boston understanding the stakes better than anyone. He has been in championship environments before. He knows what a healthy version of himself at 36 can do. At his Boston introductory press conference, he sounded refreshed and motivated — a player energized by a fresh start and a genuine title contender around him.

What Boston Is Getting If It Works

The Celtics already have Jayson Tatum as their primary offensive engine and Jrue Holiday as a defensive anchor. Adding George gives Boston a third perimeter player who can guard the other team's best wing, hit threes off the catch, and make reads in the half court. In the playoffs — where schemes tighten and size and switching matter most — that combination is genuinely elite.

The Celtics beat Detroit in the East last season by operating at the margins: a switch here, a timely three there, a stop at the end of a quarter. George, at his best, makes every one of those moments easier.

The draft pick compensation is also significant. If George ages poorly and needs to be moved, those picks become the foundation for the next rebuild. Boston did not trade future assets for an aging star with nothing in return. The haul mitigates the risk considerably.

The Risk Is Real

Jaylen Brown, for all his limitations as a primary creator, was one of the most reliable players in the NBA. He showed up, he played, and he improved. Replacing that kind of availability with a 36-year-old who has played more than 56 games in a season just twice in the last five years is a genuine gamble.

If George misses 30 games — a conservative estimate based on recent history — the Celtics will need Tatum to carry a heavier load than he has in any previous season. That may be sustainable in the regular season. It becomes dangerous in the playoffs when opponents gameplan specifically to tire him out.

The early preseason indications are positive. George is healthy, engaged, and shooting well in workouts. But preseason optimism has a way of colliding with the reality of an 82-game season.

What's Next

Paul George makes his regular-season debut as a Celtic on October 20 in Detroit, against the No. 1-seeded Pistons. It's a marquee game for both teams, and George's performance in that building — the same one where Boston was eliminated last spring — will say a great deal about whether this trade was a stroke of genius or a step backwards.

The bet is placed. The chips are on the table. Boston's biggest gamble since they brought together Paul Pierce, Kevin Garnett, and Ray Allen starts nine days from now.

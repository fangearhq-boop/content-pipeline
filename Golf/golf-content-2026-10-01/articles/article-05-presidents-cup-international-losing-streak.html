The International Team Has Lost 11 Straight Presidents Cups. What Would Actually Change That?

By Jake Torres | Golf Fanrecap | October 1, 2026

The 2026 Presidents Cup at Medinah ended with Team USA winning 17-13. This was not a surprise. It was the 11th consecutive Presidents Cup won by the United States. The last time the International team won was 1998, at Royal Melbourne, where they beat the Americans 20.5-11.5 in one of the most dominant results in the event's history. That was 28 years ago.

What follows every American victory is a version of the same question: what would actually need to change for the International team to win one of these? The answers tend to cycle through the same categories — captaincy, format, player pool, preparation — without settling on one. That cycling is probably itself a symptom of the problem.

The Structural Disadvantage Is Real

The International team draws from every nation not named the United States or a European country. Its members come from Australia, New Zealand, Canada, South Africa, South Korea, Japan, and a rotating cast of other nations. This is a wide geographic net and, in theory, a deep talent pool — the world rankings at any given moment include players from several of these countries near the top.

The problem isn't the talent pool in isolation. It's that the International players don't have an equivalent preparation structure to the Ryder Cup. European Ryder Cup players spend two years in a recognized team framework — captains are named early, vice-captains are announced, and players understand the team context they're contributing to. The Presidents Cup International side has historically operated on a shorter timeline with less institutional continuity between cycles.

Jim Furyk will lead the United States at the 2027 Ryder Cup. Trevor Immelman, who captained the International side in 2022, is positioned to lead again in 2028. Immelman is an intelligent golfer and a thoughtful captain, but his two cycles have now produced two losses. The question facing whoever leads the International side isn't how to replicate what Immelman did — it's how to rethink a framework that hasn't worked in three decades.

The Format Argument

One persistent argument is that the Presidents Cup format — which mirrors the Ryder Cup in using foursomes, four-balls, and singles — inherently favors the USA because American players have more experience in the Ryder Cup's equivalent pressure. European players arrive at the Ryder Cup having spent years understanding the team golf environment. International players arrive at the Presidents Cup in some cases having had very little equivalent preparation.

Changing the format is politically complex. The event is administered jointly, and neither side has formal veto power over the other, but major structural changes require consensus. Past proposals have included adjusting the number of wildcard picks, altering the session formats, or changing the points system. None have been implemented.

A more achievable version of format reform would be internal: how the International captain uses picks, which pairings are deployed in which sessions, and how the team is built around its actual strengths rather than its prestige names. The Medinah loss, like several before it, featured the International side playing relatively conservative golf on the back half of the final session. That is a preparation and culture question as much as a talent one.

What's Next

The next Presidents Cup is scheduled for 2028. That is two years of preparation time — enough, in theory, for a different approach to produce different results. The International team has the players. The world rankings include multiple non-American, non-European golfers inside the top 15 at any given time.

But having the players has been true for most of the 11-event losing run. The gap isn't that the International side lacks talent. The gap is in everything surrounding that talent — how it's organized, how it's prepared, and how it's deployed under pressure in a team format that punishes individual thinking.

The 1998 Royal Melbourne win was the International team at its best: organized, confident, and cohesive. Twenty-eight years later, that version of the Presidents Cup International team hasn't reappeared. Whether 2028 changes that depends less on who wins the next few major championships and more on decisions being made right now, while the 17-13 defeat at Medinah is still fresh enough to be honest about.

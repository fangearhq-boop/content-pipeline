### STORY 1: Europe Wins Solheim Cup — Long-Form

It took until Sunday afternoon in the Netherlands — but Europe took back the Solheim Cup.

Final score: Europe 15, USA 13. A tournament that entered Sunday singles perfectly balanced at 8-8 ended with a stunning European display that turned the Bernardus Golf crowd into a cathedral of noise.

The winning moment came from an unexpected source. Lottie Woad, 21 years old, ranked world No. 5 on the women's golf rankings, stepped to the 17th hole against American Auston Kim knowing the weight of the match on her shoulders. She won 2&1 — and in doing so, delivered Europe the half-point they needed to reach the 14.5-point total required to lift the trophy. Europe's home crowd at Bernardus Golf lost its collective mind.

Carlota Ciganda finished the job two matches later, defeating Jennifer Kupcho 4&2. For Ciganda — playing in her eighth consecutive Solheim Cup — the win is the latest chapter in one of the most decorated records in Solheim history. Different generations, same blue and yellow, same result.

Team USA captain Angela Stanford — who won the Cup two years ago in the United States — will return home with a loss that stings precisely because her team had every chance. Going into Sunday level, with Nelly Korda and a deep roster, the Americans needed 6.5 points from 12 singles matches. They couldn't get there.

Europe reclaims the Solheim Cup. The next edition is in 2028. USA will be watching the tape from this Sunday all the way until then.

### STORY 2: Presidents Cup Preview — Long-Form

Ten days. That's all that separates the 2026 Presidents Cup from its opening tee shots at Medinah Country Club.

Brandt Snedeker's USA team is set. The 12-man roster is a mix of proven Presidents Cup warriors and at least one name that will have golf fans doing a double-take: Jackson Koivun, 21 years old, months removed from leaving Auburn to turn professional, has been named to his first Presidents Cup team by Snedeker.

The full USA roster: Scottie Scheffler, Cameron Young, Wyndham Clark, Russell Henley, Sam Burns, and Collin Morikawa earned automatic qualification. The six captain's picks: Chris Gotterup, Xander Schauffele, Justin Thomas, Patrick Cantlay, Jacob Bridgeman, and Koivun.

Schauffele and Cantlay each bring a 10-4-0 Presidents Cup record. Justin Thomas is 10-3-2. These three know exactly what a Presidents Cup week looks like, feels like, and requires. Thomas in particular has been one of the most clutch Presidents Cup players in the event's modern era.

On the other side: International captain Geoff Ogilvy, the 2006 US Open champion and one of the most respected figures in the game outside of North America, has assembled a team that will be fighting against historical odds. USA has dominated the Presidents Cup since its inception, winning 13 of the 15 editions held before this one.

Medinah Country Club brings its own weight. The venue has hosted three Ryder Cups — 1991, 1999, and the dramatic 2012 edition that Europe won by completing a stunning Sunday comeback. The Presidents Cup arrives at a course steeped in golf history and partisan American crowd energy.

September 24-27. The most anticipated team event in men's golf this year begins in ten days.

### STORY 3: LIV Golf Bankruptcy — Long-Form

LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on September 8, 2026. One week later, the legal process is underway — and the players who bet their careers on this tour are watching the courts to see how much of what they're owed they'll actually receive.

The financial picture from the filing: LIV listed estimated assets of $100 million to $500 million against liabilities of $500 million to $1 billion. That's a bankruptcy with hundreds of millions of dollars of exposure, and the names attached to that exposure are some of the biggest in professional golf.

Jon Rahm, who signed with LIV ahead of the 2024 season for a reported $300 million deal, is owed more than $100 million, according to reporting. He rejected a PGA Tour return offer earlier this year — with a tight deadline that Rahm declined — and now watches the bankruptcy proceedings as his principal creditor hope.

The Saudi Arabian Public Investment Fund, which invested an estimated $6 billion in LIV before ending its support after the 2026 season, has agreed to provide $49.6 million in debtor-in-possession financing to keep LIV operational through the Chapter 11 process. Court approval is pending.

The restructuring plan — developed with BC Partner Advisors LP — envisions a "LIV 2.0" in which the reorganized company is majority owned by the players themselves. Scott O'Neil, who took over as CEO, has spoken publicly about a reduced schedule with deeper player equity. Four 2027 events were reportedly already announced before the filing (Saudi Arabia, Australia, South Africa, Hong Kong).

Whether LIV 2.0 emerges from this process, and what form it takes, remains to be seen. But the era of Saudi-funded disruption in professional golf — $6 billion poured into the game over four turbulent seasons — is effectively over.

### STORY 4: Walmart NW Arkansas Championship — Long-Form

The women's golf world just went through one of the most emotionally charged weeks of the year — the Solheim Cup in the Netherlands, ending in a European victory. Now, 11 days later, the LPGA Tour returns to one of its most beloved regular season stops.

The Walmart NW Arkansas Championship, presented by P&G, takes place September 25-27 at Pinnacle Country Club in Rogers, Arkansas. This is the 20th anniversary of the event at Pinnacle CC — a $3 million purse, 144 players, and a golf market that shows up for women's golf the way few other cities in America do.

The field will include Nelly Korda — world No. 1, two-time major winner in 2026 (Chevron Championship and U.S. Women's Open) — returning from Solheim Cup duty in the Netherlands. For Korda, it's a quick turnaround: fly home from Europe, reset mentally from team golf, and compete in a 54-hole stroke play event against the full LPGA field.

The transition from Solheim Cup team golf back to individual stroke play is one of the most interesting psychological challenges in the sport. Some players light up in the weeks immediately after a Solheim Cup, riding the elevated emotions and competitive adrenaline into their next event. Others take a week or two to recalibrate.

European players who competed heavily in the Netherlands — including multiple players who went 5-0 in all sessions — will face that challenge too if they're in the Walmart NW Arkansas field. The players who transition fastest may have a built-in advantage.

Pinnacle Country Club, Rogers, Arkansas. September 25-27. Tickets are on sale. The LPGA tour rolls on.

### STORY 5: USA Solheim Cup Post-Mortem — Long-Form

It was there for the taking, and USA couldn't take it.

Going into Sunday singles at the 2026 Solheim Cup tied 8-8, the United States had everything they needed. Nelly Korda — world No. 1, two major championships in 2026, the most dominant player in women's golf right now. A deep bench of LPGA talent. A captain in Angela Stanford who knew exactly what it took to win a Solheim Cup, having done it two years ago.

They lost 15-13.

The critical moment came in Match 8, where Lottie Woad — 21 years old, making her second Solheim appearance, ranked world No. 5 — defeated Auston Kim 2&1. That result gave Europe 14.5 points and lifted the trophy. Carlota Ciganda made it official two matches later, beating Jennifer Kupcho 4&2 in what became a victory lap rather than a deciding contest.

The anatomy of USA's Sunday defeat will be studied carefully. A few factors stand out.

Europe's home crowd at Bernardus Golf was extraordinary. Dutch golf fans adopted the European team with genuine intensity, and throughout Sunday, the noise and energy level consistently benefited the home side in close matches where momentum matters.

USA's singles lineup, as constructed by Stanford, faced a European team that put its best players in the most important positions. Woad and Ciganda closing down the order — experienced players who know how to win when the crowd is behind them — proved to be the right European call.

For Korda and the Americans, the defeat will sting. The Solheim Cup returns in 2028, and the United States will have two years to build around the best women's player on the planet. But for now, the trophy is in European hands — and Europe has earned every bit of it.

USA's Solheim Cup Post-Mortem: How Europe Turned 8-8 Into a 15-13 Dutch Triumph

By Jake Torres | September 14, 2026

The United States entered Sunday singles at the 2026 Solheim Cup tied 8-8, with Nelly Korda on their roster and a captain who had won this event two years ago. They left the Netherlands without the trophy. Here is what happened — and what it means for American women's golf.

Going into Sunday singles at a Solheim Cup level at 8-8 is as close to a coin flip as team golf produces. The Americans had everything: the world No. 1 player in Nelly Korda, a deep and talented LPGA Tour roster, and a captain in Angela Stanford who knew exactly what winning a Solheim Cup required because she had done it two years ago on American soil.

They needed 6.5 points from 12 singles matches. They could not get there.

The final score was Europe 15, USA 13. The Solheim Cup returned to European hands on a Sunday afternoon at Bernardus Golf in the Netherlands, and the American side is left to process a defeat that will sting precisely because it was not inevitable. This was a winnable week. USA had the players to win it. Something went wrong on Sunday, and that something is worth examining.

The Critical Match

Lottie Woad was 21 years old and playing in only her second Solheim Cup when she stepped to the first tee of Match 8 against Auston Kim on Sunday. Everything about that matchup, on paper, suggested the experienced Kim would be the tougher competitor. Woad is the future of European women's golf; Kim is a proven LPGA Tour player with the competitive résumé to handle pressure moments.

Woad won 2&1. Her victory gave Europe their 14.5 points — the number required to lift the trophy — before several matches behind her were even finished. The moment Woad closed out Kim, the Solheim Cup was over. Match 11, where Carlota Ciganda defeated Jennifer Kupcho 4&2, was a victory lap.

The Woad-Kim result is the most significant single outcome of Sunday, and it deserves scrutiny. Was Kim in a position to win that match? Did the Bernardus Golf crowd, having adopted the European team with genuine and vocal intensity, play a role in how the match developed? These are questions Stanford will have spent the hours since Sunday trying to answer.

The Home Crowd Factor

Bernardus Golf is in the Netherlands. Europe was the home team. And the crowd that turned out at Bernardus Golf — on a Sunday afternoon with a Solheim Cup trophy on the line — was not neutral. Dutch golf fans adopted the European team with a passion that carried across every session of the week, but it was at its most intense in Sunday singles, when individual matches play out in relative isolation and crowd energy becomes one of the most powerful forces in team golf.

Momentum in Solheim Cup singles is contagious. When a European player makes a birdie and the crowd erupts, it carries to the next tee box in the match behind them. When a USA player misses a putt and the noise dies, that sound — or the absence of it — travels down the course. The home crowd at Bernardus Golf was, by multiple accounts, extraordinary in its intensity and its effect. Whether that was the deciding factor in USA's Sunday performance is impossible to prove, but it is impossible to dismiss.

Stanford will face questions about whether she accounted for the home crowd factor adequately in how she built her singles lineup — about which players she trusted to handle hostile noise and which might have been better served by a different position in the order.

Stanford's Decisions

Captains always face judgment on their lineup and pairing decisions, and Stanford will be no exception. She put together a USA team that went into Sunday with 8 points — the minimum required to be competitive — and structured a singles order that ended with Korda and her top players positioned to close.

The problem was that Europe had similar thinking. Woad, ranked world No. 5 and clearly playing the best golf of her young career, and Ciganda, with eight consecutive Solheim Cup appearances behind her, were in positions where they could deliver — and they did. Stanford's decisions about who played where in Sunday singles will be analyzed in the context of those results.

The more difficult question is whether there were specific matchups Stanford could have constructed differently. Sending different players to different positions in the order, giving Korda an earlier singles slot to generate momentum rather than a closing role, adjusting which veterans played where — these are the details that will be debated. Whether any of those changes would have produced a different result is unknowable. What is known is that the lineup as constructed produced a 15-13 defeat.

What's Next

The Solheim Cup returns in 2028. The United States will have two years to rebuild around Nelly Korda, who will be at the peak of her powers through that period, and a generation of LPGA Tour talent that runs deeper than the final score on Sunday suggests. Stanford will face scrutiny in the weeks ahead — about the singles lineup, the pairings strategy across the weekend, and the decision-making that led to a 15-13 defeat from a tied position. That scrutiny is deserved. So is the full credit that Europe has earned. They came to the Netherlands, built a home crowd, and won a Solheim Cup in front of it. The Americans will be watching the tape from this Sunday all the way until 2028.

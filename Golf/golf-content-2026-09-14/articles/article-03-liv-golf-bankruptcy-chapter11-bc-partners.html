LIV Golf's Chapter 11 Bankruptcy: PIF's $49.6M Lifeline, BC Partners Deal, and What It Means for Players Owed Millions

By Marcus Cole | September 14, 2026

One week after LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection, the legal and financial process is underway. The Saudi sovereign wealth fund is keeping the lights on. A restructuring plan is taking shape. And the players who bet their careers on this tour are watching the courts.

LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on September 8, 2026. One week later, the numbers are on the table — and they are significant. LIV has listed estimated assets of $100 million to $500 million against liabilities of $500 million to $1 billion. That gap, measured in hundreds of millions of dollars, is the financial reality that professional golf now has to absorb.

The players attached to those numbers include some of the biggest names in the sport. Jon Rahm — who signed with LIV ahead of the 2024 season for a reported $300 million deal — is owed more than $100 million, according to reporting. He is the most visible symbol of what happens when a player builds a career decision around a financial structure that is now in bankruptcy court.

The PIF's Emergency Financing

The Saudi Arabian Public Investment Fund invested an estimated $6 billion in LIV Golf before ending its support after the 2026 season. After four turbulent seasons, the era of Saudi-funded disruption in professional golf is effectively over.

But the PIF has not walked away entirely. It has agreed to provide $49.6 million in debtor-in-possession financing — emergency capital designed to keep LIV operational while the Chapter 11 process plays out. Court approval is pending.

DIP financing in a bankruptcy is not a gesture of confidence. It is a tactical decision that allows the lender to maintain a secured position in any restructured entity while keeping the business alive long enough to determine its salvage value. The PIF has $6 billion already committed to LIV. A $49.6 million bridge loan to protect some of that exposure is not a surprising move.

For the players who are owed money, the DIP financing matters in one specific way: it means the tour will continue operating during the restructuring, which means the possibility of a reorganized entity — "LIV 2.0," as it is being called — remains on the table.

BC Partners and the LIV 2.0 Plan

The restructuring plan that has emerged from the bankruptcy process was developed with BC Partner Advisors LP. The vision: a reorganized LIV Golf that is majority owned by the players themselves. LIV CEO Scott O'Neil has spoken publicly about a reduced schedule and deeper player equity as the organizing principles of the reorganized entity.

Four 2027 events were reportedly already announced before the filing — in Saudi Arabia, Australia, South Africa, and Hong Kong. Whether those events survive the bankruptcy process and become the foundation of a new league is one of the key questions the courts will resolve in the months ahead.

For the players, the question is simpler: how much of what they are owed will they actually see? In a Chapter 11 restructuring, that answer depends on where each creditor sits in the priority structure. Secured creditors — like the PIF with its DIP financing — get paid first. Unsecured creditors, which may include some of the player contracts depending on how they were structured, get what is left over after the secured claims are satisfied. In a bankruptcy where liabilities potentially exceed assets by hundreds of millions of dollars, the math for unsecured creditors is almost never good.

Rahm's Situation

Jon Rahm's decision to sign with LIV was one of the most consequential moves a professional golfer had made in years. He rejected the PGA Tour. He accepted a reported $300 million deal. He built his competitive future around a circuit that has now filed for bankruptcy.

Earlier this year, the PGA Tour extended a return offer with a tight deadline. Rahm declined. He is now watching the bankruptcy proceedings as LIV's largest reported individual creditor, with more than $100 million still owed to him under his contract. What he actually receives will be determined by the courts, by the restructuring plan, and by where his contract claims fall in the creditor waterfall.

Rahm is the face of the LIV bankruptcy in a way no other player is, simply because his reported exposure is the largest. But he is not alone. The players who signed with LIV — many of whom did so because of the financial security the contracts appeared to offer — are now navigating a legal process most of them did not expect when they made those decisions.

What's Next

Court approval of the PIF's $49.6 million DIP financing is the next procedural milestone. After that, the restructuring timeline will become clearer — whether LIV 2.0 emerges from bankruptcy as a player-majority-owned entity with a reduced schedule, or whether the process ends in liquidation rather than reorganization. The era of Saudi-funded disruption in professional golf consumed four seasons and an estimated $6 billion. Whether anything comes out the other side will be determined in the courts over the months ahead.

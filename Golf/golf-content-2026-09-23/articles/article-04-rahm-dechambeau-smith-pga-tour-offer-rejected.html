Rahm, DeChambeau, Smith Said No to the PGA Tour. Now the Clock Has Run Out.

By Marcus Cole | Golf Fanrecap | September 23, 2026

In January 2026, Jon Rahm, Bryson DeChambeau, and Cameron Smith had a decision to make. The PGA Tour had opened a narrow reinstatement window for LIV Golf players who had won a Major championship or The Players Championship between 2022 and 2025. The terms were expensive: a $5 million donation to charity and the forfeiture of five years of Tour equity. But the path was real. The door was open.

All three said no. Brooks Koepka said yes.

The LIV Golf bankruptcy restructuring deadline is now October 13 — 20 days from today. PGA Tour CEO Brian Rolapp confirmed on September 17 that the Tour is "not currently contemplating a returning member programme." The decision Rahm, DeChambeau, and Smith made in January is the decision they are living with now, in conditions that are considerably less favourable than they were then.

What the January Offer Actually Was

The reinstatement programme was structured deliberately. It was not a broad amnesty for all LIV players — it was targeted at the most commercially significant names, the ones whose presence on the PGA Tour would have the greatest impact on television ratings, sponsorship, and competitive credibility.

The qualifying window covered Major winners and The Players champions from the period when LIV Golf was most actively poaching PGA Tour members. Jon Rahm qualified twice over: he won the 2023 Masters and the 2023 US Open before signing with LIV. Bryson DeChambeau won the 2024 US Open. Cameron Smith won The Players in 2022.

Brooks Koepka also qualified — the 2022 and 2023 PGA Championships, plus the 2023 US Open — and he was the only one who calculated that $5 million and five years of equity was worth it to come back to the world's most competitive tour. He is back on the PGA Tour. His former LIV colleagues are watching a deadline approach.

Why They Said No

In January, each of the three players had reasons that were, at the time, coherent. LIV Golf was still operating. The Saudi backing that funded the tour showed no signs of withdrawal. The existing contracts were worth substantially more than the $5 million penalty the PGA Tour was asking. Accepting the deal meant accepting the framing that LIV had been a mistake, personally and professionally — a public concession that none of the three was prepared to make while the organisation they had joined was still running events and paying guaranteed contracts.

DeChambeau had been vocal about believing LIV Golf represented a genuine alternative model for professional golf. Smith, who had won The Players just before signing, was one of LIV's most prominent early signings and had shown no inclination toward publicly reconsidering. Rahm, the most decorated of the three by Major count, had joined LIV with a reported contract worth a sum that made the $5 million penalty look manageable only in comparison to what he was already earning.

The calculation made sense in January. It makes less sense now.

What's Next

The October 13 deadline will produce some kind of resolution to the LIV Golf bankruptcy. Whether that resolution involves a continued operation under restructured ownership, a sale, or a wind-down determines what the players' contracts are actually worth. It also determines whether the ranking points LIV events have been awarding continue to accrue — a question the Official World Golf Ranking and the Major championships will have to answer in their own time.

What will not happen, barring a significant change in the PGA Tour's stated position, is a new reinstatement window. Rolapp's statement was clear. The window that Koepka used is closed. For Rahm, DeChambeau, and Smith, the question is not whether the deal they declined in January will return. The question is what professional golf looks like for three Major champions without a major tour to play on.

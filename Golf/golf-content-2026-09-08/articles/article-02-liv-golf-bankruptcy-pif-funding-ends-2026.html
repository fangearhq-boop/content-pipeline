LIV Golf Is Preparing for Bankruptcy: Saudi Funding Gone, Staff Laid Off, Players Offered Cents on the Dollar

By Jake Torres | Golf Fanrecap | September 8, 2026

Four years after launching with the ambition of reshaping professional golf, LIV Golf is preparing to file for bankruptcy as Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund ends its reported $5-8 billion investment. Staff layoffs have begun. Player settlement offers have gone out at a fraction of guaranteed values.

The end has arrived for LIV Golf as it was conceived.

Multiple reports confirm that Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund — the sovereign wealth fund that bankrolled LIV Golf's creation in 2022 and funded its aggressive expansion for four seasons — is ending its investment in the circuit after 2026. LIV Golf is now preparing a US Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing that could come as early as next week, according to reports from Semafor, Newsmax, and other outlets citing people familiar with the situation.

The human cost is already visible. The majority of LIV Golf's 300-plus full-time staff have been laid off. Players who were promised guaranteed contracts worth tens or hundreds of millions of dollars are receiving settlement offers that sources describe as pennies on the dollar — a small fraction of what they were owed.

How LIV Got Here

LIV Golf launched in 2022 with a simple and audacious premise: use Saudi oil wealth to buy the best golfers in the world away from the PGA Tour, create a rival circuit with shorter 54-hole formats and shotgun starts, and build a new global golf brand from scratch.

The initial phase worked better than critics expected. LIV signed Phil Mickelson, Dustin Johnson, Bryson DeChambeau, Jon Rahm, Cameron Smith, and dozens of other established professionals to deals worth hundreds of millions collectively. The events ran. The circuits held 14 events per season. The players got paid — for a while.

But the structural challenges proved insurmountable. LIV never secured a major U.S. broadcast distribution deal that would have given it the mainstream audience needed to attract sponsorships and grow organically. Without advertising revenue and media rights money, the circuit depended entirely on PIF subsidies — a funding structure that was never meant to last indefinitely.

The golf public, meanwhile, largely stayed loyal to the PGA Tour. Narrative, majors, and the established order of professional golf proved more durable than anyone who bet against them anticipated.

What Happens to the Players

The bankruptcy implications for LIV's highest-profile players are significant and unresolved. Bryson DeChambeau, Jon Rahm, and Cameron Smith were three of the highest-paid players in the circuit's history, having signed deals that reportedly guaranteed tens of millions of dollars regardless of performance or event results.

The settlement offers going out — at a fraction of the remaining guaranteed amounts — force those players into an uncomfortable calculation: accept what they can get, or pursue legal remedies against a bankrupt entity in what would likely be an extended and expensive process with uncertain outcomes.

The PGA Tour, meanwhile, has maintained through CEO Brian Rolapp that there is currently no path back for LIV golfers through standard reinstatement. The Tour created a limited Returning Member Program based on "elite-performance criteria" in January 2026 that provided a route for specific players — but the program was narrow, and the question of whether a mass return of LIV players would be permitted remains unanswered.

The PIF's Calculus

The PIF reportedly invested between $5 billion and $8 billion into LIV Golf across its four-year run. Measured against its stated goal of using golf to advance Saudi Arabia's global sports brand and normalizing the kingdom's presence at international sporting events, the investment produced mixed results at best.

LIV did succeed in demonstrating that a rival golf league was possible and that many of the sport's top players could be recruited with sufficient financial incentive. But without a deal with the PGA Tour — negotiations that consumed much of 2023 and 2024 without producing an agreement — LIV was always going to need either a self-sustaining business model or indefinite sovereign wealth subsidy. Neither materialized.

What's Next

The bankruptcy filing, if it proceeds as reported, would mark the effective end of LIV Golf's current structure. A separate effort by CEO Scott O'Neil to create a scaled-down "LIV 2.0" funded by private equity remains ongoing. The immediate future for players like DeChambeau, Rahm, and Smith — their careers, their financial claims, and their path back to major championship golf — will be among the most closely watched stories in the sport over the coming months.

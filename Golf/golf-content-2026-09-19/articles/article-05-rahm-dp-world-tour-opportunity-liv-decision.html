Rahm Sees "Opportunity" on DP World Tour — His Oct. 13 Call Is Golf's Most Consequential

By Jake Torres | Golf Fanrecap | September 19, 2026

Jon Rahm / LIV Golf / DP World Tour

On September 15, Jon Rahm said publicly that he is "noncommittal" on LIV Golf 2.0 and that he sees "opportunity" on the DP World Tour. The words themselves are carefully measured. The context around them is not subtle.

Rahm is LIV Golf's largest individual creditor. The bankruptcy filings submitted by LIV on September 8 show that the tour owes him $7.47 million. Under the BC Partners restructuring agreement, the commitment of players representing at least two-thirds of total claim dollar amounts is required by October 13. No single player's decision carries more financial weight than Rahm's. His $7.47 million is the single largest individual claim in the player creditor pool.

What He Has Already Done

Rahm reached a separate agreement with the DP World Tour in May 2026. He paid his outstanding fines — accumulated when he missed required DP World Tour events to compete in LIV Golf tournaments in previous seasons — and committed to playing a required quota of DP World Tour events for the remainder of 2026. That agreement restored his full DP World Tour membership status.

The more significant consequence: it made him eligible to represent Europe at the 2027 Ryder Cup in Ireland. The Ryder Cup is the most high-profile team event in golf, and Rahm's desire to compete for Europe — he is Spanish, and the event has enormous personal meaning for him — has been publicly established for years. The DP World Tour agreement appears designed to keep that door open regardless of what happens with LIV Golf.

What the Language Signals

The phrase "I see opportunity" on the DP World Tour is not a committed statement. Rahm did not say he has made his decision. He did not say he is leaving LIV. But in the context of the October 13 deadline, his language is telling in what it declines to offer: affirmation of LIV 2.0, a positive framing of the restructuring plan, any suggestion that he sees the player-ownership model as something that draws him toward committing.

LIV has also petitioned the bankruptcy court for permission to reject its existing player contracts. If granted, the old deal is gone — and players would need to re-sign with the restructured tour voluntarily. That removes the question of contractual obligation and replaces it with a clean opt-in decision. For Rahm, whose contract included amounts still owed to him under the original terms, rejection of the contract means he retains his bankruptcy claim for the money owed, while his competitive future becomes entirely his choice.

Why His Decision Matters Beyond His Own Career

The BC Partners restructuring deal's success depends mathematically on the largest creditors. Rahm's $7.47 million, combined with the claims of Bryson DeChambeau and Dustin Johnson, totals approximately $18.7 million. In a pool where two-thirds by dollar amount must commit, the alignment or defection of that top cluster can move the threshold materially. If Rahm declines and encourages others to assess their situations independently, the dollar-amount threshold becomes significantly harder to satisfy.

Other players are watching what Rahm does. He has been, since his first season at LIV, one of the most prominent names associated with the league — a two-time major champion whose decision to join was one of the largest contracts LIV issued. His departure would be a visible signal about the restructured tour's viability. His commitment would be the opposite signal.

What's Next

October 13 is 24 days away. Rahm has not committed to LIV 2.0. His DP World Tour agreement is in place. His Ryder Cup eligibility is intact. His $7.47 million bankruptcy claim exists independent of whatever he decides. The language he used on September 15 — "opportunity" — points in a direction. Whether it becomes a formal decision before the deadline will be one of golf's defining news events of the fall.

One Ad, Three Walkouts — How Good Good Lost Callaway, the PGA Tour, and Golf Channel in Weeks

By Ryan Calloway | September 13, 2026

A controversial Good Good advertisement showing co-founder Garrett Clark knocking down a female golfer triggered one of the fastest partner collapses in golf's recent history — Callaway, Golf Galaxy, the PGA Tour, and Golf Channel all walked away within weeks.

Good Good had spent years building a golf content brand that crossed over from YouTube audiences into institutional partnerships. Callaway equipment deals. Golf Galaxy retail placement. A PGA Tour event sponsorship. A Golf Channel "Big Break" production deal. Those relationships represented the kind of legitimacy that content creators typically spend years working toward.

Then came the advertisement.

The Ad

The Good Good commercial at the center of the controversy featured co-founder Garrett Clark. In the ad, female golfer Alexis Miestowski touched a Callaway driver bearing Good Good co-branding. Clark responded by rushing toward Miestowski, knocking her down, and then standing over her with a warning.

The ad's intended tone — comedic, protective of equipment, in the register of Good Good's irreverent brand voice — did not land that way with viewers or partners. The image of a male content creator physically knocking down a female golfer in a branded commercial produced an immediate and sustained negative response.

CNN reported on the controversy on August 27, 2026. By that point, the partner decisions had already begun.

The Fallout

Callaway cut ties with Good Good. The equipment company's co-branding appeared on the driver in the advertisement itself — a detail that made the association impossible to separate from the controversy. Callaway's decision to end the partnership was announced without lengthy explanation. The advertisement was the explanation.

Golf Galaxy, which had carried Good Good-branded merchandise and content partnerships, also cut ties. The retail relationship ended alongside the equipment one.

The PGA Tour removed Good Good as the title sponsor of an event that had been scheduled for November 2026. The tour's decision reflected both the brand risk of maintaining an association under the circumstances and the speed at which the institutional golf world was moving to distance itself from the controversy.

Golf Channel's decision carried its own dimension of cost. The network canceled its fully filmed 2026 season of "Big Break" — a production that had already completed filming. The season existed. Golf Channel chose not to air it. That is a financial write-off and an editorial decision that underscores how severe the network viewed the reputational exposure.

What It Means

Good Good had achieved something that most golf content creators never reach: institutional credibility alongside creator appeal. The partnerships with Callaway, the PGA Tour, and Golf Channel were not small deals. They represented the brand's arrival as a recognized player in the golf ecosystem beyond YouTube.

The collapse of those partnerships — triggered by a single advertisement — is a case study in how quickly institutional relationships can dissolve when the reputational calculus changes. The organizations that walked away moved fast, and they moved together.

What's Next

Good Good has not publicly announced its response to the partner departures or the path forward for its brand and content operations. The creator economy in golf has not seen a fallout at this scale from a single piece of content before. Whether Good Good rebuilds its institutional relationships — or pivots to a model that doesn't depend on them — is a storyline that will play out over the coming months. The partners have spoken. The next chapter belongs to the brand.

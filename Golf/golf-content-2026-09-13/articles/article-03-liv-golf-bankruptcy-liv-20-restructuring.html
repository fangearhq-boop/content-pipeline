LIV Golf 2.0 — What the Bankruptcy Filing Means for the Players, the Structure, and the Future

By Marcus Cole | September 13, 2026

Five days after filing Chapter 11 in the United States, LIV Golf's restructuring plan is taking shape — but $45 million owed to players, terminated contracts, and a departed Saudi backer leave the league's future more complicated than the headline suggests.

On September 8, 2026, LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the United States. The filing documented more than $500 million in debt against assets estimated between $100 million and $500 million — the financial consequence of five years of aggressive spending, a $5 billion Saudi investment, and a business model that never achieved the sustainability its backers hoped for.

Five days later, the restructuring plan is taking shape. Whether it delivers is a different question.

The Backstory: Why Saudi Arabia Left

LIV Golf was created and sustained by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, which committed more than $5 billion to the project over three-plus years. In April 2026, the PIF announced it was pulling its funding at the end of the year.

The exit was tied to the geopolitical disruption caused by the war in Iran that unfolded earlier in 2026 — a conflict that scrambled energy prices and forced Saudi Arabia to reassess its external investment priorities. Golf was not exempt from that reassessment. What the PIF had built as a soft-power project became, in the context of the broader geopolitical moment, an expendable one.

Even in walking away, the PIF provided a $49.6 million debtor-in-possession loan to support LIV through the bankruptcy process. It is the final act of a backer cutting its losses cleanly while minimizing the damage to an asset it can no longer afford to carry.

LIV Golf 2.0: The Plan

The restructuring backed by BC Partners carries the working title "LIV Golf 2.0." The structural changes on the table include expanding the field from 57 to 75 players and — for the first time in LIV's history — introducing a 54-hole cut. Both changes are designed to make the league more competitive in format and more attractive to players beyond the original 48 who were contracted when the league ran its 2026 season.

The ownership model shifts to majority player-owned — a significant conceptual change from the Saudi sovereign-wealth-fund model that defined LIV's first years. Whether player ownership is meaningful governance or a marketing term will depend on how the restructuring is actually structured when the court process concludes.

The Player Situation

All 48 LIV player contracts were terminated when the bankruptcy was filed. The league's 2026 season ended on August 30, and those contracts are now among the unsecured claims before the bankruptcy court.

The court documents detail the player debts. Jon Rahm is owed $7.5 million. Bryson DeChambeau is owed $5.7 million. Dustin Johnson is owed $5.5 million. Cameron Smith is owed $4.8 million. Tyrrell Hatton is owed $3.4 million. Brooks Koepka — who had already returned to the PGA Tour through the Returning Member Program before the bankruptcy — is owed $1.7 million. Total player claims exceed $45 million.

These players are creditors, not partners, until the restructuring resolves their claims. What they are owed, and whether LIV 2.0 provides the path for them to recoup it, is an open question that a bankruptcy court will help answer.

What's Next

The 2026 LIV season — 12 events from February 5 through August 30 — is now a closed chapter. Jon Rahm, who led the individual standings, is already playing on the DP World Tour. The wave of player movement that will define the post-LIV landscape is just beginning. For LIV Golf itself, the restructuring timeline runs through the bankruptcy court. For the players, the next move depends on what the PGA Tour, the DP World Tour, and — eventually — LIV Golf 2.0 offer them. None of those answers have fully arrived yet.

LIV Golf's Collapse: Chapter 11, $500M in Debt, and an Uncertain Road to 2027

By Jake Torres | Golf Fanrecap | November 3, 2026

The Saudi-backed experiment that reshaped professional golf has ended in bankruptcy court.

LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on September 8, 2026, disclosing more than $500 million in debt and marking the end of the league's original incarnation. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, which had poured more than $5 billion into the venture over five years, made the decision not to continue its financial support beyond the 2026 season — a withdrawal that left the league with no viable path to survival in its current form.

How It Fell Apart

LIV Golf launched in 2022 with an audacious promise: to disrupt professional golf through guaranteed contracts, shotgun starts, no cuts, and more money than the PGA Tour had ever offered its players. Saudi Arabia's PIF was the engine — willing to absorb enormous losses in the short term in pursuit of sporting legitimacy and the long game of sports washing its international image.

For a time, the strategy appeared to work. LIV attracted major champions, built a television deal, and created genuine tension with the PGA Tour that seemed poised to reshape the sport's governance forever. A proposed merger framework between LIV and the PGA Tour in 2023 generated enormous coverage, only to collapse amid congressional scrutiny and internal disagreements.

But the economics never worked. LIV's broadcast deals brought in a fraction of what the PGA Tour earned. Sponsor integration lagged. Without PIF's backing, there was no pathway to profitability. When the Saudi fund finally pulled the plug, the outcome was inevitable.

What Bankruptcy Revealed

The Chapter 11 filing laid bare the scale of LIV's financial challenges. The league's more than $500 million in debt reflected years of guaranteed player contracts, operational costs, and the expense of staging events across multiple continents without the revenue base to support them.

The bankruptcy filing also voided player contracts — freeing the league's roster to make their own decisions about the future. Jon Rahm and Bryson DeChambeau were listed as the highest unsecured creditors, reflecting the scale of what LIV still owed its top players at the time of filing.

PIF agreed to provide $49.6 million in debtor-in-possession financing to keep the league operational through the restructuring process — a signal that Saudi Arabia had not entirely abandoned the golf investment, even if the format was going to change dramatically.

The Plan to Relaunch

LIV secured a restructuring deal with BC Partners, a European private equity firm, and announced plans to re-emerge in 2027 as a fundamentally different operation. The revamped league would feature a 75-player field — significantly smaller than the original — and would adopt a player-majority ownership structure designed to align incentives more directly with the athletes.

The team format, one of LIV's signature features, was reportedly scaled back or modified under the restructuring terms. The new league would operate on a smaller budget, with fewer events and a more focused competitive schedule.

The Rahm Problem

For LIV's restructuring plans, the announcement in early October 2026 that Jon Rahm had rejected the terms of LIV 2.0 was a severe blow. Rahm was the league's biggest active star — a two-time major champion who had given LIV credibility when he signed in late 2023. His departure, with approximately $100 million left on the table, signals that the restructured terms were not compelling even for the player with the most to gain from a successful relaunch.

Bryson DeChambeau's position remains publicly unclear, as do those of the dozens of other players whose contracts were voided by the bankruptcy. Each of those players now faces a genuine choice: sign with a restructured LIV under BC Partners, or pursue opportunities on the PGA Tour, the DP World Tour, or other circuits.

What This Means for Golf

LIV Golf's collapse ends the era of unlimited Saudi spending on professional golf as a competitive vehicle. Whatever comes next — whether LIV 2.0 launches in 2027 or not — the game will look different than it did in 2022 when LIV first announced its ambitions.

The PGA Tour emerges from the LIV era financially intact but structurally complicated by years of acrimony and the unresolved questions around the merger framework. The DP World Tour, which maintained its position as European golf's home circuit throughout the LIV disruption, may be the clearest beneficiary if players like Rahm return to its ranks.

The professional golf landscape in 2027 remains genuinely uncertain. But the LIV Golf experiment — as it was designed and executed — is over.

What's Next

LIV Golf's restructuring under BC Partners is expected to produce a formal 2027 schedule announcement in the coming months. The viability of that relaunch will depend heavily on how many marquee players choose to participate. Simultaneously, the PGA Tour and DP World Tour will be working to attract returning LIV players, potentially reshaping the competitive balance of world golf heading into 2027 and beyond.

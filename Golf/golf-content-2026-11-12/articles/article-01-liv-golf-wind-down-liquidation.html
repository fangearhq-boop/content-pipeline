LIV Golf Is Done: Five-Year Experiment Ends in Liquidation After Saudi Funding Collapses

By Ryan Calloway | November 12, 2026

LIV Golf has formally abandoned its restructuring plan and entered a wind-down toward full liquidation, with a formal plan filed November 10 and a January 7 court hearing that will finalize the dissolution of the Saudi-backed league after five turbulent years.

The LIV 2.0 Plan Is Dead

The announcement came at a November 5 bankruptcy hearing in New Jersey. A lawyer representing LIV Golf's debtors told the court that the league was "toggling to a value-maximizing wind-down" — legal language for giving up on the restructuring plan and moving toward dissolution. Five days later, on November 10, LIV Golf filed a formal liquidation plan. A disclosure statement hearing is set for November 24, and the final liquidation hearing is scheduled for January 7, 2027.

That is the end of a chapter in professional golf that began with enormous ambition and ends with creditors waiting in a New Jersey courtroom.

LIV Golf launched in 2022 with the full financial weight of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund behind it, signing marquee players with guaranteed contracts that the PGA Tour had never offered. Jon Rahm, Bryson DeChambeau, Dustin Johnson, Cameron Smith, Brooks Koepka, Phil Mickelson — the names on the LIV roster were among the most recognizable in world golf. The league held events across multiple continents, built a team format designed for new audiences, and forced professional golf into a legal and commercial conflict that produced more drama than most golf fans had experienced in a generation.

How the Money Ran Out

The timeline of LIV Golf's collapse tracks directly to Saudi Arabia's decision. In April 2026, the PIF withdrew its funding — a move that had been anticipated in some quarters as PIF recalibrated its global sports investment strategy, but which still hit the sport like a seismic event when it was confirmed.

Without the PIF's backing, LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in September 2026, listing assets of $100 million to $500 million against liabilities of $500 million to $1 billion. The filing revealed who was owed the most: Jon Rahm sat at the top of the creditor list with approximately $7.4 million in unsecured claims. DeChambeau, Dustin Johnson, and Cameron Smith were also among the largest creditors — a remarkable situation where the sport's biggest stars were simultaneously the game's largest unpaid debtors.

The LIV 2.0 plan, which envisioned an emerging league majority-owned by its players and backed by private equity firm BC Partners, required a critical mass of player participation. It never materialized. Without player commitment to the restructured model, the plan collapsed, and the lawyers went back to court.

What LIV Golf Changed

Whatever one thinks of LIV Golf's origins, the sport it leaves behind in 2026 is different from the one it found in 2022. PGA Tour players receive significantly higher prize money than they did before LIV arrived. The guaranteed contract model, once considered radical, has influenced how golf organizations approach player compensation. Global expansion, long a stated ambition of the PGA Tour, became an urgent priority once Saudi money was competing for the sport's talent.

These are not minor changes. They are structural shifts driven entirely by competitive pressure from a league that is now gone.

What's Next

The January 7 hearing will formalize the liquidation. After that, creditors — including the players owed tens of millions — will navigate the claims process. And the golf world will begin the complex, politically loaded process of figuring out where the LIV players land next. The PGA Tour has signaled it is not in a hurry to make that easy.

Five years. Billions of dollars. A permanent mark on professional golf. LIV Golf is done.

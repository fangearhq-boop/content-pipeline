LIV Golf Expected to File for Bankruptcy by September 7 — Players Offered Pennies on the Dollar

By Ryan Calloway | Golf Fanrecap | September 3, 2026

LIV Golf is preparing to file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection as soon as September 7 — four days from now — according to reporting from the Financial Times and multiple outlets. The league, launched in 2022 with more than $5 billion in Saudi Arabian investment, is reaching the end of that financial runway with no confirmed replacement deal in place.

The bankruptcy filing, if it proceeds on schedule, would arrive on the same weekend the 2026 Solheim Cup opens in the Netherlands. The juxtaposition is sharp: while women's golf celebrates its marquee team event, men's professional golf absorbs the most significant structural disruption in a generation.

The Sequence of Events

LIV's crisis has been building for months. In April 2026, Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund announced it would not extend its funding commitment beyond the 2026 season. The news triggered a chain of consequences: budget reductions, player contract renegotiations, and ultimately the mass layoff of the majority of LIV's 300-plus employees, which was confirmed in late August.

BC Partners Credit, a private capital firm, has been in discussions to provide a $250 million to $350 million debt facility that would allow LIV to restructure and continue as LIV 2.0. The transaction would be structured as senior secured debt — lending against LIV's assets including contracts, media rights, sponsorship receivables, and team equity interests. However, no definitive agreement has been signed as of September 3, and BC Partners' willingness to commit has reportedly been hampered by insufficient player commitments to the new structure.

A Chapter 11 filing is not a liquidation — it is a restructuring mechanism that allows an organization to continue operating while its debts and obligations are reorganized under court supervision. LIV's intended use of bankruptcy is to extend its lifespan, not end it.

What Players Are Facing

For the more than 50 players currently under LIV contract, the bankruptcy creates an immediate and difficult set of choices. Settlement offers have been sent to contracted players for their guaranteed future payments — and those offers, by multiple accounts, amount to a small fraction of the full contractual value.

Players face three broad options. They can accept the settlement offer and commit to playing in LIV 2.0, which would provide equity ownership in the new structure but reduced prize purses and a 10-event schedule split between five US and five international venues. They can accept the settlement and walk away, giving up the guaranteed money in exchange for freedom to seek reinstatement on the PGA Tour or DP World Tour. Or they can reject the settlement and pursue their full contractual claims through the bankruptcy process — a path that could take years to resolve and may yield less than the pennies-on-the-dollar settlements now being offered.

Jon Rahm, who won the LIV individual title for the third consecutive season at the Indianapolis finale, is among the highest-profile names navigating this decision. Bryson DeChambeau, who has publicly expected a new LIV contract, faces the same calculus.

What Comes Next

A Chapter 11 filing opens the formal bankruptcy process but does not immediately resolve LIV's future. If LIV 2.0 proceeds — player-owned, leaner, with BC Partners or another financier providing the capital bridge — it could emerge as a sustainable, smaller circuit within a year or two. If player commitments do not materialize, the restructuring could devolve into a more disorderly dissolution.

The PGA Tour's response to potential reinstatement requests from dozens of LIV players is the other unknown. The Tour's reinstatement policies have not been publicly updated since LIV's launch, and the prospect of absorbing a large cohort of returning players — some of whom had publicly antagonized the Tour during their LIV tenure — presents a complex administrative and political challenge for Commissioner Jay Monahan's office.

The deadline approaching September 7 is not the end of this story. It is, in many ways, the beginning of its most consequential chapter.

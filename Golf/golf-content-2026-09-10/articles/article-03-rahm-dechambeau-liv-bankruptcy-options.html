Jon Rahm and Bryson DeChambeau: The Fork in the Road After LIV Golf Chapter 11

By Marcus Cole | Golf Fanrecap | September 10, 2026

LIV Golf's Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing has terminated player contracts and left Rahm (~$7.4M unsecured) and DeChambeau (~$5.7M contingent) as creditors of the estate. Three paths exist. Each carries its own timeline and risk profile.

When Jon Rahm signed with LIV Golf in 2024 for a reported guarantee in the range of $150-300 million over the life of the deal, the structure of golf's emerging alternative ecosystem made that number plausible. Two years later, with LIV Golf now a Chapter 11 debtor in New Jersey federal court, the number that matters is different: approximately $7.4 million unsecured.

That is what the bankruptcy filing lists for Rahm in the creditor register — a fraction of what he was owed, representing the portion of his outstanding guaranteed money that the estate has classified as a general unsecured claim. Bryson DeChambeau appears as approximately $5.7 million contingent. Those designations matter because they determine what comes next for both players.

Option 1: LIV 2.0 with BC Partners

The most discussed path for Rahm, DeChambeau, and the other 46 players whose contracts were voided by the filing is participation in LIV 2.0 — the restructured entity that BC Partners, a London-based private equity firm, is positioning to fund with $250-350 million.

The LIV 2.0 offer converts outstanding guaranteed money owed to players into equity stakes in the new league. Players who sign on would forgo the cash they are owed as creditors in exchange for ownership interest in an organization that BC Partners is building around a 10-event schedule beginning in 2027. Announced venues include Saudi Arabia, Australia, South Africa, and Hong Kong.

The argument for LIV 2.0 is certainty: equity in a funded entity beats an unsecured creditor claim against an insolvent one. The argument against it is that the equity stake may be worth less than whatever partial recovery the bankruptcy court ultimately distributes, and it requires continued commitment to an organization with no proven track record as an investment vehicle.

Option 2: Court Claims

Players who decline LIV 2.0 retain their rights as creditors of the LIV Golf estate. The bankruptcy court process will eventually determine what percentage of unsecured claims can be satisfied by the estate's available assets. In practice, unsecured creditors in Chapter 11 restructurings involving insolvent organizations typically recover a small percentage of face value — or nothing — after secured creditors and administrative claims are paid.

Rahm's $7.4 million unsecured listing suggests the estate views that portion of his claim as recoverable from general assets. His full contract claim may be larger; the creditor filing reflects what LIV Golf owes under specific contractual terms rather than the total theoretical value of the original deal. DeChambeau's $5.7 million contingent designation adds uncertainty — contingent claims may or may not be allowed by the court depending on whether the underlying triggering conditions are met.

Legal experts familiar with similar restructuring proceedings advise that the expected value of an unsecured claim against an insolvent entity, discounted for time and probability, rarely exceeds a negotiated settlement. The bankruptcy process could take years. Players who choose this path may not see resolution — or recovery — until 2028 or later.

Option 3: Tour Returns

PGA Tour CEO Brian Rolapp stated in early September that there is "no current path back" for LIV players, naming Rahm, DeChambeau, and Cameron Smith specifically. That statement signals the Tour's posture going into the bankruptcy period: the filing does not automatically trigger a general reinstatement window.

Individual applications remain theoretically available. The PGA Tour has procedures for reinstatement that were in place before LIV Golf existed. Whether those procedures would be applied to former LIV players at scale — or whether the Tour would create specific terms for high-profile returns — depends on how the Tour's membership and policy board choose to respond to the bankruptcy.

For Rahm specifically, the DP World Tour represents a third-country alternative. As a Spanish player, Rahm has European Tour membership history and would be eligible to pursue a return to the DP World Tour schedule without necessarily triggering the PGA Tour reinstatement question. The DP World Tour and PGA Tour maintain a partnership arrangement, which creates its own complications, but the European pathway represents an option that Rahm's nationality makes available to him in a way it isn't for American players like DeChambeau.

The Clock

LIV 2.0 player decisions are not being made with unlimited time. The DIP financing from PIF covers operational costs during the restructuring period, but BC Partners needs player commitments to validate the LIV 2.0 investment case. A new league without a critical mass of elite players is not the same investment as one with Rahm, DeChambeau, and the field LIV built over four years.

The timeline for formal settlement offers — the point at which players receive specific equity terms and deadlines — is likely weeks away, pending court approval of the DIP financing and BC Partners' formal agreement. Players who are evaluating Tour return options will need to make simultaneous inquiries about reinstatement eligibility, since the answer from the PGA Tour or DP World Tour affects which paths are actually open.

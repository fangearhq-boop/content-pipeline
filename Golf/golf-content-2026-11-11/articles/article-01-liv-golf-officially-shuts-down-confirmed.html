LIV Golf Is Done: Lawyers Confirm Shutdown, No Successor League Planned

By Ryan Calloway | November 11, 2026

LIV Golf's lawyers confirmed in court that the Saudi-backed breakaway league will shut down with no successor organization planned. After five years and approximately $5 billion in investment from Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, LIV Golf is entering liquidation — bringing to a close one of the most disruptive chapters in professional golf history.

The End of LIV Golf

What began as one of the most ambitious challenges to the established order in professional sports is ending in liquidation. LIV Golf's lawyers appeared in court and confirmed what had been building for months: the league will shut down, and there will be no LIV Golf 2.0 to replace it.

The collapse came after BC Partners Credit withdrew its offer to fund a revived circuit. The investment firm had been in discussions about providing the financial backing needed to continue LIV's operations, but those talks ended when LIV could not persuade enough high-profile players to commit to a continuation of the league. Without the talent pool that made the original LIV compelling, the investment case evaporated.

LIV Golf filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in September 2026 after Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund decided not to fund the league beyond the 2026 season. The bankruptcy filing bought time, but not a resolution. With no other funding partner and no player commitments sufficient to build a new venture, the conclusion was inevitable.

Five Years and $5 Billion Later

The numbers associated with LIV Golf are extraordinary. The PIF's investment across five years reached approximately $5 billion — a figure that would have seemed unfathomable to professional golf observers before the league launched. That money flowed into player signing bonuses, guaranteed contracts, event production costs, marketing, and the infrastructure required to stage a global golf circuit.

At its peak, LIV attracted dozens of tour-exempt players, including several former major champions and world-ranked stars. The guaranteed money was real, the events were professionally staged, and the team format offered a genuine alternative to the individual-focused structure of the PGA and DP World tours.

But the financial model never resolved its fundamental challenge: generating revenue that could justify the investment. Media rights deals fell short of projections. Sponsorship revenue never matched the league's ambitions. And with no major championship connection, LIV events occupied a space that proved difficult to monetize at the level required to sustain the operation.

What Happens to the Players

For the players who committed to LIV, the shutdown creates an immediate problem: where do they compete in 2027? The situation varies considerably depending on each player's history and eligibility.

Some players, like Patrick Reed and Eugenio Chacarra, have already secured their path back through performance on the DP World Tour. Others face longer roads. The PGA Tour's Returning Member Program — the original pathway for LIV defectors to return — has been closed, and PGA Tour CEO Brian Rolapp has stated it will not be revived. Players who want to return to the PGA Tour will need to earn their way back through tour qualifying or alternative circuits.

What's Next

As LIV Golf's remaining assets — real estate, trademarks, and infrastructure — move through the liquidation process, the sport begins the work of absorbing several dozen newly available professional golfers. Some will find their way back to the established tours. Others may face retirement or diminished careers on lower-tier circuits.

For professional golf broadly, the LIV era is ending with less resolution than its most dramatic moments promised. The sport did not split permanently. The established tours did not capitulate. And Saudi investment, while enormous, did not purchase the influence over the sport's governance that it originally sought. What it did purchase — and what will outlast LIV itself — is a changed landscape of player compensation, tour economics, and power dynamics that every stakeholder in the game is still navigating.

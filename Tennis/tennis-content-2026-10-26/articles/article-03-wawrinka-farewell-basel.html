Wawrinka Takes the Court One Last Time — A Farewell Night in Basel

By Ryan Calloway | Tennis Fanrecap | October 26, 2026

Monday night at the Swiss Indoors in Basel, Stan Wawrinka walks out for his final ATP match. He is 41, a three-time Grand Slam champion, and one of the defining players of his generation. The venue is fitting: Basel is where he won the Swiss Indoors three times, where Swiss tennis crowds have always held him in a particular regard, and where this kind of ending makes sense.

A Career Built on Defying Expectations

Wawrinka was born on March 28, 1985, and turned professional in 2002. For much of the early part of his career, he competed in the long shadow cast by Roger Federer. It was a shadow many Swiss players before him had struggled to escape. Wawrinka did not escape it so much as he carved out his own space beside it.

He reached a career-high ranking of No. 3 in the world in 2014 — the same year he won his first Grand Slam title, defeating Rafael Nadal in the Australian Open final. It was the kind of performance that rewrote what was thought possible for him. He was 28 and had been a respected professional for years, but suddenly the sport saw something different in him.

The Roland Garros title followed in 2015, and the US Open crown came in 2016, where he dismantled Novak Djokovic in a performance many consider among the finest in a Grand Slam final in the modern era. Three Slam titles in three years, three different surfaces. It is a record that belongs to a very short list of players.

Basel: Three Titles, One Final Night

The Swiss Indoors is not just a convenient farewell location — it is one of the tournaments most deeply woven into Wawrinka's story. He won his first title there in 2006, early in his professional career, when the event was already defined by Federer's dominance. He returned to win it again in 2015 and 2016, by which point he was among the two or three best players in the world.

Three titles at one event across a span of ten years tells you something about the consistency of his indoor game and his attachment to the tournament. Basel gave him early-career credibility, midcareer validation, and now it gives him a proper stage for goodbye.

Monday night is his tribute night. He faces Jiří Lehečká in the first round. The result, whatever it is, will matter less than the reception and the atmosphere surrounding it.

The Gold, the Backhand, the Era

Any accounting of Wawrinka's career requires mention of the 2008 Beijing Olympics, where he and Roger Federer won doubles gold together — Switzerland's first Olympic tennis medal in the open era. It remains one of the more memorable moments in Swiss sporting history.

Then there is the backhand. Wawrinka's single-handed backhand was, at its peak, one of the most dangerous shots in the sport — capable of winning points from positions that would have ended the rally for nearly anyone else. It defined his style as much as any tactical preference, and it made him genuinely watchable even in matches he was losing.

He steps away as one of the last active players from the generation shaped by Federer, Nadal, and Djokovic — the generation that also gave tennis Tsonga, Gasquet, Berdych, and Murray. Most of them are already gone. Wawrinka is among the last.

What's Next

After Monday night, Wawrinka's professional career is over. He leaves with three Grand Slam titles, an Olympic gold medal, a career-high ranking of No. 3, and a record at Basel that most players would consider a career highlight on its own. His retirement brings the curtain down on one of the more richly textured careers of the last two decades. The Swiss Indoors crowd will send him off the way Basel has always treated him — as one of their own.

Alexander Zverev's Historical Legacy: A Second German Champion, 37 Years in the Making

By Marcus Cole

September 15, 2026

When the final point of the 2026 US Open men's final went past Ben Shelton, Alexander Zverev did not immediately celebrate. He stood at the baseline, uncertain whether the ball had been called out. It was his older brother Mischa, sitting courtside, who made him realize the truth: the match was over. Zverev had won the US Open.

What followed was a moment of pure joy — arms spread, a roar directed at the Arthur Ashe Stadium crowd, and tears that had been building for years. The 29-year-old German had just made history, and he was only beginning to understand it.

The Second German Champion

With his 6-3, 7-6(2), 5-7, 6-2 victory over Shelton, Zverev became just the second German man to win the US Open. The first was Boris Becker, who triumphed at Flushing Meadows in 1989 — the year Zverev was born. Thirty-seven years separated the two German champions, a gap that spanned multiple tennis generations and outlasted the careers of dozens of would-be major winners.

In his victory speech, Zverev acknowledged the significance. "We went 30 years without winning a Grand Slam, and now we've won two within a span of three months," he told the crowd, referring to himself and his country. His first Grand Slam had come in June, when he defeated Carlos Alcaraz at Roland Garros. His second arrived on the hard courts of New York on a warm Sunday in September.

The Fourth Man in the Open Era

The Roland Garros and US Open double places Zverev in a remarkably exclusive company. He is the fourth player in the Open Era to win his first two Grand Slam titles in the same calendar year — a distinction that speaks to the sustained excellence required to win multiple majors while the world's best players are simultaneously peaking.

The feat is made more impressive by the fact that Roland Garros and the US Open are played on different surfaces — clay and hard court, respectively — requiring Zverev to demonstrate versatility as well as peak performance.

A Season for the Record Books

The numbers from Zverev's 2026 season are extraordinary. His match record stands at 51-13. He won Roland Garros in Paris and the US Open in New York. He collected $5.5 million in prize money for the US Open title alone. He leads the PIF ATP Live Race to Turin, the year-long points race that determines who qualifies for the season-ending ATP Finals.

None of this, Zverev has said in interviews, was expected in the way it happened. He had been to Grand Slam finals before — most notably a painful loss at the 2020 US Open, when he blew a two-set lead against Dominic Thiem and collapsed in five sets. That defeat haunted him for years. This title, on the same court six years later, carried a weight of redemption that money and rankings cannot fully capture.

What's Next

Zverev will take a brief rest before returning to action on the Asian swing. He leads the Race to Turin but world No. 1 Jannik Sinner — just 1,810 points behind after the US Open — will be gunning for the year-end No. 1 ranking. With Shanghai, Paris-Bercy, and the ATP Finals still to come, the 2026 season has not finished writing its story. Zverev, now firmly among the legends of his generation, intends to write as much of it as possible.

Arozarena's $100M+ Ask Is Stalling His Market — With 11 Days Until the Lockout

By Ryan Calloway

November 20, 2026

Randy Arozarena declined the qualifying offer earlier this month and hit free agency with a simple ask: pay him more than $100 million. Per ESPN's Jeff Passan, suitors are balking. The Seattle Mariners — the team that employed him, the team most fans expected to retain him — have reportedly decided they will not offer a long-term deal. With 11 days until the December 1 CBA expiration and a lockout expected December 2, Arozarena's market has a problem.

What Arozarena Brings

The case for Randy Arozarena at a significant contract is straightforward. He is a switch-hitting outfielder who posted 23 home runs and 41 stolen bases in 2026 — a combination of power and speed that only a handful of players in the sport deliver. He plays capable defense in left field. He has a postseason track record that includes one of the most memorable World Series performances in recent memory (2020, Rays) and consistent production in October.

At 29, he is entering what should be his peak earning years. The qualifying offer he declined was worth $23.15 million — essentially the market's floor offer to its own best free agents. Arozarena presumably felt that offer undervalued him, and four or five years at or above that AAV would put him in the $100-to-$120 million range that sources indicate he has been seeking.

Why the Market Is Hesitating

Several factors are creating friction in Arozarena's negotiations. First, his 2026 slash line (.246/.328/.407) was down from his peak — lower batting average, lower OPS, and a walk rate that suggests some regression. That decline, even a modest one, makes teams more cautious about committing to a four-or-five-year deal that runs through his early thirties.

Second, the qualifying offer compensation attached to signing Arozarena means the team that lands him loses a draft pick. For teams that value their pipeline, that pick cost adds friction to an already-expensive commitment. In a market where every dollar has to compete with other needs, the QO penalty is not trivial.

Third, the lockout timeline is compressing every negotiation. Teams that are uncertain about Arozarena's value at $100 million-plus may prefer to let the market reset after the CBA is resolved rather than overpay under artificial time pressure. That instinct — to wait — is exactly what Arozarena's side cannot afford to have take hold.

The Mariners' Exit

Seattle's decision to pass on a long-term Arozarena deal changes the landscape significantly. The Mariners were widely expected to retain their corner outfielder, who had become one of the team's most popular players and a genuine star in a city that follows baseball closely. Without Seattle as a bidder, the pool of teams willing to meet Arozarena's ask becomes smaller and potentially less competitive.

Teams with outfield needs and payroll flexibility — the Cubs, Blue Jays (if they miss on Suzuki), Orioles — are the logical targets for Arozarena's representation to work. But none of those teams have made a public push at his asking price, and the QO compensation complicates fits for clubs that prize their draft position.

What's Next

Eleven days is a short window for a negotiation that requires one side to move significantly on price. If Arozarena's ask stays above $100 million and the market has already determined it won't go there, something has to give — and in almost every historical precedent, it's the player's ask that adjusts. If Arozarena's representation brings the number down to the $80-to-$90 million range, a deal likely gets done before December 1. If they hold firm, Arozarena may be one of the most prominent free agents frozen by the lockout — a situation that is uncomfortable for everyone involved and resolves only when a new CBA is ratified.

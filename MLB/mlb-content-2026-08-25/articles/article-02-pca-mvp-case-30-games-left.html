PCA's MVP Case — Cold Streak, Cubs 76-56, and 30 Games That Decide Everything

By Jake Torres | Ballpark Banter | August 25, 2026

The most interesting subplot in the NL MVP race right now is the gap between what the numbers say and what the narrative says. The numbers say Pete Crow-Armstrong is the best player in the National League. The narrative says he's in a cold streak and falling behind Shohei Ohtani. Both things are true, which makes the next thirty games fascinating to watch.

The Cubs are 76-56. They've been winning while their MVP candidate worked through an approximately 4-for-36 (.111) slump over roughly eight games. Winning and a player slump coexisting on the same roster is either a testament to team depth or a complication in the MVP narrative — depending on what story you want to tell.

The Case That Doesn't Disappear in a Slump

PCA's season line entering the stretch run: .281 batting average, 30 home runs, 78 RBI, 31 stolen bases, 7.7 fWAR leading all of baseball, 22 Outs Above Average, 24 fielding run value. He is the first Cubs player in franchise history to post back-to-back 30-30 seasons.

The slump affects the batting average number. It doesn't affect the fWAR. It doesn't change the 22 OAA. It doesn't erase the stolen bases. And it doesn't alter the fact that PCA's total contribution — offense, defense, and baserunning combined into a single value — is the highest of any position player in the National League this year.

That's the argument that holds when the batting average dips. The slump makes it a harder argument to have in public. The voters who prioritize counting stats will focus on the OPS gap between PCA (.906) and Ohtani (.928) and make the case for the Dodgers' two-way star. That's a legitimate argument. It's also an argument that existed before the cold streak, which means the eight-game slump did not create the Ohtani challenge — it amplified it.

Ohtani's Position

Ohtani at .928 OPS is posting one of the most dominant offensive seasons in baseball. For a player who cannot pitch this year (presumably managing his arm toward the postseason), his bat alone is MVP-caliber. The argument for Ohtani in November is simple: the Dodgers are a championship contender, his offensive numbers are elite, and voters historically reward players whose teams succeed in October.

PCA's counter-argument is the one that requires voter education: fWAR, OAA, and total player value are the metrics that capture what he does at a level Ohtani's pure offensive stats don't reach. A voter who believes the award should go to the most valuable player in the broadest sense has a straightforward case for PCA. A voter who runs the traditional counting-stats comparison has a case for Ohtani.

That conversation will happen in October and November. The next thirty games will determine which side has more statistical ammunition.

What "Bounce Back" Looks Like

The slump was real. What matters now is whether PCA's September resembles the player who posted 30 home runs and 31 steals through August or the player who went 4-for-36 in a small sample. Hot hitters go cold. Cold hitters get hot. The question is timing — and in an award race that ends with the regular season, timing is everything.

The Cubs need PCA to be at his best in September. They're chasing the No. 1 Wild Card seed specifically to avoid facing the Brewers and Misiorowski in the first round. The team's October prospects and PCA's MVP campaign are running on parallel tracks for the next month.

What's Next

Thirty games remain. The Cubs are 76-56, holding the No. 1 NL Wild Card seed. Ohtani and the Dodgers continue to press the MVP case from the other side. PCA's performance in September — whether the cold streak ends or extends — is the chapter that closes the regular-season argument before November's vote.

Joshua Baez Is Back — And the Cardinals Still Don't Know Where to Bat Him

By Jake Torres | Ballpark Banter | August 21, 2026

Joshua Baez is back in the Cardinals lineup after Thursday's day off, and the deployment question that has surrounded him since his arrival continues to define one of the most compelling stories in the National League's stretch run. Where Oliver Marmol bats him — and how he uses him — could matter considerably over the final 35 games.

The question is not simple. The numbers Baez has put up since his August 15 debut suggest a player capable of devastating pitchers when the conditions are right. But those same numbers also tell a complicated story about where in a lineup he belongs.

What the Debut Did — and Didn't — Tell Us

Baez's Major League debut on August 15 against the Cubs was one of the most remarkable offensive performances in recent memory. He homered in his first three career at-bats — an MLB first — including a 449-foot rocket on his very first swing. He went 3-for-4 with five RBI. The entire Cardinals Stadium was buzzing by the second inning.

But debuts — even historic ones — are data points, not verdicts. Pitchers had not seen Baez. He was operating entirely on instinct against a league that had no book on him. That advantage disappears quickly. The adjustment period has already begun.

His current line of .251 batting average, .332 on-base percentage, and .552 slugging percentage tells a more nuanced story. The slugging is elite — the kind of number that justifies any role in the middle of the lineup. The strikeout rate (32.7%) and the batting average both push back against a leadoff deployment where consistency matters more than power.

The Leadoff Experiment and What It Revealed

Marmol moved Baez to leadoff on August 17 with JJ Wetherholt resting. The decision was partly circumstantial — Wetherholt needed the day, and Marmol needed a solution at the top of the lineup — but it also reflected genuine curiosity about whether Baez's athleticism and baseball IQ could translate to the on-base demands of the leadoff role.

The answer so far is inconclusive, which is perhaps the most interesting outcome. Baez was not a disaster at leadoff. He was not revelatory, either. He showed flashes of the plate patience that a leadoff hitter needs, while also swinging at pitches that a prototypical leadoff man lets pass. At 21 years old, with weeks of major league experience, that is not a damning assessment. It is, in fact, a reasonable one.

The fuller picture: Baez has the tools to be a leadoff hitter eventually. His speed and athleticism give him a base-stealing dimension that changes how pitchers attack him. His on-base percentage, at .332, is functional but not standout. For a long-term leadoff solution, that number needs to be closer to .370. He is not there yet.

The Middle-Order Argument

Put Baez in the cleanup or number-five slot and a different player emerges. The .552 slugging percentage is a genuine threat to any pitcher who leaves a pitch in the zone. With 34 Triple-A home runs before his call-up and 12 MLB home runs in early innings, the power is not a fluke — it is who this player is.

The Cardinals have runners on base more often when RBI opportunities are created higher in the lineup. A middle-order Baez gets those opportunities. He sees more sliders in the dirt, more fastballs on the hands, more of the pitching sequences that come with being feared rather than managed. That is the version of Baez that most scouts anticipated.

Marmol's challenge is managing the now — a team in a playoff chase — against the opportunity to develop Baez into his long-term role. Getting it right in August matters for October.

What's Next

Baez is in the lineup and the Cardinals have 35 games to make the deployment work. Marmol will likely settle on a primary role within the next week — the pennant race does not leave time for extended experimentation. Watch where Baez bats over the next three series for the answer to the question everyone in St. Louis is asking.

The White Sox Rotation Is Young, Unproven, and Playing October Baseball

By Jake Torres | October 5, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

The Chicago White Sox lost 121 games in 2024. They lost more than 100 games in both 2023 and 2025. They are the first team in Major League Baseball history to reach the postseason after three consecutive 100-loss seasons. They are playing the Cleveland Guardians in the American League Division Series right now. And the reason any of this is possible is a starting rotation built almost entirely from players who are under 25 years old.

Hagen Smith and the Youth Core

Hagen Smith started ALDS Game 1 for Chicago. He is 23 years old. It was not a showcase start meant to let a young arm feel what October is like from the dugout. He was given the ball for one of the most important games of the season because the organization believes he is ready for it.

Smith is not alone. Noah Schultz, Grant Taylor, and Sean Burke are all active in the rotation conversation. These are not names that have been household staples for years. They are young arms coming up through a system that was rebuilt from the ground while the losses piled up, and they are now being asked to deliver when it actually matters.

The result so far has been competitive baseball. Chicago won Game 1. They are in a best-of-five series with a chance to go up 2-0 today.

The Veterans Return

The youth movement does not exist in isolation. Luis Castillo, the White Sox ace, is scheduled to return in 2027 after missing this season. Castillo is a front-line starter — the kind of pitcher who, when healthy, gives a rotation a different identity entirely. His return will layer genuine top-of-the-rotation talent onto a group of young arms that, at this exact moment in October, are proving they can hold their own without him.

Edwar Fedde and Garrett Newcomb are both pending free agents at the end of this season. Their futures in Chicago are uncertain. What the rotation looks like next April will depend in part on whether the organization retains them and in part on how Smith, Schultz, Taylor, and Burke develop over the winter. The direction of that development will be shaped heavily by how they perform between now and whenever this postseason ends.

What the History Means

Three straight 100-loss seasons should not produce a playoff team. In most rebuilds, the gap between that kind of losing and competitive October baseball spans five or six years. Chicago has done it in three. The youth rotation is both the cause and the proof — these arms were drafted, developed, and deployed in a compressed timeline, and they have delivered a postseason berth ahead of schedule.

The history of it — first team ever to reach the playoffs after three straight 100-loss campaigns — will get attention no matter what happens in this ALDS. The rotation that made it possible deserves its own accounting.

What's Next

The White Sox host Game 2 today against Cleveland. A win would put Chicago up 2-0 and give the organization its most significant postseason moment in years. Smith, Schultz, Taylor, and Burke will all be part of whatever comes next. Castillo will be back next year. The window is opening for a White Sox team that has spent three painful years building it. October is the first real test of whether it holds.

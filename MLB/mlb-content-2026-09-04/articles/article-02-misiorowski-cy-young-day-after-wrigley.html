The Day After Wrigley: Jakub Misiorowski's Cy Young Case Has Never Been Stronger

By Jake Torres | Ballpark Banter | September 4, 2026

The morning after is when the baseball world catches up. On September 2, Jakub Misiorowski went to Wrigley Field and did what he has done all season — dominated. Scoreless innings. Eight strikeouts. A peak velocity of 101.5 miles per hour on a night when questions about his arm health had been building for weeks. The doubts didn't survive the evening. What's left is the clearest Cy Young case in either league.

The numbers that Misiorowski has put together in 2026 are not the product of soft competition or a friendly schedule. They are historically elite: 14-5 record, 1.73 ERA, 222 strikeouts, 0.75 WHIP. The Milwaukee Brewers are 87-53 and lead the NL Central. His Cy Young odds sit at -1400. In a race with a legitimate contender at -1400, there is no race. There is a frontrunner and then everyone else.

What the Wrigley Start Answered

The velocity narrative mattered because it introduced doubt. Misiorowski's average fastball velocity had dipped from 100.5 mph to 98.7 mph through parts of late August — not a dramatic decline, but noticeable for a pitcher whose calling card is elite heat. Any dip in velocity for a power pitcher raises questions about arm fatigue, mechanical issues, or something deeper.

On September 2, those questions were answered with a number: 101.5 mph. That's a peak velocity that would rank among the highest in baseball on any given night. Coming off a stretch where his average had dipped, throwing 101.5 mph peak at Wrigley in an 8-strikeout scoreless outing wasn't just impressive — it was clarifying. The velocity is there. The stuff is elite. The concerns were temporary.

For Cy Young voters who track health and durability alongside statistics, that start was the equivalent of a closing argument. You wanted to know if he could hold up for the stretch run? Watch September 2. You wanted to know if the velocity dip was the beginning of something? It wasn't.

The Statistical Case

A 1.73 ERA across a full season is the kind of number that gets compared to historical comps. For context, an ERA under 2.00 across a full starter workload is something that happens once every few years across both leagues. Misiorowski is doing it in 2026 while pitching for a team in first place, which eliminates the argument that his numbers are inflated by pitching behind a dominant offense.

The 0.75 WHIP is equally elite. WHIP below 1.00 across a full season is historically significant — it means Misiorowski is allowing fewer than one baserunner per inning. Combine that with 222 strikeouts and you have a pitcher who not only misses bats but limits traffic entirely. His starts are not high-leverage, high-stress outings where he escapes trouble. They are methodical shutdowns where trouble rarely materializes.

The win-loss record at 14-5 matters less as an individual metric in an era where run support determines wins as much as pitcher performance, but it also reflects that Misiorowski has been the ace of a first-place team when it counts. He hasn't lost starts at the worst times. The Brewers have won games when he pitches.

What Comes Next

Misiorowski's next start projects for September 7 or 8. That gives him a handful of appearances remaining before the regular season ends, and each one will receive national attention now that the Wrigley performance has reset the conversation. There is no scenario in which his Cy Young is in serious jeopardy barring injury or a catastrophic collapse — but the baseball world will be watching anyway, because watching him pitch is worth the attention regardless of what it means for the award.

The Brewers need him for the stretch run and for October. Their NL Central lead is comfortable but not sealed. Each Misiorowski start is a chance to lock down another series and add another line to an already historic season.

What's Next

The Cy Young Award will be announced after the season. But the conversation is already over. Misiorowski went to Wrigley and closed the case himself, one 101.5 mph fastball at a time. The voters will confirm what September 2 made obvious: the best pitcher in the National League wears a Brewers uniform, and it's not particularly close.

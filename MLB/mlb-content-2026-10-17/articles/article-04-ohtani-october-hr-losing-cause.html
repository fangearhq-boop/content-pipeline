Ohtani's October Ends With One Homer in Four Games — and a Lot of Questions for LA

By Jake Torres | October 17, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

Shohei Ohtani finally hit a postseason home run Friday night. He had not done it in the first three games of the NLCS. He had not done it since August 18, a regular-season game in Colorado, the last time he had found the seats before October arrived. And when he finally did it — a 2-run shot in the second inning of NLCS Game 4 that put the Dodgers up 4-0 — it came in the same game that Milwaukee completed its sweep of Los Angeles.

The Dodgers lost 6-4. The Brewers are in the World Series. Ohtani's home run, the one everyone had been waiting for, was the kind of moment that almost makes the drought worse in retrospect.

The October That Wasn't

Ohtani's NLCS statistics are a study in postseason difficulty. One home run. A .150 batting average. An OPS of .411. These numbers, from one of the most productive offensive players in the sport during the regular season, tell the story of an October spent managing physical limitations and facing a Milwaukee pitching staff that executed its game plan better than anyone expected.

Throughout the NLCS, Ohtani's camp acknowledged he was managing discomfort in his knee and biceps — ailments that accumulated over a long, productive regular season. Playing through postseason discomfort is not unusual, and Ohtani has never been one to point to health as an excuse. But the numbers suggest the October version of Ohtani was a diminished version of the player who had dominated the regular season.

The Brewers' approach to Ohtani was methodical. They attacked him differently depending on the count, they moved the ball around the zone, and they took away the pitches he had driven all season. When he finally found one to attack in Game 4, he hit it 400 feet. It put his team up four runs. It was not enough.

The End of the Streak

Los Angeles made the postseason for 14 consecutive years, from 2013 through 2026. That is a dynasty by any reasonable definition — multiple World Series appearances, multiple championships, and a sustained level of organizational excellence that few franchises in baseball history have matched.

The 2026 season ends in a sweep. The Brewers, a franchise that had not been to the World Series since 1982, sent the Dodgers home in four games. That is a complicated result for an organization that has invested billions in building what felt like a perennial championship contender.

The offseason questions for Los Angeles will be significant. How do you evaluate a season in which Ohtani had a historically good regular season but a quiet October? How do you address the physical concerns that were clearly real? How do you rebuild the roster around the pieces that remain? Those answers will start to emerge in November.

What's Next

For Ohtani, the winter will bring rest, recovery, and presumably a return to full health before spring training. For the Dodgers, the offseason will bring roster decisions and an honest accounting of what went wrong in October.

The 14-year streak is over. One home run in four games is not the October Ohtani or his team was expecting. The Brewers played a part in making it end that way, and that is what baseball does — it gives you the moment you've been waiting for, and then it puts you on a plane home. Shohei Ohtani will be back. The 2026 NLCS story was not his.

8 Days Until the MLB Lockout: Why There Is No Deal Coming

By Marcus Cole | Ballpark Banter | November 23, 2026

The MLB collective bargaining agreement expires at 11:59 PM ET on December 1 — eight days from today. A lockout is expected to begin at 12:01 AM on December 2. All transactions freeze: no signings, no trades, no waiver claims, no arbitration hearings. Baseball stops.

No one who covers this sport seriously expects a deal before December 1. The MLBPA's executive leadership has said publicly that a lockout is "all but guaranteed." Commissioner Rob Manfred has made clear the owners will impose one when the agreement expires. Those are not positions that leave room for a last-minute handshake.

The gap isn't procedural. It's structural. This fight is about whether Major League Baseball will have a salary cap.

What Owners Want

The owners' proposal includes a salary cap of $258.2 million and a salary floor of $180.2 million, to be implemented in the 2028 season. The cap-and-floor model is not unusual in American professional sports — the NFL, NBA, and NHL all operate under versions of it. Owners argue it creates competitive balance and prevents the kind of spending arms race that has defined the top of the market in recent years.

The timing of that argument is notable. It comes in the same week that the New York Mets agreed to pay one pitcher $450 million over nine years. Steve Cohen's ability to spend at that level, while teams in smaller markets operate on a fraction of that budget, is exactly the dynamic owners cite when they push for a cap.

What Players Won't Accept

The MLBPA's position has never wavered: no salary cap in any form. The union views a cap as a ceiling on earning potential for the best players in the sport, and they have rejected it in every collective bargaining negotiation in baseball history. That consistency is not an accident — it reflects a fundamental disagreement about how baseball's economics should work.

The players point to the revenue baseball generates, the relatively short careers most players have, and the pre-arbitration system that suppresses salaries in a player's first few years. Their argument is that a cap would permanently lock in a structure that undervalues players at the precise moment when revenues and franchise valuations are at an all-time high.

These are not positions that meet in the middle. That's why no one expects a deal.

The 2021 Precedent

The last MLB work stoppage began December 2, 2021 and ended March 10, 2022. It lasted 99 days. No regular-season games were lost, though some spring training games were canceled. The 2021 fight centered on pre-arbitration salary, the competitive balance tax thresholds, and the minimum salary. Those were meaningful disputes, but they were not structural ones.

This fight is structural. A salary cap is not a threshold to negotiate up or down — it is either there or it isn't. That binary makes a quick resolution less likely. If this lockout runs as long as 2021's, baseball doesn't return until March. If it runs longer, the 2027 season is in real jeopardy.

What's Next

Free agents, teams, and players are operating in an eight-day sprint. Jazz Chisholm, Seiya Suzuki, and dozens of other players are racing to get deals done before December 1. Teams are finishing trades and arbitration-eligible decisions. Everything that can be done will be done in the next eight days, because after that, nothing can be done at all. When the lockout ends — whether in weeks or months — the baseball calendar will tell the story of how much damage was done. Right now, baseball's best bet is to hope the precedent from 2021 holds. The gap between the two sides suggests it won't.

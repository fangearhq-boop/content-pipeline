Seiya Suzuki Is on the Market. The Cubs Could Lose a Third of Their Lineup.

By Marcus Cole | Ballpark Banter | November 23, 2026

Seiya Suzuki turned down $23.15 million. That is a significant number by almost any measure — it represents the qualifying offer the Cubs extended after deciding he was worth protecting with draft-pick compensation. He rejected it November 17, which means he is now testing a free market projected to pay him $90 to $110 million over four years.

When a player turns down a one-year, $23 million guarantee to bet on himself, it means his representation believes the multi-year market is substantially better. In Suzuki's case, the projections suggest they are right. Spotrac projects a four-year, $109.9 million deal. Sports Illustrated projects four years at $90 million. Those figures represent different methodologies, but they bracket a range that is significantly better than the QO's value on a per-year basis.

The Teams Involved

Reports have linked the Mariners, Red Sox, Blue Jays, and Phillies to Suzuki's market. That's a mix of contenders and organizations with real financial capacity. Seattle has long coveted outfield help with right-handed power. Boston's outfield has been a conversation since the end of last season. Philadelphia wants to stay competitive and needs depth around their core. Toronto is aggressively building before the lockout and has been linked to multiple free agents this week.

None of these reports have included confirmed offers. That's typical at this stage of the market — teams are positioning, communicating interest, and gathering information before making formal bids. The formal bidding typically accelerates when players signal readiness to decide. With the lockout eight days away, everyone's timeline compresses simultaneously.

What This Means for the Cubs

The Cubs extended Suzuki a qualifying offer, which means they made a credible attempt to retain him. His rejection signals that his market value exceeds what Chicago is comfortable paying for a 32-year-old outfielder over multiple years — or that the Cubs, recognizing the gap, chose not to escalate to a true negotiation.

Either way, the organization is looking at an offseason that could reshape a third of their lineup. Ian Happ is also a free agent. Carson Kelly is a free agent. Those three departures combined would remove proven contributors from a roster that reached the postseason in 2026. Replacing them before the lockout — or waiting until baseball resumes — are the two options, and neither is clean.

Suzuki's departure would also trigger a draft-pick compensation payment from whichever team signs him. The Cubs collect that pick regardless of where Suzuki lands. It's a small return for a player of his caliber, but it reflects the qualifying offer system working as designed.

What's Next

Suzuki has eight days to get a deal done before the December 1 lockout. If he doesn't sign by then, he waits out the freeze alongside every other unsigned free agent. For a player who turned down guaranteed money to maximize his value on the open market, waiting through a lockout that could last months is not an appealing prospect. Expect his market to move quickly this week. The Cubs will watch. The team that lands him gets a proven hitter with international appeal and three to four years of meaningful production. That's a real asset in a market where outfield offense is never easy to find.

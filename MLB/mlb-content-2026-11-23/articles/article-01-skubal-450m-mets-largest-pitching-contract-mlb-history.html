Tarik Skubal Is a Met: 9 Years, $450 Million, and the Largest Pitching Contract in Baseball History

By Ryan Calloway | Ballpark Banter | November 23, 2026

Tarik Skubal is a New York Met. Nine years. $450 million. The largest pitching contract in the history of professional baseball, pending a physical, agreed to Sunday per multiple national reports from ESPN, CBS Sports, and the Associated Press.

The number needs context. Yoshinobu Yamamoto's $325 million, 12-year deal with the Dodgers was considered a watershed moment when it was signed. It set a record that everyone assumed would hold for a long time. Skubal's deal doesn't just break it — it makes it look modest. At $50 million per year, Skubal's average annual value is nearly double Yamamoto's $27 million mark.

The deal includes a full no-trade clause and a $45 million signing bonus, which was reportedly structured to be shielded from any work-stoppage complications given the CBA expiring December 1.

How the Mets Won

The Mets were not alone. The Los Angeles Dodgers, San Diego Padres, and Toronto Blue Jays all made serious pushes for Skubal. The Dodgers were considered a frontrunner for weeks — their pitching infrastructure and playoff history make them a natural fit for any elite starter. The Padres entered late and offered aggressively. The Blue Jays had the financial capacity and a compelling competitive window.

None of them got the deal done. Steve Cohen, the Mets' owner and baseball's biggest spender by habit, went to a number that ended the competition. The initial Mets offer was reported at approximately $400 million. The final agreed figure is $450 million. The gap between those two numbers is not small — and it reflects the kind of negotiating leverage a two-time Cy Young winner at the peak of his career carries in an open market.

The Mets' argument to Skubal was straightforward: come to New York, be the foundation, and build something. Cohen's willingness to spend past what anyone else would do is what distinguishes the Mets in every major free-agent competition. It's the same quality that landed Juan Soto on his record-setting deal. It worked again here.

What Skubal Brings

Skubal is a generational pitcher at a moment when generational pitchers rarely reach free agency. Detroit's extension conversations reportedly collapsed with a gap of more than $200 million — meaning no deal was ever close. That opened the market, and the market responded with the kind of contract Skubal's performance demanded.

He is a two-time Cy Young winner. He works deep into games. He misses bats at a rate that holds up across multiple seasons, not just one exceptional year. He is 29 — old enough to have an established track record, young enough that the Mets are not paying for decline years at the front of the deal.

For a Mets team that went 74-88 last season despite carrying baseball's highest Opening Day payroll, Skubal is not just an addition — he is a philosophical reset. The team has tried to buy wins by assembling expensive rosters around rotation uncertainty. That approach failed. This approach says: start with the best pitcher available, build the rotation around him, and let the rest follow.

The Lockout Wrinkle

The CBA expires at 11:59 PM ET on December 1. A lockout is widely expected at 12:01 AM on December 2. Skubal's signing bonus was reportedly structured with that in mind — but the nine-year commitment extends well beyond any potential work stoppage.

The deal being completed before the lockout matters for practical reasons: once the freeze starts, no additional players can be signed or traded, no roster construction moves can be made, and the Mets cannot further supplement Skubal's supporting cast until baseball resumes. For a team that just committed $450 million to a starting pitcher, the pressure to build a complete contender around him before December 1 is now enormous.

What's Next

Skubal's deal is the defining transaction of the 2026-27 offseason. It sets the financial floor for how the market values elite pitching and signals what other teams will have to pay if they want to compete for frontline starters going forward. The Mets now have eight days to add around him before the lockout. If the Mets front office uses that window aggressively, the roster that takes the field in 2027 could look dramatically different from the one that finished last in the NL East. That's the bet Cohen just made: $450 million on the foundation, everything else before December 1.

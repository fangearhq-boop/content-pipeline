What Would a 2026 MLB Lockout Mean? The 2021 Precedent and What's Different This Time

By Jake Torres | November 26, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

Baseball has been here before, and it was not quick or painless.

The 2021-22 MLB lockout lasted 99 days — from December 2, 2021, to March 10, 2022. It canceled spring training games, delayed opening day by one week, and shortened the regular season schedule. Players and owners negotiated through the winter and into the spring before federal mediators helped push the two sides to a resolution.

The lockout that appears likely to begin December 2, 2026, is built on the same foundation. The same central dispute. The same union position. Five years later and not much closer to resolution.

What Caused the 2021 Lockout — and What's Causing This One

The 2021-22 lockout was driven by the owners' push for a salary cap and the union's categorical refusal to accept one. After 99 days of negotiation, the two sides settled on a deal that included competitive balance tax thresholds, minimum salary increases, and a pre-arbitration bonus pool — but no salary cap. The union's core position held.

Five years later, MLB is back at the table with a cap proposal. The league's latest offer would delay implementation until 2028, set the cap at $258.3 million, establish a floor of $180.2 million, and guarantee all contracts under the system. MLBPA executive director Bruce Meyer has described any cap structure as a nonstarter. The position is the same.

When the same disagreement produces the same impasse, the same outcome — a prolonged work stoppage — is the logical expectation.

What Freezes When the Lockout Begins

A lockout is imposed by the owners, not by the players. It is not a strike. What it is, in practical terms, is a complete halt to all player-team business. No free-agent signings. No trades. No contract extensions. No minor league signings. No player-team communications of any kind. The players and their representatives are locked out of team facilities and all official activities.

For teams with unfinished business — rosters to fill, free-agent deals to close — the lockout creates immediate urgency. Any player who does not have a signed contract in place before December 1 at 11:59 PM ET becomes unreachable the moment the lockout begins.

What Is Different This Time

The 2021 lockout had one major advantage: it did not threaten the regular season until very late in the process. The 99 days consumed spring training but only cost a week of the regular season. That math is different in 2026.

The 2027 MLB regular season is scheduled to begin in late March. If a lockout begins December 2 and follows a similar 99-day timeline, it would end around mid-March — just two weeks before opening day. That would be enough time to hold abbreviated spring training but would create significant roster and preparation concerns for every club. If the lockout extends past 99 days, regular-season games are genuinely at risk.

MLB also filed a federal lawsuit this week seeking a declaration that the lockout is legal under antitrust law — an escalation that the 2021 lockout did not feature. That legal action runs on its own timeline and may complicate or prolong negotiations.

What's Next

The CBA expires December 1. A lockout is expected December 2. Both sides say they will continue negotiating even after the lockout begins, following the 2021 model. The 2027 regular season opener is targeted for late March, giving the two sides approximately 115 days to reach a deal before games are threatened. With the salary cap as the central dispute and both sides publicly holding firm, the first weeks of the lockout are likely to pass with little visible progress. Baseball fans should prepare for a long winter.

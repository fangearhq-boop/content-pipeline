The Joshua Baez Chess Match Has Started — And It Goes Both Ways

By Jake Torres

August 19, 2026

The debut was one for the record books. On August 15 at Wrigley Field, Joshua Baez became the first player in major league history to homer in each of his first three career at-bats — all in a single game. The first was a 449-foot blast to center field on the first pitch he ever saw. He finished 3-for-4 with 5 RBI and introduced himself to the sport in the most dramatic way possible.

Four days later, Baez was in the lineup against the Cincinnati Reds. He did not homer. He hit a sacrifice fly — exit velocity 90.6 mph, launch angle 44°. He drove in a run. The Cardinals scored. And something important happened in the process: the chess match between Baez and the rest of the league officially began.

What the League Knows

Every hitting debut that makes history immediately attracts attention from the rest of the sport. Pitching coaches watch the at-bat video. Advance scouts build a report. By the time Baez faced Cincinnati on August 18, there was a game plan.

The specifics of any pitcher's approach stay internal, but the general framework for handling a hitter who destroyed the first three fastballs he saw is predictable: test the inner half, throw more breaking balls early in counts, use velocity variations to disrupt timing. A hitter who has not seen major league pitching consistently has not had the chance to make the adjustments that experienced players have built over years of at-bats.

Baez had 36 home runs in Triple-A before the call-up. He is not an inexperienced hitter — he is an inexperienced MLB hitter, which is a different thing. The adjustment curve at this level is real, and it starts the moment pitchers decide they have seen enough to form a plan.

The Encouraging Sign

The sacrifice fly on August 18 is actually a more encouraging result than it sounds. A hitter who is purely swinging for the fences in his first week — trying to duplicate the debut — is a hitter who can be gotten out with the right approach. A hitter who makes contact and drives in a run with a fly ball is demonstrating baseball IQ alongside the raw power.

Baez is not just a home run hitter. He is a hitter who can adjust within at-bats and within games. That quality matters more in the long run than any single game outcome.

His partnership with Jordan Walker is already drawing attention. Two powerful young Cardinals hitters who complement each other in the lineup creates a sequencing problem for opposing pitchers: you cannot just work around one of them.

The Timeline

How quickly a hitter adapts to the major league adjustment period varies. Some players struggle for weeks before finding their footing. Others figure it out within days. The metrics on Baez's August 18 sacrifice fly — 90.6 mph exit velocity and a 44-degree launch angle — suggest he is making hard contact even in the games that do not produce home runs.

The next two weeks will reveal the shape of the adjustment. If pitchers are exposing a consistent weakness, the numbers will show it quickly. If Baez is adapting as fast as pitchers are adjusting to him, he will keep showing up in the box score even without hitting home runs every game.

What's Next

Watch Baez's first two weeks of data closely — his strikeout rate, chase rate on breaking balls below the zone, and hard-contact percentage will tell the real story. A debut like his creates immediate film for every opposing pitching staff. The number that matters now is not how many HRs he hits this week, but how much of the strike zone he covers consistently.

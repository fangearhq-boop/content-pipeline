Joshua Baez, One Week In: What the Chess Match Looks Like Now

By Jake Torres | Ballpark Banter | August 23, 2026

One week ago, Joshua Baez walked to the plate at Wrigley Field for the first at-bat of his major league career and hit a 449-foot home run off the Cubs' starter. Then he did it again. Then he did it again. Three home runs in his first three career plate appearances — a feat no player in baseball history had ever accomplished. The week since has been a more complicated story. And a more interesting one.

The Debut, the Slump, the Recovery

After the historic debut, Baez went 0-for-15 with six strikeouts over his next three games. The adjustment by opposing pitching staffs was immediate and predictable. Rookies with historic power displays get attacked in specific ways — soft stuff away, high heat inside, breaking balls in the dirt early in counts to establish the strikeout pattern. Baez saw all of it in those three games.

The slump was expected by anyone who has watched how major league pitching processes a new threat. It was not a sign that the debut was a fluke. It was a sign that the league had begun the adjustment process.

Then Baez recovered. His current line — .251/.332/.552 with 12 home runs and a 32.7 percent strikeout rate — reflects a player who absorbed the initial wave of adjustments and responded. Twelve home runs in his first week of major league baseball. The power is real. The questions now are the right ones.

The Strikeout Rate

The 32.7 percent strikeout rate is the number worth watching. That is high — meaningfully above what a player can sustain long-term while maintaining offensive value at the level his slugging percentage suggests. The league average strikeout rate runs in the mid-twenties. Baez's 32.7 is the kind of number that makes pitching staffs willing to offer a walk in order to avoid a mistake that leaves the park.

The power compensates for strikeouts at a .552 slugging clip. Whether it continues to compensate depends on how Baez's strike zone discipline evolves over the next several months of pitching adjustments. That is the real question. Not whether the power is real — it is — but whether the contact rate develops enough to prevent the strikeout rate from becoming a consistent liability.

What Round Two Looks Like

The chess match between Baez and major league pitching is at its beginning. Pitching staffs have their first week of data. They have found some things that work — particularly the ability to generate whiffs with breaking balls down and in. Baez's job is to find countermeasures: lay off the chase pitches, make adjustments to the high-and-tight fastball, and force pitchers to attack him differently.

Players who make careers out of historic beginnings are the ones who can win multiple rounds of that cycle. The debut was Round One, and it went to Baez in historic fashion. The adjustment stretch was the league's counter. Baez's current line — 12 home runs, .552 slugging — suggests he has already answered in Round Two.

Round Three starts tomorrow.

What's Next

Baez remains in the Cardinals' everyday lineup, and the Cardinals will continue to let him face every kind of pitcher and every kind of situation that September provides. The strikeout rate will be a storyline that follows him into the offseason. So will the home run total, which may grow substantially before the season ends. One week in, the most interesting story in baseball is still the most interesting story.

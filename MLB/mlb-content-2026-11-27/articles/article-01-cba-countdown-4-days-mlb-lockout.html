CBA Countdown: MLB Has 4 Days Before the Lockout Begins

By Ryan Calloway | November 27, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

The collective bargaining agreement between Major League Baseball and the Major League Baseball Players Association expires in four days — December 1, 2026, at 11:59 PM Eastern Time.

Both sides know it. Neither has moved. The game is heading toward its second lockout in five years, and this one may be harder to resolve than the last.

On November 25, MLB filed a preemptive federal lawsuit in the Southern District of New York, seeking a court declaration that the impending lockout is legal and attempting to block any potential antitrust challenge from the players' union. The MLBPA was direct in its response: "The lawsuit filed today by MLB is completely frivolous."

What MLB Wants

Commissioner Rob Manfred has made the owners' position unambiguous. Asked whether he could envision a deal without a salary cap and floor, Manfred said: "I can't imagine the owners ratifying a deal that does not include a cap and floor."

The owners' current proposal calls for a hard salary cap of $258.2 million and a payroll floor of $180.2 million, with both figures set to begin in 2028. The proposal also includes a minimum player salary increase and adjustments to service time rules.

For the 30 MLB team owners, the cap-and-floor system would address their primary concern: competitive balance and cost certainty in an era where the gap between the highest-spending teams and the lowest has become a structural fault line.

What the Union Wants

The MLBPA has made its position equally clear: a salary cap is a nonstarter. The union has historically viewed a cap as a ceiling on player earnings, one that would permanently limit what the market can pay the game's elite players.

The union's preferred system — a luxury tax-based competitive balance framework — has governed the game for decades. Under the current structure, teams pay a tax when they exceed a threshold, but there is no hard ceiling on what any team can spend. The Mets' nine-year, $450 million deal with Tarik Skubal is the latest example of what that system allows.

A hard cap, in the union's view, would have made that deal impossible.

The 2021 Precedent

The last time the CBA expired without a new agreement, the lockout started December 2, 2021 and lasted 99 days — ending March 10, 2022, just weeks before the season was scheduled to begin. Spring training games were canceled. Opening Day was pushed back by one week. The dispute that caused it: the same fundamental disagreement about player compensation, revenue sharing, and service time manipulation.

That lockout ended when both sides reached a last-minute compromise that neither was fully satisfied with. The result was a five-year CBA that expires December 1, 2026 — today's deadline.

The 2026 version of the dispute carries a new element: the federal lawsuit. MLB's preemptive legal move has no precedent in recent CBA negotiations. It signals that the owners are more prepared to weather a prolonged dispute than they were in 2021.

What It Means for the 2027 Season

The 2027 schedule puts Opening Day around March 25. That gives both sides approximately 115 days from the start of the lockout before the first game is threatened — longer than the 2021 lockout lasted. Spring training, however, would be in jeopardy weeks before Opening Day. If the lockout extends into late February without resolution, Grapefruit and Cactus League games will begin to fall off the calendar.

ESPN has reported that a deal would need to be in place by approximately the start of May to avoid any lost regular-season games. That is a longer runway than the 2021 lockout produced, but it is not an unlimited one.

Four days. Then the game stops.

What's Next

The CBA expires December 1, 2026 at 11:59 PM ET. A lockout is expected to begin December 2. All free agent signings, trades, minor league transactions, and player-team communications will be prohibited for the duration. The first negotiating sessions under lockout conditions will determine whether this dispute trends toward a quick resolution or a prolonged standoff. Watch for any change in the MLBPA's position on the salary cap as the primary indicator of movement.

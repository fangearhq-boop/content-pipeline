What Is an MLB Lockout? A Fan's Guide to What Happens Next

By Marcus Cole | November 27, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

If you were not paying close attention to baseball in the winter of 2021-22, here is what you missed — and what you are about to see again. Major League Baseball is heading toward a lockout. The collective bargaining agreement expires December 1, 2026, and a work stoppage is expected to begin December 2. For fans who have not been through one of these before, here is what it means and what to expect.

What Is a Lockout?

A lockout is an owner-imposed work stoppage. Unlike a player strike — where the players walk out — a lockout is a decision by team owners to cease operations rather than continue working under expired contract terms. The owners are not required to lock out the players, but it is a tool they can use once a CBA expires.

During a lockout, all 30 MLB teams cannot sign, trade, or even contact any player under contract or on their 40-man roster. Free agents cannot sign with any team. Minor league players cannot be called up. No trades can be completed. The Hot Stove — the baseball term for the offseason transaction market — goes completely cold.

What Happened in 2021

The last MLB lockout started December 2, 2021 and lasted until March 10, 2022 — 99 days. All spring training games during that period were canceled. Opening Day was pushed back by one week, and two series from the regular season schedule were initially lost before a final agreement allowed the full 162-game season to be played.

The 2021-22 lockout was the longest MLB work stoppage since the player strike of 1994-95, which canceled the World Series. The 2021 dispute was resolved when both sides reached a compromise that neither was fully satisfied with. The result: a five-year CBA that expires December 1, 2026 — today's deadline.

What Is the Dispute This Time?

The core disagreement is the same one that caused the 2021 lockout. Owners want a hard salary cap and a payroll floor — a system that would set a maximum and minimum for what any team can spend on players. Commissioner Rob Manfred has proposed a cap of $258.2 million and a floor of $180.2 million, to begin in 2028.

The MLB Players Association has categorically rejected a salary cap. The union views a cap as a ceiling on earnings that would permanently limit what the market can pay the game's elite players. The current luxury tax system allows teams to exceed a threshold by paying a tax, but there is no hard limit on spending. The Mets' $450 million deal with Tarik Skubal is the latest example of what that system allows. A hard cap, in the union's view, would have made that deal impossible.

Neither side has moved. That is why the lockout is coming.

When Does It Affect the 2027 Season?

The 2027 MLB schedule puts Opening Day around March 25. That is approximately 115 days from the start of the lockout on December 2 — a longer runway than the 2021 lockout produced. The regular season is not immediately threatened.

Spring training is a different story. Grapefruit League and Cactus League games typically begin in mid-February. If the lockout extends past late January without resolution, those games will begin to fall off the calendar. Players cannot report to camps, cannot participate in team workouts, and cannot receive any communication from team staff while the lockout is in effect.

ESPN has reported that a deal would need to be in place by approximately the start of May to avoid any lost regular-season games. That is a longer window than 2021 provided, but it is not unlimited. If this dispute runs as long as 2021 did, the 2027 season will not be in immediate danger. If it runs significantly longer, the math changes.

What's Next

The CBA expires December 1, 2026 at 11:59 PM ET. A lockout is expected to begin December 2. The first indicator of how long this one will last is whether the MLBPA shows any flexibility on the salary cap question — the foundational issue neither side has moved on. Watch for the first formal negotiating sessions under lockout conditions and any public statements from union leadership about their willingness to revisit their position on the cap. That movement, or its absence, will tell you everything about the timeline ahead.

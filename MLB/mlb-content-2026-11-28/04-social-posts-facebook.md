### STORY 1: Chisholm to Blue Jays

#### Long-Form Post

Jazz Chisholm Jr. is a Toronto Blue Jay.

After weeks of reported interest from more than 11 teams — including the Yankees, Nationals, Mets, and others — Chisholm lands in Toronto on a one-year, $20 million deal with a player option for the 2028 season. And unlike many of the other top free agents on the market, Chisholm came without a qualifying offer attached, meaning Toronto didn't have to surrender a draft pick to sign him.

For the Blue Jays, this is exactly the kind of move they needed. Chisholm brings elite speed (41 stolen bases in 2026), legitimate power (19 home runs), and the kind of defensive versatility that lets a team deploy him at second base, outfield, or wherever the lineup needs him most. He's a catalyst — the kind of player who makes lineup construction easier for a manager, because he works at the top, the middle, or as a table-setter in any configuration.

The player option is the interesting wrinkle. If Chisholm has a big year — or the market expands in a post-lockout world — he can opt out and chase a larger contract in 2028. If things go sideways, the safety net is there. It's a smart structure for a player coming off a strong year who wanted to bet on himself without locking in a long deal at a time when CBA uncertainty clouds the whole market.

Toronto gets an impact player. Chisholm gets optionality. If the Blue Jays make a run in 2027, he could be the reason.

### STORY 2: 72-Hour Countdown

#### Long-Form Post

Seventy-two hours. That's what's left before baseball's collective bargaining agreement expires and the market freezes.

The CBA runs out at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1. Owners are expected to impose a lockout the following day — Dec. 2 — which would immediately halt all transactions: no free agent signings, no trades, no minor league deals. Nothing moves until a new agreement is reached.

In that environment, every team that hasn't solved its roster problems is making calls today. And the two most prominent players still unsigned — pitcher Michael King (3.21 ERA, 32 starts, declined the Padres' $28 million option) and outfielder Seiya Suzuki — will either find deals in the next 72 hours or enter an indefinite holding period.

The historical parallel is familiar. The 2021-22 lockout lasted 99 days. Spring training was canceled, Opening Day was pushed back, and players went three months without being able to negotiate with any team. The sides spent most of that time arguing over the same salary cap issue that is at the core of the current dispute, and the league filed a preemptive antitrust lawsuit this week — an escalation that wasn't present in 2021.

A 2027 Opening Day is around March 25. That gives about 115 days before games would actually be threatened. But spring training, minor league signings, Rule 5 Draft logistics, and the entire rhythm of a baseball offseason go into deep freeze the moment the lockout begins.

Teams are rushing. Players are waiting. Baseball has 72 hours to figure this out.

### STORY 3: Cubs Drop $240M in 12 Hours

#### Long-Form Post

For 12 hours on Friday, the Chicago Cubs looked like the most aggressive franchise in baseball.

Their headline move: Trevor Rogers on a five-year, $120 million contract. Rogers was widely considered the second-best left-handed starter on the free-agent market behind Tarik Skubal (now a New York Met), and the Cubs grabbed him in what turned out to be part of a larger 12-hour spending surge that pushed their reported total above $240 million.

For context: the Cubs went 74-88 last season. That's not a roster that typically demands this kind of investment. But the front office appears to have decided that the talent base is good enough — and the market window narrow enough before a lockout freezes everything — to accelerate the timeline dramatically.

Rogers' numbers weren't overwhelmed in a down year — he stayed relatively healthy, showed improved command, and demonstrated the kind of consistency that earns a five-year commitment. For a rotation that added Dylan Cease earlier this offseason, Rogers gives the Cubs a legitimate 1-2 lefty combination at the front of the staff heading into 2027.

The North Side hasn't felt this like a win-now operation in several years. Friday's spending spree served notice: 2027 is different.

### STORY 4: White Sox Sign Dustin May

#### Long-Form Post

When the White Sox make a $52.5 million pitching investment, it means something.

Chicago's South Side franchise has spent the better part of four seasons in full rebuild mode — shedding veterans, accumulating prospects, losing games at record rates to get there. That chapter appears to be closing. Former Dodger right-hander Dustin May just agreed to a three-year, $52.5 million contract with the White Sox, and while one signing doesn't flip a rebuild, the signal is unmistakable.

May has had a complicated career: extraordinary upside when healthy, derailed by two Tommy John surgeries that have cost him time at the highest level. But the stuff, when he's on the mound, is elite. His fastball generates elite spin rates, his slider is a wipeout pitch against right-handers, and his delivery creates deception that makes average hitters look foolish. The question has always been durability — and the White Sox are betting three years and $52.5 million that the injury chapter is behind him.

It's a risk. But it's the kind of risk a team takes when it's transitioning from "collecting assets" to "trying to win." The White Sox have that look right now — new ownership attention, a stadium project on the horizon, and now a rotation piece that signals they're ready to compete.

### STORY 5: Who's Still Unsigned When the Freeze Hits

#### Long-Form Post

By Wednesday morning, the most significant free-agent market in recent memory will be on ice.

When the lockout takes effect — expected Dec. 2, the day after the CBA expires — all transactions stop completely. No signings. No trades. No negotiations. The free agents who haven't found a home by midnight Tuesday will wait however long it takes for a new collective bargaining agreement to get done.

Three names stand out as the most significant players likely to still be unsigned when the freeze hits: pitcher Michael King, outfielder Seiya Suzuki, and outfielder Randy Arozarena.

King, 31, turned down the Padres' $28 million qualifying offer after posting a 3.21 ERA across 32 starts. He's the best starting pitcher left on the market after the top of the class was quickly claimed. Any team that hasn't yet found a rotation anchor will be calling King's representation right now — but if no deal gets done by Tuesday, he waits.

Suzuki is the top remaining position player in the class. His defense is elite, his bat has shown consistent contact ability, and teams with outfield vacancies were expected to pursue him aggressively. A lockout puts that pursuit on hold indefinitely.

The 2021-22 lockout gives a template: 99 days from Dec. 2 to March 10 before baseball resumed transactions. If this one runs the same length, spring training ends entirely, and the 2027 season opener gets pushed back. The 2027 Opening Day window is around March 25, which gives about 115 days before actual games are threatened.

It's going to be a long winter for the unsigned players. And for the fans who wanted to see their teams make moves.

White Sox Sign Dustin May for $52.5M — The Rebuild Phase Is Over

By Jake Torres

November 28, 2026

The Chicago White Sox are spending money on a starting pitcher. That sentence alone would have seemed unlikely 18 months ago. Now it's official: Dustin May has agreed to a three-year, $52.5 million contract with the White Sox — the most significant pitching investment the South Side franchise has made in years.

May, the former Los Angeles Dodger, brings an arsenal that generates genuine excitement: a fastball with elite spin rates, a slider that plays off it effectively, and a physical profile that projects as durable once the injury history stops repeating itself. The challenge for Chicago — and the reason this signing carries risk — is that May's career has been repeatedly interrupted by significant elbow surgeries.

What May Brings — and What He's Cost Teams Before

At his best, Dustin May looks like a frontline starter. His stuff is genuinely elite — the kind of arm that coaches point to in development conversations as the ceiling to reach for. The fastball command, the spin on his breaking ball, the ability to work both sides of the plate — it's all there.

What's also there is a rehabilitation history that has cost him significant time at the major-league level. The White Sox are betting that the latest recovery is complete, that May's arm is durable enough to handle 25-plus starts per season, and that three years at $17.5 million per year represents fair value for the upside he provides.

It's the kind of calculated risk that contending teams take — and the fact that the White Sox are taking it is itself the signal.

The End of the Rebuild

The Chicago White Sox's rebuild has been one of the most committed and painful in modern baseball history. Multiple consecutive 100-loss seasons. A systematic tear-down of anything resembling a major-league roster. The logic was clear: acquire prospects, absorb losses, build toward a window that would justify the suffering.

That window may now be approaching. Signing May to a $52.5 million contract is not a rebuild move. It's a contention move. Teams that are still acquiring prospects don't hand out three-year pitching contracts. Teams that are transitioning from "collecting assets" to "trying to win" do.

The White Sox have additional talent developing — position players and pitchers who have moved through their system and are now closer to the major-league level. Adding May gives that talent a veteran arm to build a rotation around, rather than asking young starters to carry the entire burden from day one.

What's Next

May's deal is pending a physical and expected to be finalized before the Dec. 2 lockout. If the lockout resolves in time for a normal spring training, May would be expected to anchor the White Sox rotation from Opening Day.

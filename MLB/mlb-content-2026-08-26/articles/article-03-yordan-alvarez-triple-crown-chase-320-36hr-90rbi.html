Yordan Alvarez Holds All Three AL Leads. The Home Run Race Is the Problem.

By Ryan Calloway | Ballpark Banter | August 26, 2026

Yordan Alvarez is leading the American League in batting average, home runs, and RBIs. That sentence sounds simple. What it describes is not. The last player to finish first in all three categories in the same season was Miguel Cabrera, who did it with the Detroit Tigers in 2012. Before that, you have to go back to Carl Yastrzemski in 1967. The Triple Crown is the rarest sustained achievement in baseball — not a single night, not a streak, but three separate races held simultaneously over a full season. Alvarez is currently winning all three.

The batting average and RBI leads are comfortable. The home run lead is not.

Where Each Race Stands

Alvarez is hitting .320, leading the AL by 10 points over Chandler Simpson (.310) of Tampa Bay. That is not an insurmountable lead, but it is not a narrow one either. Batting averages tend to stabilize late in the season — the large sample size works against dramatic swings. If Alvarez finishes September hitting .290, he still wins the batting title comfortably.

The RBI lead tells a similar story. Alvarez has 90, with Pete Alonso of Baltimore the closest competitor at 85 — five behind. Five RBIs is a single productive week. But it is also a lead that requires a sustained cold stretch by Alvarez and a sustained hot stretch by Alonso to close. Historically, the RBI lead in a Triple Crown bid tends to be the most stable category once it is established.

The home run race is different. Alvarez has 36. Junior Caminero of Tampa Bay has 35. Ben Rice of the Yankees has 34. Caminero is one swing away from tying. The two of them are separated by a single home run with approximately 29 games remaining. In a normal September, a right-handed power hitter in a warm-weather ballpark can hit eight to twelve home runs. That means the lead Alvarez holds right now could change multiple times before the final week of the regular season.

Why This Is Historically Rare

Triple Crown seasons are not just rare because all three leads are hard to maintain. They are rare because the strategic calculus of three simultaneous races affects a hitter's approach. With a comfortable batting average lead, a hitter can be more aggressive. With a tight home run race, a hitter might expand the zone chasing power pitches. Those instincts, sometimes, work against each other.

Alvarez has managed this before. He is one of the most disciplined, high-contact power hitters in the game. His strikeout rate is below the league average for a player with his home run total. That discipline is what makes the batting average lead (.320) credible for a 36-home-run hitter — he is not trading contact for power, he is producing both. The question over the final month is whether that approach holds up when Caminero is tracking him and Alvarez knows the home run lead is thin.

What's Next

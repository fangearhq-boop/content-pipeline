Qualifying Offer Deadline: 8 Players, $23.15M Each, One Week to Decide

By Ryan Calloway | November 10, 2026 | Ballpark Banter

By 4 PM Eastern on November 17, eight major league baseball players will have made one of the most consequential decisions of their careers. Each received a one-year, $23.15 million qualifying offer from their respective teams on November 2. Each has seven days from today to accept or decline. The historical track record on this decision is clear: of 157 qualifying offers issued since the system was implemented after 2012, only 18 have been accepted.

The Eight Players

The players who received qualifying offers are: Seiya Suzuki (Chicago Cubs), Randy Arozarena (Seattle Mariners), David Bednar (New York Yankees), Jake Bauers, Trevor Rogers, Ryan Jeffers, Brandon Lowe, and Adrian Morejon.

Suzuki is the most prominent name. The Cubs outfielder is 30 years old and coming off a productive offensive season. His market is expected to be strong, particularly from teams seeking a disciplined right-handed bat with power. Accepting $23.15M for one year rather than pursuing a multi-year deal would be unusual for a player at his stage of free agency.

Arozarena signed a one-year, $15.65 million contract with Seattle for 2026 after his arbitration process — and is now in full free agency. As a 25-year-old who has shown consistent offensive production, he is expected to command a multi-year deal that far exceeds the QO value over its full term.

Bednar, the Yankees' closer, would likely receive strong interest from multiple clubs seeking elite bullpen options. Closers with his track record regularly sign contracts that dwarf the one-year QO value.

How the System Works

The qualifying offer system is designed to compensate teams when they lose elite free agents. If a player declines the QO and signs with a new team, the original team receives a compensatory draft pick. The signing team forfeits a draft pick in exchange — a cost that makes teams with premium draft selections less likely to sign QO players.

This matters for roster construction beyond the individual signings. The Cubs and Mariners, if Suzuki and Arozarena decline and sign elsewhere, receive compensatory picks that add to their draft capital. Teams that sign multiple QO players in one offseason face meaningful pick forfeiture costs.

The QO value itself — $23.15 million — is calculated as the average of the top 125 salaries in baseball. It increases each year as overall salaries rise. The figure is designed to be attractive enough to impose a draft-pick tax on the signing team, but not so attractive that elite players prefer it to multi-year security. So far, the formula has worked: the acceptance rate remains below 12 percent.

What to Expect

Suzuki, Arozarena, and Bednar are all expected to decline. Their markets are active — with the GM Meetings running this week, teams are establishing interest in all three — and multi-year deals from the most interested suitors will far exceed the one-year value. For the lower-profile QO recipients, the calculus is similar: even a two-year deal at modest annual value exceeds the total protection offered by the QO.

What's Next

The November 17 deadline falls the same week as the MLB Owners Meetings (November 17-19). By the time the QO deadline passes, the broader offseason picture will be clearer — either because Skubal has signed, or because the CBA situation has escalated. For players like Suzuki and Arozarena, the QO decision is a formality. The real market begins November 18.

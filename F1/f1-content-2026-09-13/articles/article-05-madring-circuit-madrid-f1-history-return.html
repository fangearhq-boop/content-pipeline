Forty-Five Years in the Making: The Madring Brings Formula 1 Back to Madrid

By Marcus Cole | September 13, 2026

The Madring's debut on the 2026 F1 calendar marks the end of a 45-year absence of Formula 1 from the Madrid area — and the beginning of a new chapter for the sport in Spain.

The date was September 27, 1981. The circuit was Jarama, 20 miles north of Madrid. The race was the Spanish Grand Prix — the last Formula 1 race held in the Madrid area for 45 years.

Gilles Villeneuve and Alan Jones swapped positions in that 1981 race, with the championship playing out around them in ways that defined the season. Then F1 left Madrid. For decades, the Spanish Grand Prix migrated between circuits — Jerez, Valencia, and ultimately Barcelona's Circuit de Catalunya, which became the sport's home in Spain for the modern era.

On September 13, 2026, Formula 1 returned to the capital.

Building the Madring

The Madring is not a conversion of an existing track or a circuit adapted from a street layout. It is a purpose-built Formula 1 venue constructed on the grounds of IFEMA Madrid, the city's international exhibition and congress centre. The location — accessible by metro, surrounded by modern infrastructure, embedded in a major European capital — was part of its appeal for Formula 1's commercial strategy.

The circuit stretches 5.47 kilometres and incorporates 22 corners. Two of those corners are underground tunnels, giving the Madring a visual drama unlike any other circuit on the 2026 calendar. Drivers passing through the tunnels at race speed — where the Madring's average speed of approximately 218 kilometres per hour is maintained — experience a complete cut-off of ambient light and external sightlines that is unique in contemporary F1.

La Monumental

The circuit's centrepiece is Turn 12, known as La Monumental. The name is well-earned.

La Monumental is a 550-metre banked corner — the longest sustained banking in any current Formula 1 venue — with a 24% gradient and 13.5 degrees of banking. At Formula 1 speeds, drivers sustain approximately 6 seconds of continuous lateral G-force through the corner before transitioning to the next section of the layout. There is nothing else like it in the 2026 F1 season.

The design draws inspiration from the great oval banking tracks of motor racing's history, updated for the technical demands and safety requirements of modern Formula 1. Drivers and engineers alike cited La Monumental as the circuit's defining challenge during the Madring's practice sessions, with grip levels, tyre wear rates, and aero behaviour through the banking all representing unknowns that teams had to solve in real time.

Replacing Barcelona

The Madring's arrival on the 2026 F1 calendar comes at the expense of Barcelona-Catalunya — the Circuit de Catalunya, which had hosted the Spanish Grand Prix for decades. The transition from a traditional permanent circuit outside the city to an urban venue in the capital is a reflection of Formula 1's strategic direction: stadium-style events in major cities, accessible to millions of fans who would never travel to a remote permanent circuit.

The formula has worked elsewhere. Las Vegas, Miami, Singapore, and Monaco all demonstrate that urban F1 races draw different audiences and generate different commercial returns than traditional venue events. Madring is the newest addition to that portfolio.

What's Next

The 2026 Spanish Grand Prix at the Madring is Round 14 of 23. The circuit's debut, now complete, establishes it in the historical record of Formula 1 venues. The next step is earning its place as a permanent fixture on the calendar — a process that depends on race quality, crowd attendance, and the commercial partnerships that underpin the event's viability. Judging by the reaction to the 2026 qualifying weekend and the dramatic race itself, the Madring's future on the Formula 1 calendar looks assured.

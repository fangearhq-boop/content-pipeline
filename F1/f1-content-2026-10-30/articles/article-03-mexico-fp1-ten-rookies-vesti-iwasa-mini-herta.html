Ten Rookies Take Over in Mexico City FP1 — The Next Generation Gets Its Biggest Day

By Marcus Cole | F1 Fanrecap

October 30, 2026

Friday's opening practice session at the Mexico City Grand Prix features ten regular-grid drivers stepping aside for mandatory rookie substitutions — the largest single-session wave of the 2026 Formula 1 season. Among the most prominent swaps: Frederik Vesti takes George Russell's Mercedes seat, Ayumu Iwasa gets into Max Verstappen's Red Bull, Gabriele Mini takes over Franco Colapinto's Alpine, and Colton Herta drives Valtteri Bottas's Cadillac.

The Regulation Behind the Wave

The FIA's mandatory rookie FP1 rule requires each constructor to field a driver who has not started more than two Formula 1 Grands Prix in at least two Friday practice sessions throughout the season. The regulation's intent is to provide meaningful track time for the sport's next generation in current-specification machinery — and to create a path for drivers to accumulate the hours required for FIA Super License eligibility.

Mexico City's round, positioned in the final third of the season, serves as the deadline for teams who have not yet completed both mandatory sessions. The convergence of ten substitutions in a single FP1 is the result of multiple teams scheduling their second mandatory run here.

The Most Watched Substitutions

The pairing of Frederik Vesti and the No. 63 Mercedes W17 generates immediate attention. Russell's car is one of the quickest on the grid, and Vesti — the Mercedes junior driver and reigning Formula 2 champion — carries expectations of performing. Any lap time Vesti sets will be scrutinized as data for what the machine can produce in rookie hands.

Ayumu Iwasa in Verstappen's Red Bull carries similar weight. The Japanese driver has been a Red Bull junior for years, and his FP1 outings serve as both evidence gathering for the team and a reminder to Iwasa that a path to Formula 1 remains open if the car and the times align.

Gabriele Mini's Alpine outing is noteworthy for different reasons. Mini is one of the most talked-about young drivers in the junior series, and the Alpine seat — occupied by Franco Colapinto — will one day need to be filled by someone. FP1 performance means something in that context.

The Herta Dimension

Colton Herta's appearance in Valtteri Bottas's Cadillac carries unique stakes. Unlike the other rookies, Herta's FP1 session has a specific measurable objective beyond pace: complete 100 kilometers without incident and earn the FIA Super License point that takes him to the required threshold of 40. It is, in practical terms, a license-earning run wearing the guise of a practice session.

For that reason, Herta's approach to FP1 will be different from his counterparts — controlled, deliberate, focused on distance over performance. If the car holds together, the American's long campaign for a Formula 1 race seat moves to its final chapter.

Reading the Times

FP1 sessions featuring rookie substitutions are useful as data points but require context when analyzing lap times. Rookies are often running unfamiliar car setups and conserving pace in the interest of learning the circuit and the machinery. A Vesti time 1.5 seconds off Russell's regular pace tells us little about either the car or the driver.

More illuminating is composure under pressure, racecraft in traffic, and the ability to communicate technically useful feedback to engineers. Those metrics — invisible in a timing screen — are what the teams are actually evaluating.

What's Next

FP2 brings the regular drivers back. The fast times return, and the focus shifts toward Saturday qualifying and Sunday's race — where Kimi Antonelli can clinch his title and where Colton Herta, if his morning goes to plan, will watch as a Super License holder rather than a hopeful.

But for 60 minutes Friday morning, Mexico City's opening practice belongs to the next generation of Formula 1.

The FIA Knew 2026's Power Unit Problems Were Coming — Why It Couldn't Stop Them

By Marcus Cole | F1 Fanrecap | August 5, 2026

Eleven rounds into the 2026 Formula 1 season, the picture is clear: the technical regulations have turned the competitive order upside down, produced reliability problems that have cost multiple drivers race wins they had earned, and generated a level of mechanical chaos that the grid's most experienced drivers are still struggling to articulate.

Max Verstappen called the power units "Formula E on steroids" during winter testing. Oscar Piastri, after the Belgian Grand Prix at Spa-Francorchamps, said qualifying grids were being decided by "computers behaving or misbehaving." These were not isolated complaints. They reflected something that now has official confirmation: the FIA saw the problems coming.

Nikolas Tombazis, the FIA's head of single-seater matters, told Motorsport.com that the organization simply did not have manufacturer backing to adjust the regulations before the season began. "A shame," was how he described it — a rare public admission from inside an institution that usually prefers careful, neutral language. The implication is not that the FIA made catastrophic errors in judgment. It is something more structurally important: in Formula 1, the manufacturers who develop the power units have a significant voice in whether regulations can be modified, and that voice said no.

What the 2026 Rules Actually Changed

The 2026 technical regulations are the most radical in a generation. The power split between the internal combustion engine and the electric motor is now 50/50 — equal contributions from each source. The MGU-H, a complicated heat energy recovery component that had been part of F1 since 2014, was eliminated entirely. In its place, a substantially more powerful MGU-K delivers 350 kilowatts to the rear wheels. For context, the previous maximum was 120 kilowatts. The electric power available to drivers increased by nearly three times.

DRS, the drag reduction system that has been a staple of F1 since 2011, was also eliminated. Cars now run on fully sustainable fuel, with fuel flow measured by energy content rather than mass. Every manufacturer — Ferrari, Mercedes, Red Bull/Ford, Honda (supplying Aston Martin), Alpine, and the two new entries in Audi and Cadillac's GM unit — started from an entirely different place.

The practical consequence: an opening half of the season marked by anti-stall failures, energy management crises, power unit DNFs, and a level of driveability unpredictability that has made certain circuits and certain sessions feel like a lottery. Mercedes emerged as the clear leader in power unit development. Red Bull's homemade unit with Ford has struggled. Ferrari sits somewhere in between.

Why the FIA Couldn't Act

The manufacturers who compete in Formula 1 have invested billions of dollars into their 2026 power unit programs. By the time the regulation concerns were apparent enough to prompt modification discussions, those programs were already too far advanced for significant change to be feasible. The manufacturers did not want their investments disrupted mid-development, and the FIA, without unanimous or near-unanimous support from the power unit suppliers, did not have the institutional leverage to impose changes unilaterally.

Tombazis's admission is notable because it names the structural tension directly: the FIA governs Formula 1, but it does not govern it alone. The teams, the manufacturers, and the commercial rights holder all have interests and voices. When those interests conflict with regulatory modification, the result is sometimes an FIA that sees a problem clearly but cannot solve it on the timetable the problem demands.

What Comes Next

Audi, which introduced a driveability-focused power unit upgrade at the Barcelona Grand Prix, provides one data point on how quickly teams can adapt: ten of their 12 championship points came in the three rounds following that upgrade. The learning curves are real. The performances in the second half of 2026 may look nothing like the first half.

The longer-term question is what the FIA does with Tombazis's admission. If the 2026 regulations are producing genuinely dangerous situations or systematically distorting competitive outcomes beyond what development will fix, the question of whether emergency modification powers need to be more clearly defined — or manufacturer resistance to such powers needs to be reduced — becomes a regulatory discussion that will outlast this season.

What's Next

The Dutch Grand Prix at Zandvoort on August 21-23 begins the second half. Eleven rounds. 291 championship points. And a power unit landscape that every team on the grid is working urgently to understand better. Verstappen called it Formula E on steroids. The FIA says it is a shame things got here. The question for the rest of the season is whether the chaos that defined the first eleven rounds gets better — or whether the summer break simply delays it.

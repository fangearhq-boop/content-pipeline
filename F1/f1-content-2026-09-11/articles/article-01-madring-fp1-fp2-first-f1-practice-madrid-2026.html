Formula 1 Turns Its First Competitive Laps at Madring — And Nobody Knows What to Expect

By Ryan Calloway | F1 Fanrecap | September 11, 2026

Friday's practice sessions at the new Madring circuit in Madrid mark the first time F1 cars have ever run competitively at the venue, creating an unusually level playing field as teams navigate a completely data-blind weekend.

Formula 1 has raced on circuits with rich histories — Monaco's tunnel and swimming pool section, Spa's Raidillon, Monza's banking and memory. Every established venue comes with years of accumulated data: tire wear curves, optimal braking points, temperature models, aerodynamic sensitivity maps built across dozens of race weekends.

Madring has none of that. Friday's practice sessions were the first time F1 cars have ever competed at the 5.416-kilometer circuit built around the IFEMA exhibition center on the eastern edge of Madrid, and every team in the paddock arrived in exactly the same position: starting from scratch.

The implications run deeper than the obvious. When there is no historical data, there is no baseline advantage. Teams that excel at building setups quickly — those with the most effective simulation tools, the most efficient engineering feedback loops — hold an edge that would not exist at a track where reliable reference data is already built into the setup philosophy. The best-prepared team is the one that learns fastest.

La Monumental and Everything It Changes

Turn 12 at Madring — named La Monumental — is the most distinctive element on a circuit with several unusual features. The corner spans approximately 550 meters and sweeps through 270 degrees of sustained directional change with banking angles approaching 24%. No current Formula 1 circuit asks cars to generate that kind of lateral load for that duration.

The engineering question La Monumental creates is not simply how fast you can go through it. It is what the tire situation looks like after going through it repeatedly over a race distance. Sustained high-lateral-load cornering generates heat in tires in ways that more punctuated high-speed corners do not, and the behavior of compounds across the Madring stint will be something teams are actively discovering in FP1 and FP2 on Friday.

The main straight leading toward the end of the lap allows speeds approaching 340 km/h. The combination of that high-speed requirement and the banked mid-sector corner creates an aerodynamic setup challenge with no obvious precedent — teams cannot simply take their settings from a comparable circuit and apply them here.

The Equal-Knowledge Problem

Perhaps the most unusual aspect of any new-circuit weekend is what it does to the normal competitive landscape. Some teams consistently outperform their championship standing at specific circuits — Mercedes is strong at power circuits, Ferrari finds form in high-downforce environments, Red Bull historically excels on tracks with long, high-speed sequences. Those tendencies are quantified over years of racing.

At Madring, those tendencies are hypothetical. Team principals and engineers can look at the circuit map, model it in their simulators, and generate predictions — but they cannot validate those predictions against real data until FP1. The track will also grip up across the weekend as rubber from the cars builds a racing line, which means Friday setups will need to account for a surface that behaves meaningfully differently from the version that will be present during Sunday's race.

What's Next

FP2 data will be the primary input for Saturday's setup decisions heading into qualifying. Teams will spend Friday night analyzing tire data, analyzing sector performances, and making the baseline aerodynamic decisions that will define their Saturday. FP3 runs Saturday morning at 12:30 PM local time before qualifying at 4:00 PM. The race on Sunday at 3:00 PM will run 57 laps over 308.524 kilometers.

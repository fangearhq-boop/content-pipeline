Ferrari Is Back at the Front — And Madring's Data Says It May Last

By Elena Voss | F1 Fanrecap | September 12, 2026

Charles Leclerc finished P2 in FP2 at the Madring, 0.09 seconds behind Kimi Antonelli. Lewis Hamilton was P3, 0.1 seconds back. Ferrari hasn't won since June. The data from Madrid's new circuit suggests this weekend might be different.

Ferrari's 2026 summer has been a lesson in proximity. They have been close to the front often enough to maintain the narrative, close enough to keep championship hopes technically alive, but not quite close enough to actually win. Their last race victory came at the Canadian Grand Prix in June, three months ago and six races back. Monza was a particularly painful reminder — Kimi Antonelli drove from 19th to first while Charles Leclerc, who was available at the front, could not sustain the gap when it mattered.

The Madring data from Friday was different. Genuinely, measurably different.

In FP2, Leclerc finished second to Antonelli by 0.09 seconds. Lewis Hamilton was third, 0.1 seconds behind the championship leader. That gap — less than a tenth for both drivers — represents Ferrari's closest collective approach to Antonelli's best lap in months. At a circuit where nobody carries institutional knowledge, where aerodynamic setup must be found from first principles, Ferrari's SF-2026 appears to have found a configuration that rewards Madring's characteristics.

Why Madring Might Suit Ferrari

The Madring features a particular combination of corner types that may favor certain mechanical setups. La Monumental — the signature 550-meter banked turn at Turn 12 — places an unusual vertical load on cars that rewards downforce efficiency differently than conventional high-speed corners. The circuit's mix of slower technical sections and flowing high-speed arcs creates aerodynamic requirements that differ from Monza's low-drag philosophy, which clearly suited the Mercedes package more than Ferrari's.

Ferrari's recent development work has focused on improving their car's behavior in sustained high-load corners — exactly the kind of corner that La Monumental represents. Whether that development has produced a measurable step will be answered by qualifying, where lap time is definitive rather than directional.

Leclerc's Return

Leclerc's presence at the front of FP2 carries an additional context. He was cleared to race in Madrid following a frightening incident at Monza, where an object struck his visor during the race and created vision disturbance. Medical evaluations confirmed no lasting effects, and Leclerc returned to the Madring paddock without any restrictions. His FP2 performance — smooth, fast, and consistent in the sessions available — suggests his physical and mental preparation for Madrid is complete.

Hamilton, meanwhile, provides Ferrari with complementary strategic options. A front-row presence from both drivers — however unlikely given Mercedes' overall strength — would give Ferrari the kind of race leverage it has not had since its early-season form.

What a Ferrari Win Would Mean

Ferrari trails Mercedes by 122 points in the Constructors' Championship with 446 remaining. A victory in Madrid — whether by Leclerc or Hamilton — would be the opening move in a constructors' challenge that still has a mathematical path. More immediately, it would end a three-month winning drought and signal that the SF-2026 has genuinely improved rather than simply appearing competitive at circuits that happen to suit it temporarily.

Friday said Ferrari is close. Qualifying will say whether close is close enough.

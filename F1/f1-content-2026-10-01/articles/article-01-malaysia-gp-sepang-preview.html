F1 Returns to Sepang After Nine Years: What to Know About the Malaysia GP Weekend

By Ryan Calloway | F1 Fanrecap | October 1, 2026

The last time Formula 1 raced at Sepang International Circuit, Sebastian Vettel drove his Ferrari to victory with a lap record that still stands today. That was 2017. Nine years later, the Malaysian circuit returns to the F1 calendar with a new generation of drivers, a new technical ruleset, and a championship fight that couldn't be more tightly framed.

This weekend's race carries the official title of the 2026 Formula 1 Gulf Air Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia — a geographically unusual name explained by the Bahrain GP's displacement from the Sakhir circuit due to regional circumstances. It is Round 16 of 23, and it begins Friday with the first of two practice sessions.

The Circuit and the Context

Sepang is 5.543 kilometres of heat, humidity, and technical challenge. Fifteen corners, 56 race laps, and a surface abrasiveness comparable to Bahrain. The FIA has confirmed four Straight Mode zones for the 2026 active aerodynamics system that replaced DRS this year — meaning drivers will have four points per lap where the rear wing opens for straight-line speed advantage.

The weather is exactly what Sepang has always been: oppressive. Forecast temperatures of 30-32 degrees Celsius, humidity in the high 70s, and a 30-50 percent chance of rain at any point. The tropical shower threat is real and unpredictable. Teams that build flexible strategies around a variable weather window historically perform better at this circuit than those who lock in a two-stop plan and hope the sky cooperates.

Vettel's 2017 lap record of 1:34.080 stands as the circuit benchmark, though the 2026 regulations — and their new power units with more electrical deployment — will almost certainly produce faster times once the weekend settles in. The record books will need updating.

The Drivers Who Have Never Been Here

Twelve of the twenty-two drivers on the 2026 grid have never raced an F1 car at Sepang. That number includes the championship's two central figures — Kimi Antonelli and George Russell, both of whom were teenagers when the 2017 Malaysian GP ran. It also includes Lando Norris, Oscar Piastri, and Charles Leclerc.

This absence of institutional track memory creates a leveling dynamic that teams must account for. The advantage goes to those who can extract the most information from Friday's two practice sessions and translate that data into a qualifying setup by Saturday afternoon. Mercedes, with both their drivers navigating an unknown circuit simultaneously, must manage that information gap across two cars rather than one.

The drivers who have raced at Sepang in F1 include Max Verstappen, Lewis Hamilton, and Fernando Alonso — names whose Sepang knowledge represents a meaningful if modest advantage in the early sessions. Verstappen in particular has finished on the Sepang podium and will arrive with specific setup intuitions that younger drivers simply lack.

What Antonelli Needs This Weekend

Kimi Antonelli arrives in Malaysia with a 66-point championship lead and the specific motivation of a driver who knows he should have done better in Baku. His qualifying crash at the Azerbaijan GP — a misjudgment on a circuit that punishes any contact with the barriers — forced him to start from 16th. His recovery to fifth was impressive by most standards and catastrophic by his own.

"We will bring upgrades and we will continue to push until the last corner," he said heading into the Malaysian weekend. The message is clear: Baku was an anomaly, not a trend. A pole-to-win performance at Sepang would restore the championship's natural order — Antonelli in front, Russell in the rearview.

The gap of 66 points with eight rounds remaining gives Antonelli significant comfort. Russell must outscore him in nearly every remaining race to close the gap, and that requires both Antonelli to slip further and Russell to be dominant. Malaysia is the first opportunity to determine which way the momentum runs.

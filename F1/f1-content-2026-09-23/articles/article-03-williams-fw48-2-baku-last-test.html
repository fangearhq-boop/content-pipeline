Williams FW48 2.0 in Baku: The Last Test Before 2027 Begins

By Marcus Cole | F1 Fanrecap | September 23, 2026

The Williams FW48 2.0 has arrived at the Baku City Circuit. After a season defined by the weight penalties and performance deficits that came with the original FW48's rushed production timeline, the team has completed what it calls a near-total overhaul of the chassis — and this weekend marks the first time Carlos Sainz and Alex Albon will drive it in a live race weekend environment.

Williams team principal James Vowles is not selling this as a sudden breakthrough. He described it, to the paddock and to his own drivers, as an upgrade that "won't be enough for points." What it will do, he said, is validate everything that comes next.

What the FW48 2.0 Is

The original FW48 was built under enormous time pressure. Williams missed the pre-season Barcelona shakedown due to production delays, and the car that emerged was significantly heavier than its design specification — a weight penalty that compromised aerodynamic efficiency, tire behavior, and straight-line speed across the entire 2026 season.

The FW48 2.0 addresses this at the most fundamental level. The chassis itself has been rebuilt using a new, lighter carbon manufacturing process — one that the team did not have available when they built the first car. The result is a chassis that is meaningfully lighter and reportedly stronger than its predecessor, achieving closer to the actual design weight that Williams' aerodynamic simulations assumed.

Vowles has called it "almost completely new." The word "almost" is doing work in that sentence — the floor, some suspension components, and aerodynamic elements carry forward — but the core of what makes the car heavy has been addressed.

Why the Result Matters Less Than the Data

In most racing contexts, a team's best upgrade of the season would be judged by where the car finishes. For Williams in Baku, the more important numbers come from the telemetry.

This is Williams' last major commitment of resources to the 2026 car. After Baku, the team's engineering focus shifts to the FW50 — the 2027 car that will be designed entirely under the current regulations from the beginning, without the compromises that afflicted the FW48 from day one. The FW48 2.0 uses the same new carbon process that will underpin the FW50. If Sainz and Albon report that the car behaves as the simulations predict — that the weight is where the model says it should be, that the aerodynamic performance matches the expected numbers — Williams has confidence that the 2027 project is built on a solid foundation.

If the data does not match the models, there is still time to adjust.

The Baku Circuit and the FW48 2.0

Baku's high-speed sections and long straight will test the aerodynamic efficiency gains from the weight reduction. The FW48's overweight issue was most costly in slow-speed corners — where the extra mass meant slower acceleration out of corners and greater tire degradation — but the straight-line speed deficit was also visible through the season. A lighter chassis should help in both areas.

The Turn 18-19 sequence, where 2026 cars may need to lift where previous cars were flat, adds a variable. But for Williams, the question is not whether they will challenge for pole position — it is whether the car responds to driver inputs the way the engineers' models say it should.

What's Next

Sainz and Albon run the FW48 2.0 in FP1 on Thursday, September 24. The initial pace comparison to the FW48.1 data will tell Vowles and his engineers whether the weight reduction is tracking to expectations. Race performance on Saturday September 26 will give a full-distance picture. After Baku, Williams will know what they have — and whether the 2027 project is starting from a position of confidence or one that requires further recalibration.

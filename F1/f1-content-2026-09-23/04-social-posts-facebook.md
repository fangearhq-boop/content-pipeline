### STORY 1: Leclerc Grid Penalty Confirmed

**FB Long-Form (8:00 AM ET)**
It's official: Charles Leclerc will start the 2026 Azerbaijan Grand Prix from the back of the grid.

Ferrari confirmed the plan on Wednesday. On Thursday, Leclerc will run his existing (Monza-damaged) engine through FP1, then switch to a brand-new spec power unit for the rest of the weekend. The moment that new unit is installed, the penalty is triggered — regardless of where he qualifies on Friday.

The new engine is reported to be approximately 0.2 seconds per lap faster than the ADUO1 unit. Over 51 laps at Baku, that is a meaningful performance gain — but it means starting last.

Why take the penalty here? Baku's 2.2 km main straight and the heavy braking zone going into Turn 1 create more overtaking opportunities than almost anywhere else on the calendar. Team principal Frédéric Vasseur identified it weeks ago as the "quite suitable" circuit for absorbing a grid drop.

The real question now is how far Leclerc can climb in the race from wherever he starts. It won't be a championship run — Ferrari's constructors' title defense takes priority — but a Leclerc comeback drive at Baku, with new engine pace, could be one of the most compelling stories of the weekend.

If Antonelli wins in Baku (26 pts including fastest lap), wins the Singapore Sprint on October 10 (8 pts), and then scores 12 points in the Singapore Grand Prix the next day, his lead would be too large for any rival to overcome — the championship would be mathematically his.

### STORY 3: Williams FW48 2.0

**FB Long-Form (12:00 PM ET)**
The Williams FW48 2.0 has arrived at the Baku City Circuit, and it is more than an upgrade package — it's a full reset of the 2026 project.

The B-spec car uses a new, lighter carbon manufacturing process that was not available when the original FW48 was built. The original chassis was significantly overweight from the start, a consequence of production delays that forced Williams to miss the Barcelona pre-season shakedown. The team had been carrying that weight penalty through every race since.

The FW48 2.0 addresses it directly. Team principal James Vowles describes it as "almost completely new," while being careful with expectations: this upgrade "won't be enough for points." The deficit to the midfield is large, the aerodynamic window narrow.

But the point is not 2026. The point is 2027.

Williams is using Baku as a validation exercise for the manufacturing and chassis concept that will underpin the FW50 — their first car designed entirely under the 2026 technical regulations from the ground up. If FW48 2.0 performs as expected, it proves the process works. If it doesn't, they learn that before committing to the same approach for next year's car.

Carlos Sainz and Alex Albon will debut the new package in FP1 on Thursday. The data from this race weekend may matter more than the result.

### STORY 5: 2026 Regulations at Baku

**FB Long-Form (8:00 PM ET)**
The 2026 Formula 1 regulations have changed almost everything about how a car performs. At Baku, some of those changes become particularly stark.

The most talked-about difference this weekend is the Turn 18-19 sequence — a fast, flowing corner combination near the end of the lap. Under the previous regulations, the best cars could run through this section flat out, using aerodynamic grip to stay on the circuit at full throttle. The 2026 machines generate significantly less downforce. Williams, in their technical briefing for the race, identified this as a potential lift point for the new cars — where the driver may need to ease the throttle to stay within the car's limits.

The other factor is energy management. The 2026 cars have a larger hybrid power unit deployment role, but the system has a delay before the MGU-K (Motor Generator Unit – Kinetic) delivers full power after a high-speed section. On the Baku main straight — 2.2 kilometres, speeds approaching 340 km/h — that delay before a qualifying push lap is expected to be the longest of the season. In qualifying, a mistimed deployment could cost meaningful lap time.

In the race, the team that best manages its battery deployment through the long straight, recharging under braking into Turn 1 and deploying efficiently through the street circuit's braking zones, will have a clear performance edge.

Baku under new rules is a different puzzle — and teams are still finding the optimal solution.

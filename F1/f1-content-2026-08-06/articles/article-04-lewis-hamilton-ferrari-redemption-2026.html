Lewis Hamilton Is Second in the World Championship — And His Ferrari Is "Worlds Away" From a Year Ago

By Marcus Cole | F1 Fanrecap | August 6, 2026

F1 Championship / Ferrari / Lewis Hamilton

One year ago, Lewis Hamilton's move to Ferrari looked like one of the most consequential and possibly misguided decisions of his career. His 2025 season with the Scuderia was, by his own standards, a disappointment — a difficult adaptation to a new car, a new team culture, and a new mechanical language after more than a decade at Mercedes.

One year later, he is second in the 2026 Drivers' Championship with approximately 169 points, 50 behind leader Kimi Antonelli with 11 races remaining.

The word Hamilton used to describe the transformation: "Worlds away."

The Numbers of a Comeback

Hamilton's 2026 numbers do not just tell a story of survival — they tell a story of genuine competitiveness at the front of the field. Through 11 races, he has one Grand Prix victory (Spain, in June), five podiums, and has been fractionally quicker than teammate Charles Leclerc on one-lap pace — outqualifying him by an average margin of 0.017 seconds across the season.

That qualifying margin matters. Leclerc is a reference point the F1 paddock respects deeply — a driver who has shown pole-position pace at Ferrari in multiple seasons and who brings his own championship credentials to the comparison. For Hamilton to be marginally ahead of Leclerc on raw one-lap speed in the same car is a statement about his adaptation to Ferrari's machinery.

The Spanish Grand Prix win — Hamilton's first for Ferrari — was the breakthrough moment. It validated that the car was capable of winning in his hands, and it established him as a genuine contender rather than a historical figure accumulating points.

Ferrari's Limitations and Hamilton's Response

The Ferrari is not a perfect car. Its most persistent weakness is a 0.3 to 0.4 second straight-line speed deficit against Mercedes on power circuits — a gap that has cost both Hamilton and Leclerc meaningful points across the season's first half. On circuits that favor mechanical grip, cornering balance, and strategy flexibility, Ferrari is fully competitive. On the fastest tracks, the gap to Mercedes is real.

Hamilton's response to those limitations has been tactical rather than frustrated. He has been unusually aggressive in maximizing what the car can do on circuits where it performs better, while managing expectations — and tire performance — on the power tracks where he knows the ceiling is lower.

That adaptability, combined with the direct feedback loop he has developed with Ferrari's engineering group in his second year at the team, has produced the form his 2025 campaign suggested he was capable of but couldn't quite reach.

The Championship Math

Fifty points is a meaningful gap with 11 races remaining — equivalent to roughly two victories. Antonelli has been remarkably consistent: even when he hasn't won, he has accumulated. A DNF for Antonelli in the second half, combined with back-to-back victories for Hamilton, would bring the title race genuinely alive.

Those scenarios are possible. In a sport where mechanical failures, incidents, and strategic calls can reshape an afternoon — and a season — they are always possible. Hamilton knows this better than almost anyone.

What's Next

The summer break gives Hamilton and Ferrari time to prepare the car for the second half — and to analyze what worked in the first eleven races, and what didn't. The Dutch Grand Prix at Zandvoort on August 21-23 returns the championship conversation to a circuit where downforce and mechanical balance matter significantly.

Hamilton said his Ferrari is worlds away from the car he drove in 2025. The championship standings back him up. The question now is whether those 50 points can be closed — and whether the seven-time world champion has one more title run left in him.

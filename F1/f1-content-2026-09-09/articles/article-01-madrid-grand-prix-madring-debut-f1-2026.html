F1 Makes History in Madrid: Everything You Need to Know About the Madring Circuit Debut

By Ryan Calloway | F1 Fanrecap | September 9, 2026

Formula 1 races in Madrid for the first time ever this weekend. The Madring at IFEMA Madrid is F1's 81st circuit — and nobody has a single lap of real-world data. Here is your complete preview.

Formula 1 is going to Madrid this weekend. For the first time. Ever.

That sentence should be enough to explain why the 2026 Spanish Grand Prix at the Madring circuit is unlike any other race on the calendar this year. While other venues carry decades of data — lap time records, setup references, tire degradation models built from hundreds of race laps — the Madring arrives with nothing. No historical precedent. No institutional memory. Just 22 corners of new Tarmac waiting to be discovered at race speed.

F1's 81st circuit. Its first race in the Spanish capital. The most genuinely unknown event on a calendar that ran through 15 rounds before Madrid.

The Schedule

The Madrid Grand Prix weekend begins Friday, September 11, with two free practice sessions. FP1 runs at 1:30 PM local time (7:30 AM ET), FP2 at 5:00 PM local (11:00 AM ET). Saturday, September 12 brings FP3 at 12:30 PM local (6:30 AM ET) and qualifying at 4:00 PM local (10:00 AM ET). The race itself — the first Formula 1 Grand Prix ever held in Madrid — starts Sunday, September 13 at 3:00 PM local time (9:00 AM ET).

Weather throughout the weekend will be dry and sunny. Temperatures hover around 30°C for most of the weekend with race-day highs reaching 33°C — warm conditions that will influence tire strategy on a surface that F1 has never heated at this scale before.

The Circuit

The Madring is a 5.47 km, 22-turn hybrid circuit built around the IFEMA exhibition center on the northeastern edge of Madrid. Its design combines sections of public road with purpose-built permanent track infrastructure — a model similar to circuits like Baku and Las Vegas, though the Madring's specific combination of surfaces is unique to this venue.

The circuit's most discussed feature is its steeply banked corner — the longest banked turn in modern Formula 1. Unlike conventional flat-radius corners that generate standard lateral load on tires, a banking this steep pushes cars and tires in directions that require specific setup choices and driver adaptation. Teams have been modeling this section in their simulators since the circuit design was finalized, but there is no substitute for feeling the forces at speed on race weekend.

The main straight generates top speeds approaching 340 kph — demanding strong straight-line performance and optimal drag reduction system management. The combination of this high-speed section with the technical banked corner creates a circuit that rewards a carefully calibrated compromise between downforce and drag.

Elevation changes run throughout the lap, adding another variable that simulators approximate but cannot fully replicate until cars are physically on the Tarmac.

The Championship Context

Round 16 of 23 arrives at a moment of genuine championship drama. Kimi Antonelli leads with 267 points — 66 ahead of teammate George Russell, 76 ahead of Lewis Hamilton, and 96 ahead of Lando Norris. Antonelli has won back-to-back Grands Prix heading into Madrid. A strong result in Spain could functionally end the title race. A retirement or poor result could keep several rivals alive.

Ferrari's Charles Leclerc — cleared to race after his Monza crash — and Hamilton both need maximum points at a circuit that gives Ferrari a genuine shot at a setup gamble payoff. When nobody has data, aggressive engineering bets carry less risk than usual.

This weekend, for once, everyone is equal before Friday morning. That changes the championship dynamics in ways that the standings cannot fully capture.

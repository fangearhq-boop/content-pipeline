F1's 2026 Speed Problem: Why the FIA Is Changing Energy Management Regulations Before September

By Elena Voss | F1 Fanrecap | July 26, 2026

The 2026 Formula 1 regulations were conceived as a reset — smaller cars, more efficient power, closer competition. For the most part, the vision has been realized. The 2026 season has been among the most competitive in recent memory. But one unintended consequence has followed the cars around every circuit this year, and the FIA has now decided that the summer break is the right moment to address it.

The issue is energy derating — and the dramatic, sometimes alarming speed differentials it creates on Formula 1's longest straights.

What the Problem Is

The 2026 power unit represents the most radical regulatory change in the sport's modern history. The hybrid electric recovery system (ERS) now provides roughly 350 kilowatts of power — matching the output of the internal combustion engine in what is effectively a 50/50 split. The elimination of the MGU-H (Motor Generator Unit — Heat) removed a critical energy-harvesting component that previous generations used to keep the ERS system topped up continuously through the season.

Without the MGU-H, energy recovery relies entirely on braking and the MGU-K. On circuits with long braking zones, the ERS replenishes efficiently. On high-speed circuits with fewer hard-braking opportunities, it can deplete during intensive deployment sequences — leaving the car without half its designed power at the worst possible moment: on a straight.

When one car's ERS is full and another's is depleted, the speed differential on the straight can approach 40-50 km/h in extreme cases. Drivers have described the sensation as being "a sitting duck" — unable to control the closing speed of a pursuing car because both the defending driver's available power and the attacker's surplus are simultaneously driving the differential upward.

The FIA's Response

The FIA has been monitoring the issue since the opening races of the season and has discussed it with teams and drivers multiple times. The governing body has been reluctant to make mid-season changes to the core technical regulations — any modification to the power unit rules requires extensive consultation and a formal process. But the energy management software regulations — covering Overtake Mode (formerly Manual Override Mode) timing and deployment limits — offer a faster path to adjustment.

The proposed changes center on limiting the maximum rate of ERS depletion during the "push mode" phases — the segments where drivers instruct their cars to deploy maximum electrical power. By capping the rate at which this deployment can occur, the FIA can reduce the amplitude of the ERS derating phenomenon that follows. Cars would still have access to their full 350kW ERS allocation, but would discharge it over a longer cycle rather than in a single aggressive burst.

The tradeoff is performance: some circuits where current Overtake Mode timing provides a decisive overtaking tool would see reduced effectiveness. Teams that have built strategic advantages around precise ERS management will need to recalibrate. The FIA's position is that safety supersedes competitive strategy — and that position is non-negotiable.

What's Next

The FIA's target is to have proposed regulation changes formally announced before the sport resumes in September. Teams will be consulted during the summer break through the Technical Regulations Working Group — a standard process for mid-season adjustments. If approved by the F1 Commission, changes would be implemented at the Belgian Grand Prix (the first race back) or at the Italian Grand Prix at Monza. The 2026 Formula 1 season will continue with the revolutionary power unit intact — but with energy management rules refined by a governing body that has seen enough close calls to know that action is required before the second half begins.

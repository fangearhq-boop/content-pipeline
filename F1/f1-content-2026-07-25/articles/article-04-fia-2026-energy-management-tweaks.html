FIA Targets Mid-Season Fixes to 2026 Rules — Energy Management and Speed Differentials Are the Problems

By Ryan Calloway | F1 Fanrecap

July 25, 2026

The 2026 Formula 1 regulations were introduced as a structural reset — new power units, new car dimensions, and a new approach to hybrid energy deployment designed to make the sport more efficient, more competitive, and more accessible to manufacturers. Ten rounds in, the FIA and Formula 1 bosses are discussing whether adjustments need to be made before the season concludes.

The core problem is energy management. Teams and drivers are managing their power more aggressively than the regulations were designed to produce, creating a racing environment that has at times frustrated both competitors and fans.

What the 2026 Regulations Changed

The 2026 power unit eliminates the MGU-H (the heat-recovery system from previous regulations) and introduces a 50/50 split between internal combustion engine output and the hybrid electric system. The electric component contributes approximately 350 kilowatts to total power output.

The car themselves are also significantly smaller than their predecessors. Wheelbase was reduced by 200 millimeters (from 3,600mm to 3,400mm). Width decreased by 100 millimeters. Minimum weight dropped by 30 kilograms, to 770 kilograms. Floor width was cut and diffuser openings were extended. The result, in theory, was a lighter, more agile car with competitive energy management at its core.

The Derating Problem

In practice, the energy dynamics have created a pattern that the FIA finds concerning. When a car's battery is depleted, it must reduce its power output — a process called "derating" — to conserve reserves for later in the lap or the race. The degree to which this is happening has exceeded what the regulations were intended to produce.

The result: cars on the same straight are sometimes traveling at dramatically different speeds depending on the state of their energy systems. A car with a full battery proceeding through a high-speed section at maximum power, against a depleted car operating at reduced output, creates speed differentials that have been flagged as both aesthetically poor for racing and, in some scenarios, a safety concern.

The Overtake Mode Interaction

The 2026 regulations include an "Overtake Mode" system — formerly called "Manual Override Mode" — that allows a driver to trigger an additional electrical boost of 350 kilowatts when within one second of the car in front. The boost is limited to speeds below 337 kilometers per hour and is intended to facilitate passing.

The system has been working as designed in isolation. But its interaction with the depletion problem reduces its effectiveness: a lead car with a full battery can activate its own Overtake Mode in response, neutralizing the pursuing car's boost and resisting the overtake. The intended dynamic — pursuit, boost, pass — is complicated by the energy state of both cars simultaneously.

What Changes Are Being Discussed

The FIA and Formula 1 are evaluating whether rule changes can reduce the degree to which cars must derate during straights, potentially addressing both the racing quality and safety dimensions of the problem. Whether those changes can be implemented mid-season or require longer lead times for teams to adapt their engineering approaches is part of the discussion.

The acknowledgment that the 2026 regulations require adjustment only ten rounds in is not unusual in the history of major F1 rule changes — new regulations almost always produce unforeseen problems that require calibration. What matters is how quickly and how effectively the governing body can respond.

What's Next

Any mid-season regulation tweaks would require approval through F1's governance process and coordination with the teams. The summer break, which follows the Hungarian GP this weekend, provides a natural window for discussions to advance. Whether adjustments are in place for the second half of the 2026 season depends on how quickly the FIA and teams can align on solutions.

For fans watching races in the interim, the energy management story runs beneath every overtake attempt and strategy call. Understanding the derating dynamic explains moments of apparent hesitation or unexpected pace differences that are otherwise difficult to read from the broadcast.

Red Bull Admits Hungarian GP Shortcomings — A Dynasty Confronts Its New Reality

By Marcus Cole | F1 Fanrecap | August 2, 2026

On the first day of the Formula 1 summer break, Red Bull Racing did something that would have seemed unthinkable two years ago: the team publicly admitted it was not satisfied with its own performance.

In an official statement released August 2, Red Bull acknowledged that they were "not satisfied" with what they delivered at the Hungarian Grand Prix — the final race before the summer recess. It was an honest reckoning from a team that, for a long stretch of recent F1 history, was the one making everyone else feel unsatisfied.

The Numbers Tell the Story

Eleven races into the 2026 season, Red Bull has not won once. It marks the longest winless streak the team has experienced in any season since 2015 — the year before the Milton Keynes outfit began the run of dominance that ultimately produced four consecutive Constructors' Championship titles.

The team now describes itself, internally and publicly, as the "fourth team" in the current pecking order — behind Mercedes, Ferrari, and McLaren. That self-assessment would have been laughable in 2022 or 2023. In 2026, it appears to be accurate.

Max Verstappen, a four-time world champion, currently sits seventh in the Drivers' Championship standings. According to team sources, it is the worst mid-season position Verstappen has occupied at any point in his career. His car, he has said, is "all over the place." Verstappen's language has never been diplomatic, but when the sport's most decorated active driver describes his machinery in those terms, it reflects the scale of the problem inside the RB22.

Mechanical Failures Added to the Damage

Red Bull's troubles in 2026 have not been purely performance-related. The team has also absorbed reliability hits at the worst possible moments. Verstappen suffered an engine failure in China that cost him a likely points finish. His teammate Isack Hadjar was retired from the Australian Grand Prix with an identical power unit problem.

Two DNFs from engine failures across eleven races, combined with consistent pace that Motorsport.com has estimated at roughly one second off the championship-leading Mercedes on pure analysis, has left Red Bull fighting to identify whether their issues are primarily mechanical, aerodynamic, or some combination of both.

The 2026 technical regulations — which introduced sweeping new aerodynamic rules and revised power unit specifications — appear to have caught Red Bull on the wrong foot in a way that has proven difficult to recover from across the first half of the year.

Verstappen's October Decision

Hanging over all of this is a timeline that adds urgency the team would rather not be dealing with. Max Verstappen's contract with Red Bull runs through 2028 on paper — but embedded within that deal is an exit clause that becomes active in October 2026. If Red Bull's 2026 performance continues on its current trajectory, Verstappen has the legal right to walk away from the remaining years of his agreement.

Whether he would exercise that clause is the most discussed sub-plot in Formula 1 at the moment. What Red Bull's admission of Hungarian GP dissatisfaction makes clear is that they are under no illusions about the position they are in. The team is not pretending. They know the situation. And with the October deadline approaching, there is no time for denial.

Can Red Bull Turn It Around?

The answer, according to those inside and outside the team, is: possibly. Red Bull remains one of the most well-resourced, technically accomplished teams in the paddock. The infrastructure at Milton Keynes — the simulator, the wind tunnel programmes, the engineering depth — does not disappear because one set of regulations has not suited their car.

The summer break gives the team's engineers six weeks away from race weekends to attack the fundamental issues with the RB22. Sky Sports F1 has reported that Red Bull are bringing a significant development package for the second half of the season, targeting the Dutch Grand Prix at Zandvoort.

It will need to deliver. The sport has moved on from the years when Red Bull set the pace and everyone else chased. For 2026, they are in the position of chasing — fourth in the pecking order, their greatest driver at seventh in the standings, and a franchise player with an October exit clause ticking down in the background.

Admitting the problem publicly is, as the team would likely acknowledge, only the first step. The second step happens on the track. And it starts at Zandvoort on August 21.

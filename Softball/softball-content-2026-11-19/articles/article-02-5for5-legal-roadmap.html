5-for-5 Legal Roadmap — En Banc, Supreme Court, or State Courts Are the Final Options

By Sarah Mitchell

November 19, 2026

When the Tenth Circuit reversed the Wisne v. NCAA preliminary injunction on November 6, it did not end the 5-for-5 fight. It changed its shape. The 850 athletes whose eligibility was contingent on that federal ruling now face a narrower set of legal options — and a hard deadline.

The transfer portal opens December 1. For athletes who have been protected only by the now-reversed federal injunction, that date is not an opportunity. It is an ultimatum.

Option 1: En Banc Rehearing

Attorneys for the plaintiffs can petition the full Tenth Circuit to rehear the case en banc. If granted, all active judges on the circuit would review the three-judge panel's decision. This is the most direct path back to federal protection.

The challenge is frequency. En banc petitions are rarely granted. The standard requires a question of exceptional importance or a circuit conflict — and while sports eligibility litigation has drawn national attention, the Tenth Circuit panel's ruling on market definition in antitrust law does not automatically clear that bar. Even if a petition is filed this week, a decision would not come before December 1.

Option 2: Supreme Court Emergency Petition

Attorneys could file an emergency application with the Supreme Court asking a justice to stay the Tenth Circuit's ruling while the case continues. Emergency applications to the Supreme Court in college sports eligibility cases are not without precedent — the NIL era has seen several rushed filings. But the standard for an emergency stay is demanding: the applicant must show irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits.

The odds are long. The Tenth Circuit panel found the plaintiffs' market definition implausible under antitrust law. That is a difficult finding to reverse at emergency speed. Athletes and their attorneys should not plan around this option as a primary path.

Option 3: State Courts — The Most Viable Path

More than 200 state-court cases challenging the NCAA's eligibility rules remain active across the country. These cases run on entirely separate tracks from the federal litigation. Some have already produced state-level injunctions protecting athletes within specific jurisdictions. The 46-athlete new lawsuit filed after the Tenth Circuit reversal adds to this pool.

State courts are the most realistic ongoing protection — but they are inconsistent. A state court injunction in one state does not protect an athlete at a school in another state. Athletes at programs in states with active litigation may have protection; athletes in other states may not.

The Decision Athletes Must Make Now

Kentucky coach Rachel Lawson used the word "casualties" months ago. After the November 6 reversal, that word has taken on new weight. The 850 athletes directly affected must now choose: enter the December portal and preserve a path at a new school, or stay put and bet on a legal outcome that may not arrive before spring.

Neither option is clean. Entering the portal signals to your current program that you may not return, which creates its own complications. Staying requires trusting that the state courts or a federal emergency action will protect you — and accepting the risk that it won't.

What's Next

Watch for en banc filings from plaintiffs' attorneys in the days ahead. Monitor state court dockets, particularly in Texas, California, and other states with active 5-for-5 cases. And watch December 1 transfer portal entries — that data will tell the story of how many athletes chose to move rather than wait. The fight is not over. But the terrain has shifted dramatically against the athletes.

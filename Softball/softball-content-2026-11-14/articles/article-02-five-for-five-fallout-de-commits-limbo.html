The 5-for-5 Fallout Is Spreading — And Recruits Are Paying the Price

By Jordan Reeves | November 14, 2026 | I Love Softball

Cassidy Ott said she was "involuntarily de-committed due to the new 5-year eligibility rule." Kinley Patten and Isabell Gonzales lost their offers to Memphis the same week. Three young athletes, three scholarship offers pulled, one rule at the center of it all.

The Memphis de-commits — announced by the program the day after the November signing period opened — quickly became a flashpoint in the ongoing 5-for-5 eligibility debate. Memphis athletic leadership framed it as a roster math problem. For the athletes involved, it was anything but abstract.

"There will be more offers withdrawn without a doubt," one SEC coach said in the days after Signing Day. "Because there won't be enough roster spots." Kentucky head coach Rachel Lawson, one of the first voices to publicly address the roster squeeze, warned months ago that programs would face "casualties." Memphis may be the first to become a headline, but it almost certainly won't be the last.

The Squeeze in Plain Terms

Here is how the roster vise works: The NCAA's 5-for-5 rule — adopted in June 2026 — gives student-athletes five seasons to play five years if they enrolled in college no later than the academic year after their 19th birthday. That means some athletes who have already used eligibility may qualify to return for an additional year. Those returning players take roster spots.

At the same time, the House v. NCAA settlement established a 25-player roster cap. If a program is holding 5-for-5 returners and signed a full recruiting class, the math doesn't work. Someone gets cut. At Memphis, three incoming recruits were the ones who lost.

Three former Arizona players — Sydney Stewart, Jalen Adams, and Grace Jenkins — have reportedly expressed interest in returning under the 5-for-5 rule. Their situation adds another layer to the story: athletes who want to return during active litigation can be rostered, but they cannot practice or play in games while approximately 200 state-court cases continue to work through the system.

Legal Limbo for Returning Players

The Wisne v. NCAA case sits at the heart of the litigation timeline. A federal judge issued a preliminary injunction July 31, 2026, blocking the 5-for-5 rule. The NCAA secured a stay of that injunction on Aug. 21. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals heard oral arguments Oct. 13 and reversed the original injunction on Nov. 6. Meanwhile, nearly 200 state-court challenges to the rule continue across the country.

What that means practically: a player who qualifies for 5-for-5 eligibility and wants to return can be listed on a roster — but cannot step onto a practice field or play in a game until the courts resolve the matter. For programs planning spring rosters, it creates impossible decisions. For recruits whose offers vanish while the cases drag on, the system has failed them.

What's Next

The state-court cases have no unified timeline. Industry observers expect more de-commit announcements from other programs in the coming weeks as rosters fill and coaches work through the arithmetic of the new roster rules. Athletes like Cassidy Ott will need to find new programs — a re-recruiting process that upends years of work and relationship-building.

The NCAA's 5-for-5 rule was designed to protect athletes. For those caught in the roster squeeze on Signing Day 2026, it had the opposite effect. How the sport reckons with that tension will define the offseason conversation heading into 2027.

5-for-5 Enters December: 200+ State Court Cases and No End in Sight

By Sarah Mitchell | November 27, 2026 | I Love Softball

The Tenth Circuit ruled against the athletes. The states did not follow.

When the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed and vacated the federal injunction in Wisne v. NCAA earlier this month, the NCAA won a significant legal victory — the federal protection that had allowed athletes covered by that case to compete under the five-year eligibility model was gone. For the athletes whose cases were tied to that ruling, the reversal was a direct blow to their ability to compete.

But the Tenth Circuit's ruling did not reach more than 200 state court cases currently active across the country. Those cases cover an estimated 850 or more athletes. And state courts have been granting injunctions independently of the federal proceedings — creating a patchwork of eligibility that varies from state to state, program to program, and courtroom to courtroom.

What the Rule Actually Is

The NCAA's 5-for-5 rule, adopted June 23, 2026, establishes an age-based eligibility framework that limits Division I athletes to five years of competition beginning at age 18. The rule was designed to address roster management concerns that emerged from the COVID eligibility year extension and the proliferation of the transfer portal.

For athletes who entered college prior to the new framework, the rule effectively denied them a fifth year of eligibility that the COVID extension had previously granted. That is the core of the litigation: athletes argue the NCAA cannot retroactively change their eligibility after they made enrollment and transfer decisions based on the prior rules.

The NCAA disagrees. And federal courts have largely agreed with the NCAA — at least at the circuit level.

The State Court Patchwork

What makes the 5-for-5 fight unusual is the state court dimension. After federal courts began ruling against athletes, plaintiff attorneys shifted strategy toward state court filings. State courts operate independently of federal appellate rulings, and many have been willing to grant injunctions that federal courts refused.

The result is a system where the same athlete might be legally eligible to compete in one state and ineligible in another. Programs in states with active court injunctions are carrying athletes under state court protection orders, while programs in states without such orders are not. The NCAA, which applies one set of rules nationally, is now enforcing different standards depending on which courthouse an athlete's lawyer went to.

Marshall University's group of twelve former athletes, who filed their case in Cabell County Circuit Court in West Virginia, is one example of the state court strategy in action. Similar cases are active across the country.

What Programs Are Planning For

The practical effect for coaching staffs is planning uncertainty. Programs heading into December portal activity and spring practice cannot know with certainty which of their athletes under state court protection will still be competition-eligible by Opening Day. A state court injunction could be overturned. A new state filing could add athletes back. Congressional legislation — discussed as a potential path toward a uniform national standard — has not materialized.

Some programs are preparing two roster scenarios: one that includes athletes under state court protection, and one that does not. It is a planning burden that adds to an already complex December window.

The most significant eligibility fight in the history of college sports enters December without resolution. Athletes, coaches, and programs are heading into the offseason with the same question they have been asking for months: when does this end?

What's Next

More than 200 state court cases covering 850+ athletes remain active following the Tenth Circuit's reversal of the Wisne federal injunction. State courts are issuing independent rulings, and no uniform national standard is yet in effect. Congressional legislation has been discussed but not enacted. Programs are finalizing 2027 rosters without clarity on the eligibility of athletes competing under state court protection orders. The situation is expected to remain unresolved through the winter and into the 2027 season.

5-for-5 in Motion: How Coaches Are Responding to the Eligibility Ruling

By Sarah Mitchell | October 15, 2026 | I Love Softball

The ruling came down last week. Now the questions are more practical.

Judge Charlotte Sweeney's preliminary injunction in Wisne v. NCAA opened the door for Class of 2022 athletes to return for a fifth season of competition in 2026-27. The NCAA is appealing to the 10th Circuit Court of Appeals, but the injunction stands while that process plays out — which means, for roster planning purposes, it is in effect right now.

Coaches are already making calls. Some veterans are weighing whether to come back. And every program with a Class of 2022 player who completed their eligibility last spring is doing the same calculation: does this change anything for us?

The Landscape After the Ruling

The ruling creates a narrow but real opportunity. Class of 2022 athletes who exhausted their eligibility through the 2025-26 season — players who thought they were done — can now petition their institutions to return for a final year in 2026-27. The decision is theirs to make and their program's to manage.

The catch: scholarship limits and roster caps still apply. A 5-for-5 returner occupies a scholarship spot like any other player. That creates a direct tradeoff — the veteran's experience versus the roster flexibility the program would otherwise have. Programs at capacity face the hardest decisions.

Who Stands to Benefit Most

Power programs that lost productive seniors are the most obvious beneficiaries. If a team graduated a pitcher who anchored its rotation or a middle-of-the-order hitter who left for graduate school, the possibility of bringing that player back changes the calculus for 2027 significantly.

Mid-major programs that sent players to the portal also have reason to make calls. For a program where one veteran returning could mean a jump from bubble team to NCAA Tournament contender, the ROI of a scholarship investment is clear.

But the real wildcard is player desire. Returning means a year of eligibility has real costs — training, time, the physical demands of another season — on top of the emotional calculation of deciding whether to re-engage after having already said goodbye to the sport. Not every player will want to come back, regardless of eligibility.

The Appeal Hanging Over Everything

The NCAA's appeal to the 10th Circuit introduces uncertainty that programs cannot ignore. If the 10th Circuit overturns the injunction, players who returned under it could find their eligibility reinstated, restored, or challenged mid-season. The exact scenario depends on the court's order and its effective date.

That uncertainty is something programs are communicating clearly to any veteran considering a return. This is not a settled rule. It is an injunction in active litigation. Players returning under it do so with that context.

Programs navigating the situation are advising athletes to speak with their university's compliance office, review the court order directly, and understand the risk profile before making any decisions. The situation, while hopeful for veterans who want to play, is not without legal fog.

What's Next

Watch for individual program announcements of 5-for-5 returnees over the coming weeks. The 10th Circuit will set a briefing schedule for the NCAA's appeal; typical timelines suggest hearings would not occur for several months, meaning the injunction will likely remain in place through most — if not all — of the 2026-27 season.

The bigger picture: this is one more layer of roster complexity for a sport that already processes significant portal movement every year. Coaches who navigate it well may find a competitive edge. The ones who ignore it may find themselves watching a veteran play for a rival instead.

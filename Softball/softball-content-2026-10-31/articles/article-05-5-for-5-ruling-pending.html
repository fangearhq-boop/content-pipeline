NCAA 5-for-5 Eligibility: Stay Holds, Tenth Circuit Deliberating as November Begins

By Jordan Reeves | October 31, 2026 | I Love Softball

The NCAA's 5-for-5 eligibility rule — requiring athletes to use their five years of eligibility within five calendar years — is still in legal limbo as November begins. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals heard oral arguments on October 13, but has not yet issued a ruling on the merits. College softball programs, which have been planning 2027 rosters under this uncertainty since summer, continue to wait.

The Timeline

The rule was adopted by the NCAA on June 23, 2026. Less than six weeks later, on July 31, a federal district court issued a preliminary injunction blocking the rule for athletes in the class of 2022. The injunction provided temporary relief and threw program planning into immediate flux.

That relief was short-lived. On August 21, the Tenth Circuit issued a 2-1 stay of the injunction, pausing it pending the full merits appeal. With the stay in place, the 5-for-5 rule was effectively back in effect — and the class of 2022 athletes who had been preparing to use a potential sixth year under the injunction had that window close again.

Oral arguments before the Tenth Circuit took place October 13. A three-judge panel heard both sides. No ruling has been issued as of today, and the timeline for the decision remains unclear. Appellate courts do not operate on schedules that anyone outside the courtroom can predict with certainty.

What the Ruling Could Mean for Softball

The stakes for college softball programs are real. If the Tenth Circuit rules against the NCAA on the merits and lifts the stay, teams could potentially add players from the class of 2022 who have exhausted eligibility under the current rule. For programs near roster limits with younger talent at every position, that is an unexpected but potentially significant addition. For programs that have already built their 2027 rosters assuming those players are ineligible, the adjustment window would be compressed and complicated.

If the NCAA wins on the merits, the rule stands. Programs that planned conservatively — accounting for the possibility of losing fifth-year players — will have made the right call. Programs that counted on those players returning will have a different problem.

Ohio has seen its own state-level litigation on the issue, with a state court separately staying an Ohio-specific injunction. The legal landscape is not uniform across the country, which adds another layer of complexity for programs with athletes in multiple states.

What Programs Are Actually Doing

The practical answer, for most coaches, is planning for both outcomes while leaning toward compliance with the current rule. That means roster construction that does not depend on a ruling that may or may not arrive before signing day. It means conversations with fifth-year players about contingency plans. It means building depth through the transfer portal and recruiting that does not require the legal outcome to go a specific direction.

The uncertainty itself is the problem. Coaches are good at adapting to rules, even rules they disagree with. What they struggle to plan around is not knowing which set of rules will apply.

What's Next

The Tenth Circuit's ruling, when it comes, will not necessarily end the litigation. Whichever side loses is likely to seek further review. But an appellate ruling on the merits would at least clarify the operative legal standard — and give programs a more stable foundation to build rosters on for 2027. Until that ruling arrives, the waiting continues.

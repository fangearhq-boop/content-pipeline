The 5-for-5 Fight Has Moved to State Courts — and It's Just Getting Started

By Emily Rawlings

November 24, 2026

When Virginia Tech outfielder Bre Warren chose last week not to pursue a permanent state court injunction, it closed one of the most high-profile individual eligibility cases in softball. Warren, who had been prevented from playing in the 2026 season under the NCAA's 5-for-5 eligibility rule, will not continue the legal fight.

Her case closed. But she was one of more than 200.

Across the country, an estimated 850 student-athletes remain in active state court proceedings challenging the NCAA's eligibility rule. The federal front collapsed on November 7, when the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed the Wisne injunction that had briefly provided broad class-based protection. What remains is a patchwork of individual state court cases, each proceeding on its own timeline, under its own judge, in its own jurisdiction.

Why State Courts Are Different

Federal courts have been skeptical of challenges to the 5-for-5 rule on antitrust grounds. The Tenth Circuit's reversal of the Wisne injunction signaled that the federal path is effectively closed for large-scale relief.

State courts operate under different standards. Many state courts have been more receptive to individual athlete claims, particularly in states where consumer protection statutes or specific contract law provisions create viable theories for relief. The result is that individual athletes with strong state-law claims have continued to find courts willing to hear them — and in some cases, grant temporary relief.

The challenge is that state court relief is inherently local and inherently inconsistent. An athlete in one state might receive relief while a similarly situated athlete in another state does not. For programs trying to plan rosters and for the NCAA trying to administer a uniform rule, this creates genuine operational chaos.

The LSU Group and What It Means

Among the most consequential active cases in softball is the group suit involving LSU athletes, including players like Monticelli and Lassiter, in a group that sources have reported includes more than 40 athletes. A favorable ruling in a large group case — particularly one at a prominent SEC program — would have ripple effects far beyond Louisiana.

Kentucky coach Rachel Lawson used the word "casualties" to describe what has happened to athletes caught in the eligibility rule's implementation. That framing reflects the human cost behind the legal proceedings: players who built careers, earned degrees, and served their programs who are now left in legal limbo through no fault of their own.

The Congressional Path

Legal experts and NCAA officials increasingly agree that the only mechanism capable of producing a uniform, nationwide resolution is Congressional action. A Senate bill that would provide lawsuit protection for the NCAA in exchange for explicit eligibility standards is working its way through the legislative process. Its fate is uncertain — Washington has rarely been fast on college sports issues — but it represents the clearest off-ramp from a litigation environment that serves no one well.

Until that bill passes — if it passes — the fight continues in state courts, one case at a time.

What's Next

Watch for rulings in the LSU group case and other high-profile state court proceedings in the coming weeks. Congressional action on eligibility legislation remains possible in the current session but is not guaranteed. For the 850 athletes still in active proceedings, the December recruiting cycle adds a layer of complexity: programs uncertain about eligibility status face challenges in portal planning and roster construction. Resolution — either through the courts or Congress — cannot come soon enough for the athletes waiting.

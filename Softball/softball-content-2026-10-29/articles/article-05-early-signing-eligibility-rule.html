The NCAA's New Eligibility Rule Is Reshaping What November's Signing Day Means

By Jordan Reeves | October 29, 2026 | I Love Softball

November is normally one of the most exciting months in college softball recruiting. The early signing period opens the window for high school seniors and transfer players to make their college choices official, locking in commitments before spring. This year, for the first time, those decisions are being made in the shadow of a significant rule change — and not everyone in the recruiting world is ready for it.

The NCAA's new age-based eligibility rule, passed in June 2026, gives athletes five years to compete in five full seasons. That is a change from the previous four-year framework that most current recruits — and many coaches — were recruited under. On paper, it sounds like a simple adjustment. In practice, it has created scholarship math problems, roster uncertainty, and enough confusion that 11 athletes across multiple sports have filed a federal class action lawsuit challenging the rule.

How the Rule Is Changing Scholarship Math

For softball programs, scholarship allocation is a complex puzzle even in normal years. NCAA Division I programs have 12 equivalency scholarships to distribute across rosters that typically carry 20 or more players. Coaches spend significant time calculating how many years of eligibility each player has remaining, when scholarship money frees up, and how many offers they can responsibly extend in each class.

The 5-for-5 rule changes those calculations. Under the previous framework, a player who took a gap year, used a COVID eligibility year, or experienced a redshirt season had a predictable number of years remaining. The new age-based structure introduces age thresholds that can override those calculations in ways that are not always intuitive. A player who is slightly older than a benchmark age may have fewer competitive seasons available under the new rule than they would have had under the old one — or vice versa.

Programs that extend offers based on flawed eligibility assumptions end up in difficult conversations. Memphis reportedly pulled scholarship offers from multiple 2027 commits after the rule changed the program's internal projections. Those are real recruits whose plans were disrupted by a rule change that happened after they had already committed to a school.

The Lawsuit and What It Means for Signing Season

Eleven athletes filed a federal class action lawsuit challenging the age-based eligibility rule. The lawsuit argues that the rule is arbitrary and causes direct harm to athletes whose opportunities are being reduced by a formula that has no clear competitive justification.

Lawsuits of this kind rarely produce immediate results. Courts move slowly, and NCAA eligibility cases have a complicated legal history. But the existence of the lawsuit introduces something the recruiting world does not handle well: legal uncertainty.

Recruits who are preparing to sign in November are doing so without knowing whether the rule will be modified, suspended, or upheld in its current form. Programs that have structured their offers around the 5-for-5 framework may find themselves recalibrating if the legal landscape shifts. That is not a situation that encourages calm, well-planned decision-making from either side of a letter of intent.

What Recruits Should Know Before Signing

For players in the class of 2027 who are preparing to sign, the most important step is to have a direct conversation with your recruiting coordinator or compliance office about how the 5-for-5 rule applies specifically to your situation. Age-based rules affect different players differently depending on birth year, any previous enrollment, and how a program interprets the rule in edge cases.

Programs that are being transparent about the rule — explaining exactly how it affects scholarship duration, what happens if the rule changes mid-enrollment, and how the program is planning for roster turnover — are the ones that recruits and families should prioritize. Vague answers about eligibility math are a red flag when the question has a specific, calculable answer.

The early signing period exists to give both parties certainty. That certainty is harder to establish this year. But it is not impossible — and recruits who go into the signing period with complete information are in the best position to make a decision they'll feel good about in February, March, and beyond.

What's Next

The early signing period opens in November. As it approaches, watch for updates from the federal lawsuit and any NCAA guidance on how the 5-for-5 rule is being interpreted in edge cases. Programs that have held off on extending offers — waiting for legal clarity — may accelerate their timelines as the signing window opens.

For recruits, families, and coaches navigating this fall, the most useful thing is to be specific. Ask the hard eligibility questions now, before a letter of intent is signed. The answers will matter more than anyone realized when the rule was passed in June.

NCAA's New 5-for-5 Eligibility Rule Is Reshaping College Softball Recruiting

By Sarah Mitchell | October 28, 2026 | I Love Softball

A rule change approved by the NCAA Division I Cabinet in June 2026 is beginning to show real consequences in college softball recruiting. The new age-based eligibility model gives student-athletes five years to compete in five full seasons — a change from the old structure that allowed four seasons within a five-year window.

On paper, that sounds like a benefit. In practice, it is complicating scholarship math, prompting decommitments from the 2027 class, and generating legal challenges that could reshape how the rule is applied.

What the Rule Changed

Under the previous model, athletes had five calendar years from their first enrollment to complete four seasons of competition. A medical redshirt, a pandemic exemption, or a transfer year could all extend that window. The result was that some players competed well into their mid-20s, occupying scholarship spots for longer than the standard four years.

The new model eliminates that flexibility. Athletes now get five years — and five seasons. The redshirt path still exists, but athletes can no longer recapture years through waivers or exemptions in the same way. Once the clock starts, it runs at a fixed pace tied to age rather than enrollment.

The stated goal of the rule is competitive equity: ensuring that the college game is not tilted by older athletes competing against traditional-age freshmen and sophomores. The unintended consequence, many in the softball community say, is a scholarship planning disruption that programs were not prepared for.

Decommitments, Pulled Offers, and Legal Action

The first visible effects are arriving in the 2027 recruiting cycle. A handful of committed 2027 recruits have begun to decommit, with some posting publicly on social media about their situation. In at least one reported case, Memphis pulled offers from multiple 2027 high school commits after the rule passed — a decision the program reportedly made as it recalculated its scholarship availability under the new structure.

Eleven Division I student-athletes have already filed a federal class action lawsuit challenging the rule. The case centers on whether the NCAA's age-based model constitutes an overreach of its authority and whether it unfairly penalizes athletes who earned eligibility under the previous system.

The legal outcome is uncertain. But the recruiting consequences are already here.

What It Means for Families

For families navigating the 2027 or 2028 recruiting process, the practical advice is straightforward: ask programs directly how this rule affects their scholarship planning and your specific situation. Some programs are handling the transition smoothly; others are recalibrating in real time.

The early signing period is approaching. Before signing any National Letter of Intent, recruits and families should understand how their program is accounting for the new eligibility model and whether that changes the terms of the scholarship offer they received.

What's Next

The NCAA has indicated it will not reverse the rule, and the Division I Board has doubled down on the age-based model in the face of challenges. The lawsuit filed by eleven athletes will work through the courts, with a timeline that remains unclear.

In the meantime, the recruiting market for 2027 and 2028 classes is adjusting. The full scope of the rule's impact on softball — where scholarship limits are already tight and roster turnover through the transfer portal is high — will become clearer over the next several months.

Beyond Wisne: Individual Athletes Are Using State Courts as the New 5-for-5 Battlefield

By Emily Rawlings | I Love Softball | November 22, 2026

The NCAA won a significant legal battle in November when the Tenth Circuit reversed and vacated the federal injunction in Wisne v. NCAA. The class-wide protection that had given 850 athletes a path to a fifth year of eligibility is gone at the federal level. But the fight is not over — it has simply moved to a different arena, and the NCAA is finding the state-court landscape far harder to control.

Individual athletes are filing their own suits in state courts, and those courts are granting relief at a higher rate than the federal system ever did. Virginia Tech outfielder Bre Warren filed for emergency relief to play this season after not appearing on a 2025 roster. At LSU, Paytn Monticelli and Jalia Lassiter are part of a 40-plus athlete group that has taken the NCAA to court seeking an additional year of eligibility.

Why State Courts Are Different

The key distinction between state and federal courts in this fight is independence. A federal ruling — whether from a district court or the Tenth Circuit — creates precedent that can apply nationally. When the Tenth Circuit reversed the Wisne injunction, it effectively ended federal-level class protection across the country.

State courts do not work that way. A ruling in Virginia does not bind a court in Louisiana. A win in Tennessee doesn't create precedent in Georgia. Each state court considers the case under its own jurisdiction and state law. That means the NCAA cannot defeat the entire landscape with a single appeal — it must fight 200-plus cases individually, in dozens of state jurisdictions, often with different legal standards applying.

Athletes and their attorneys have recognized this dynamic and are increasingly choosing state venues over federal ones. The result, according to legal observers, is that state courts are granting eligibility relief at a meaningfully higher rate than federal courts did.

The NCAA's Counter-Move

The NCAA understands the threat. Reports indicate the organization is asking Congress for legislation that would block these individual state court suits entirely — a preemptive federal override that would prevent state courts from granting eligibility relief independent of NCAA rules. If that legislation passes, it would render every pending state case moot.

But Congressional action moves slowly, and the 2027 softball season begins in February. For athletes with active state cases, the most important date is not a legislative calendar — it is whether a state court grants emergency relief before spring practice begins.

What It Means for Programs

For softball programs building rosters for 2027, the 5-for-5 situation adds a layer of uncertainty on top of the December transfer portal. A player who receives state court relief could suddenly return to eligibility after a program has already filled its roster under the House settlement's 25-player cap. Programs are building contingency plans that account for both outcomes.

What's Next

The state court calendar runs parallel to — and independent of — the softball calendar. Warren's case in Virginia, the LSU group's case in Louisiana, and dozens of other individual suits will proceed through the courts on their own timelines. December portal activity may resolve roster questions for programs that move decisively. But the legal uncertainty remains until Congress acts or the courts reach final rulings. For the 850 athletes still in limbo, state courts are the last viable path — and they are using it.

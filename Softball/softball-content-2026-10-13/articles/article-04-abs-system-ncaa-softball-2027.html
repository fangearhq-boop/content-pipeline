Robot Umpires Are Coming: The ABS Challenge System Is Headed to NCAA Softball in 2027

By Emily Rawlings | October 13, 2026 | I Love Softball

College softball is about to change in a way fans have been debating for years.

The NCAA has approved an Automated Ball-Strike (ABS) challenge system for all NCAA levels starting with the 2027 season. The system was confirmed for baseball after years of testing in the minor leagues and Atlantic Coast Conference tournaments. Now it is moving to softball — initially on an experimental basis — with the Sun Belt Conference leading the charge.

The Sun Belt announced an expanded partnership with Trackman that will bring ball-strike tracking technology to their programs for both sports in 2027. Commissioner Keith Gill confirmed the conference is opting into the NCAA's experimental rules framework for baseball and softball simultaneously.

How the Challenge System Works

Under the ABS challenge model, teams are granted a set number of opportunities per game to challenge ball-strike calls made by the home-plate umpire. When a challenge is issued, the tracking system — powered by radar or camera technology from providers like Trackman or Hawk-Eye — delivers an instant ruling based on the pitch's location relative to the strike zone.

If the challenge overturns the call, the team retains its challenge. If it is confirmed, the challenge is used up.

The SEC tested a similar challenge system at its 2026 baseball conference tournament. The numbers were striking: 105 calls were challenged across the tournament, and approximately 56 percent were overturned. More than half of challenged calls reversed by technology.

That figure alone tells you how much human error exists at the most competitive level of collegiate sport — and how significant a tool challenges become when used strategically by coaching staffs who have invested in pitch-tracking data.

What This Means for Softball

A few plays in softball are particularly vulnerable to missed calls. The check-swing is the most obvious — a judgment that varies enormously between umpires and in the same umpire at different points in the same game. The inside pitch clipping the corner. The pitch that appears to catch the low end of the strike zone but gets rung up anyway, or called a ball when it was right there.

The ABS challenge system does not eliminate the umpire. Human judgment still governs everything outside ball-strike calls — runners, plays at the plate, interference, infield fly. What it does is introduce an objective check on the one call that has the most statistical impact on game outcomes.

Installation cost is a real constraint. Not every program will have the Trackman or Hawk-Eye infrastructure in place by 2027, which means the experimental rollout will be uneven. Schools in conferences like the Sun Belt, which are proactively partnering with providers, will have it first. Mid-major and Division II programs may be waiting longer.

What's Next

The Division I Softball Oversight Committee will need to formalize the experimental framework for the sport specifically, with details on challenge allocation, appeal procedures, and which games are eligible. More conferences are expected to announce similar technology partnerships over the winter as the 2027 season approaches.

For coaches, the adjustment period begins now. Teams with pitch-tracking data already built into their preparation will have a head start on understanding which umpire tendencies are challengeable and how to allocate challenges strategically across a game.

The game is changing. The technology that has redefined pitching evaluation and strategy in the sport for years is about to influence what happens in real time, in front of fans, in games that matter. College softball in 2027 will not look exactly like college softball in 2026 — and that is not a bad thing.
